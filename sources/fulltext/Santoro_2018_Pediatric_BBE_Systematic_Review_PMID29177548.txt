Pediatric Bickerstaff brainstem encephalitis: a systematic review
of literature and case series
Jonathan Douglas Santoro1 · Daniel V. Lazzareschi2 · Cynthia Jane Campen1 · Keith P. Van Haren1
Received: 23 October 2017 / Accepted: 16 November 2017 / Published online: 24 November 2017
© Springer-Verlag GmbH Germany, part of Springer Nature 2017

Abstract
Objective To characterize the phenotype of pediatric Bickerstaff’s brainstem encephalitis (BBE) and evaluate prognostic
features in the clinical course, diagnostic studies, and treatment exposures.
Methods We systematically reviewed PubMed, Web of Science, and SCOPUS databases as well as medical records at
the Lucile Packard Children’s Hospital to identify cases of pediatric BBE. Inclusion required all of the following criteria:
age ≤ 20 years, presence of somnolence or alterations in mental status at the time of presentation or developed within 7 days
of presentation, ataxia, and ophthalmoplegia.
Results We reviewed 682 manuscripts, identifying a total of 47 pediatric BBE cases. We also describe five previously unreported cases. The phenotype of these pediatric patients was similar to previously published literature. Sixty-eight percent
of patients demonstrated positive anti-GQ1b antibody titers, yet the presence of these antibodies was not associated with
longer times to recovery. Patients with neuroimaging abnormalities featured a longer median time to recovery, but this was
not statistically significant (p = 0.124). Overall, patients treated with any form of immunotherapy (intravenous immunoglobulin, steroids, or plasmapheresis) demonstrated shorter median time to resolution of symptoms compared to supportive
therapy, although this trend was not statistically significant (p = 0.277). Post-hoc t tests revealed a trend towards use of
immunotherapy against supportive care alone (p = 0.174).
Conclusion Our study identified clinical, radiologic, and treatment features that may hold prognostic value for children with
BBE. The role of immunotherapy remains under investigation but may prove of utility with further, randomized controlled
studies in this rare disease.

Introduction
Bickerstaff’s brainstem encephalitis (BBE) is a rare,
immune-mediated, disorder of the brainstem and peripheral nervous system characterized by a clinical triad of
ataxia, encephalopathy, and ophthalmoplegia [1, 2]. The

estimated to be 8 per 100 million individuals, and children
constitute a minority of reported cases [2, 4, 5].
To our knowledge, no prior systematic reviews of pediatric BBE exist, nor are there trials validating therapeutic
interventions in pediatric populations. This study explores
47 cases of pediatric BBE from an extensive search of
medical literature as well as five, previously unreported,
cases evaluated at the Lucile Packard Children’s Hospital
at Stanford University (LPCH). This analysis has three primary objectives: first, to phenotype pediatric BBE, and in

Methods
Following approval by the Stanford University Institutional Review Board, we conducted a literature review
to identify appropriate studies and searched the PubMed
database on April 29, 2016. We ran additional searches in
PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus databases on May
23, 2016, June 13, 2016, and April 24, 2017. No restrictions in date of publication were applied. The authors
included manuscripts published in either English or Spanish. The specific search strategies for all databases are
included in supplemental materials. Five additional cases
were identified through course of practice at LPCH from
2012 to 2017 (Supplemental Data).
Inclusion and exclusion criteria for evaluating the literature were determined prior to the search (“Appendices
A” and “B”) and were based on previously used criteria
published by Koga et al. [4]. All patients had to meet criteria for at least “probable” BBE. All manuscripts were
reviewed independently by the authors (JDS and DVL).

Data were extracted from manuscripts with cases identified as meeting all inclusion criteria and no exclusion
criteria using a standardized tool for term classification
(“Appendix B”), which was performed in duplicate by the
authors (JDS and DVL) to eliminate bias or inconsistencies in data extraction (Fig. 1).
To assess responses to immunotherapy, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) test was performed among four
treatment groups: supportive care only, steroid or IVIG
monotherapy, steroid/IVIG dual therapy, and any immunotherapy (steroids, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg),
or plasmapheresis (PLEX)). Additionally, we used a multivariate linear regression to test our primary hypothesis
that immunotherapy intervention would reduce the median
time to resolution of neurologic symptoms. The exposure
was binary and defined as receiving any form of immunotherapy versus receiving supportive and non-immunologicbased therapies only. The outcome was time-to-symptom
resolution using propensity score and binary presence/
absence of immunotherapy as predictors. To calculate each
patient’s propensity score, we used a logistic regression model with multiple binary and continuous predictors, including history of prior upper respiratory tract infection (URTI), severity of altered mental status, bulbar palsy,
muscle weakness, and hyperreflexia. For variable selection
in this non-parsimonious model, we excluded those measured variables whose coefficients’ p values exceeded 0.2 in

Results
In total, 859 citations were retrieved among three database
resources. A combined method of manual and citation manager de-duplication was conducted to eliminate 48 duplicate
citations and an additional eight citations that were published in neither English nor Spanish, resulting in a total
of 803 citations for review. A review at the abstract level
eliminated an additional 121 citations. In total, 682 manuscripts were included for full manual review, with authors
(JDS and DVL) determining inclusion based on pre-defined
criteria. Following application of strict inclusion/exclusion
criteria, this study identified 39 manuscripts describing 47
patients [6–42]. Five additional patients treated at LPCH
were also reviewed and included. Of these, there were 32
cases of “definitive” BBE and 20 cases of “probable” BBE
[4]. A complete collection of all cases is provided in supplemental materials.
The median age of patients was 10 years; the youngest
patient was 2 years of age. Sex was specified in 50 cases,
of which 36 were male and 14 were female (2.6:1 male-tofemale ratio). A total of 47 patients (90%) reported a transient, self-resolving, illness preceding the onset of neurologic symptoms with a median time of antecedent illness
to neurologic symptom development of 9 days. Of these
patients, 14 (30%) experienced a flu-like illness; 19 (40%)
reported an upper respiratory tract infection (URTI); 13
(25%) reported gastroenteritis; and 5 (10%) reported experiencing both a flu-like illness or URTI and gastroenteritis.

ataxia, and ophthalmoplegia, the most commonly reported
neurologic findings in the 52 cases were hypo/areflexia in
33 patients (63%), extremity weakness in 29 (56%), bulbar
palsy in 25 (48%), visual deficits/disturbances including
diplopia in 22 (42%), facial palsy in 20 (38%), hyperreflexia
in 13 (25%), and altered sensorium or paresthesias in 13
(25%) (Table 1). Intubation for mechanical ventilation, a
post hoc marker for severity of disease, was required in 9

Neurodiagnostic testing revealed 19 of 28 (68%) patients

All 52 patients in this study had a lumbar puncture (LP).
A total of 31 (60%) patients had an LP, which demonstrated cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) abnormalities. Of these
31 patients, 24 (77%) occurred within the first week of hospitalization, while the remaining seven patients (23%) were
discovered on serial lumbar punctures following previously
normal findings. Of the 31 patients with available CSF studies, 17 (55%) showed elevated protein; 12 (39%) demonstrated pleocytosis; 10 (32%) demonstrated albuminocytologic dissociation; and 3 (10%) showed decreased glucose
(Table 1). The percentage of CSF profiles with one or more
abnormality within one week of symptom onset was 61.5%,
increasing by 10.7% with additional samples obtained after
the week of hospital admission.
During hospitalization, 15 (31%) patients received supportive therapy; 12 (24%) received steroid–IVIg dual therapy; 9 (18%) received IVIg monotherapy; 7 (13%) received
steroid monotherapy; 2 (4%) received IVIg with PLEX;
and 1 (2%) received PLEX only. Additionally, 11 (22%) of
patients were exposed to acyclovir and 9 (18%) were exposed
to antibiotics in the first week of hospitalization. The median
time to resolution of symptoms in those treated with steroid
monotherapy, IVIg monotherapy, supportive therapy, IVIg
and steroid dual therapy, PLEX, and PLEX–IVIg dual therapy was 24, 60, 73, 102, 120, and 171 days, respectively. Of
the 19 patients with GQ1b Ab positivity, all but one received
immunotherapy, with a median time to nadir of 10 days and
a median time to full recovery of 111 days.

and any immunotherapy was statistically insignificant
(p = 0.277). Sub analysis with Tukey’s post hoc comparing steroids and IVIg dual therapy, steroids or IVIg monotherapy, and any form of immunotherapy against supportive
therapy revealed p values of 0.757, 0.287, and 0.174, respectively (Table 2 and Fig. 2). We used propensity scores to

Discussion
This study provides the first comprehensive case analysis of
BBE disease course, diagnostic studies, response to immunotherapy, and prognosis in pediatric populations. Pediatric
BBE has a similar clinical profile to adults, although neurodiagnostic investigations with LP, MRI, EMG/NCS and
EEG appear to be more routinely abnormal in the pediatric
population. Despite statistical insignificance, the trending p
values for use of immunotherapy in our varied cohort may
suggest a role for immunomodulatory intervention in this
population based on earlier median times to clinical nadir
and total resolution of symptoms in those treated with steroids, IVIg, or a combination of these therapies.
The clinical presentations of pediatric BBE in our cohort
appeared similar to previously published adult cases, albeit
with a higher prevalence in males [2, 4]. Biomarkers were
analyzed for frequency of abnormalities in this study. Our
study found a larger percentage of abnormal MRI studies compared to previous investigations of pediatric BBE
(41 vs. 14%) [5]. Those with abnormal neuroimaging on
MRI had longer median time to full recovery in our cohort
although this was not clinically significant. Of the pediatric
patients with reported autoimmune markers, 68% of pediatric patients demonstrated positive anti-GQ1b antibody
titers, which is comparable to adult studies (69%) [2, 4, 43,
44]. The underlying etiology of antibody production to the
GQ1b moiety remains unclear, and the diagnostic relevance
of assessing these titers merits further investigation. Prior
studies suggest a lower prevalence of anti-GQ1b antibodies
in children (43%), but these serum samples were obtained
within 3 months of symptom onset, and given the unknown
duration of auto-antibody presence in circulation, detectable
titers may have lapsed by the time of sampling, resulting in
falsely low positivity [5]. Ultimately, CSF profiling in our
study appeared to be consistent with previously reported literature. With the exception of one pediatric case, anti-GQ1b
antibody negativity did not co-occur with marked CSF
pleocytosis as has frequently been observed in adult cases
[4]. This study further demonstrated that the percentage of
abnormal CSF profiles within 1 week of symptom onset was
comparable in children and adults at 61.5 and 56%, respectively, although the percentage of adults with abnormal CSF
surveyed 2 weeks from symptom onset increased by 26%

and specificity in this disease process and thus require more
nuanced scrutiny before becoming reliable components of

Due to lack of existing treatment algorithms in pediatric BBE, therapeutic interventions and responses were
scrutinized closely. Interestingly, patients treated with
steroid and IVIg monotherapies demonstrated shorter
median times to complete resolution of symptoms at 24
and 60 days, respectively, while other treatment exposures were associated with longer symptom resolution

13

windows. Time to clinical nadir did not appear to correlate with any specific therapy in our study. Ultimately,
more pediatric patients were treated with supportive therapy alone compared to adult cases (31 versus 11%) and
among this group there was a median time to recovery
of 73 days [40]. Severity bias may explain the shortened
median time to recovery of patients receiving supportive
therapy only compared to more aggressive dual immunotherapy with IVIg and steroids (102 days), as the median
time to recovery was over 40 days longer than either individual immuno-monotherapy. Only three patients were
treated with PLEX in our study: 2 in combination with
IVIg, one of which was complicated by anaphylaxis after
IVIg. Although both steroid and IVIg monotherapies
reduced time to resolution of symptoms, available data

Journal of Neurology (2018) 265:141–150

are too limited to draw definitive conclusions about treatment outcome. Further, the longer time to full recovery of
patients treated with IVIg and steroids (102 days), PLEX
alone (120 days), and PLEX with IVIg (171 days) may be
similarly skewed by severity bias. Together, these findings
suggest a possible response to immunotherapy in pediatric BBE, although clear associations between diagnostic
criteria, treatment algorithms, and long-term outcomes
are difficult to ascertain, as previously demonstrated in an
adult-based Cochrane Review in 2007 [45].
Direct statistical comparison of treatment modalities was
attempted but ultimately underpowered, and as such, not
significant. It appears that there may be a role for immunotherapy in BBE (steroids and/or IVIg) as it was noted to be
safe (only one reported episode of anaphylaxis in response to
IVIg and no deaths reported in any study) and non-inferior to
supportive therapy with reduced median duration of symptoms. The substantial heterogeneity in treatment of BBE
further complicates the underpowered statistical analysis of
our sample. Use of these agents in BBE differs from the
primary treatment of GBS, a related entity for which steroids have been deemed inefficacious. Similarly, PLEX was
infrequently used in patients with BBE in contrast to other
pediatric neuro-immune disorders such as GBS, MFS, and
myasthenia gravis, and these treatment discrepancies may
underscore a lack of understanding of the presumed autoimmune etiology of BBE.
This study had several limitations, most notably the retrospective nature of the identification and clinical course
of included patients, as well as the incomplete outcome
data available from a variety of case studies. Additionally,
data collected in this review were unable to be statistically
compared to those of prior studies due to the absence of
raw data published in these studies’ appendices [2, 4, 43,
44]. The rarity of BBE makes prospective studies difficult,
especially in pediatric patients, and thus existing literature
outside of this manuscript has been scarce. For this reason,
a severity bias, as addressed above, and publication bias
in terms of the literature and cases available for review is
likely present in our data. Additionally, studies utilizing
supportive therapy alone were published earlier (median
year of publication was 1987) compared to those with
patients who received any immunotherapy (2006), which
adds availability heuristic-bias complicating the interpretation of earlier studies whose authors may have utilized
immunotherapy if it had been more readily available or
more commonly utilized at that time. Similarly the country of origin of reports may have also limited use of particular immunotherapy for access reasons. An additional
limitation was that only 54% of the included patients were
tested for anti-GQ1b antibodies, 23% reported incomplete
outcome data including time to clinical nadir and/or full
resolution, and 6% failed to specify any treatment course.

147

It should also be noted that none of the reference population studies used for adult comparison excluded children,
and a small minority of pediatric cases may have skewed
the results closer to those observed in our exclusively pediatric assessment, thereby rendering conservative estimates
of differences between these populations. Finally, given
the rarity of BBE, a small number of patients meeting
strict inclusion/exclusion criteria further limits the overall
power of our observations and prevents extrapolation of
these data to directly inform clinical guidelines. However,
this rigidity was needed to rule out less convincing cases
which ranged on the brainstem/rhombencephalitis spectrum although we could not rule out overlap between BBE
and other pathology in this study.
This study included a systematic review of published
cases of pediatric-onset BBE, a unique and under-investigated subset of the disease, as well as previously unpublished pediatric cases from LPCH. Clinical presentation
between age groups appeared similar, but variation in
yield of neurodiagnostic studies and their potential prognostic value warrants further investigation. Furthermore,
use of immunotherapy with either IVIg or steroids may
reduce total duration of symptoms, although it is difficult to determine this definitively, given both severity and
publication bias. This study highlights the clinical criteria and diagnostic studies needed to thoroughly evaluate
pediatric patients for BBE and provides initial data to support the potential use of immunotherapy in these patients.
Ultimately, these and similar data exist on a convoluted
spectrum of immune-mediated neurological disorders, and
rigorous analysis in larger, prospective studies is needed
to evaluate the diagnostic and therapeutic utility of these
observations.

Appendix B: Standardized manuscript
and case review criteria
Antecedent illness: reported as described in manuscripts
unless not listed. For terms not listed, the following terminology were used to determine antecedent illness:
URTI: cough, cold, nasal discharge, increased work of
breathing, fever.
Flu-like syndrome: fever, headache, myalgia, nausea,
emesis, and/or general malaise.
Gastroenteritis: diarrhea (non-bloody), abdominal pain,
abdominal cramping, loose stools, emesis, fever.
Symptom classification: clinical presentation of cases
was determined to include ataxia, somnolence or altered
mental status and ophthalmoplegia based on use of any of
the included terms:

13

– Ataxia: truncal/appendicular, gait instability, widebased gait, dysmetria, dysdiadochokinesia, cerebellar
ataxia, postural instability, positive Romberg.
– Somnolence: any form of altered mental status (irritability, confusion, somnolence, drowsiness, fluctuating levels of consciousness, excessive sleeping, coma,
etc.).
– Ophthalmoplegia: any limitation of ocular movement,
ptosis, nystagmus, any pupillary defect (sluggish pupillary reflexes, anisocoria, mydriasis, etc.).
Treatment: the following criteria were used to determine
type of treatment used:
Supportive: antibiotics or antivirals only, respiratory support, airway support, physical therapy, occupational therapy,
IV fluids, hospitalization, speech therapy.
Steroids: use either methylprednisolone, prednisone,
dexamethasone, or ACTH.
IVIg: any formulation of immunoglobulin.
PLEX: any formulation or duration of plasmapheresis.
CSF studies: studies were deemed “abnormal” in each
category based on the following criteria:
Glucose < 50 mg/dL.
Protein > 45 mg/dL.
WBC ≥ 5 mm3.
Opening pressure > 20 cm H20.
Cytoalbuminologic dissociation: combination of an
increased protein concentration (> 45 mg/dL) and a cell
count < 5 mm3.
Time to recovery: all recovery times are estimated from
the onset of BBE symptoms, not hospital days.
– 1st improvement: not fever by itself, extubation or isolated improvement in altered mental status only if no new
treatment modalities attempted afterward (otherwise first
improvement in clinical triad), time to first improvement
after first nadir if course is biphasic.
– Full recovery: completely asymptomatic or “near complete recovery” accepted if only 1–2 trace symptoms
remain that did not result in disability (persistent symptoms noted in appendix C).
