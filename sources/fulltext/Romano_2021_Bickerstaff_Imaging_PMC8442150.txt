CASE PRESENTATION

Bickerstaff Brainstem Encephalitis (BBE) represents a rare autoimmune disease,
firstly described in 1950s by Edwin Bickerstaff. The condition displays classical
presentation involving ophthalmoplegia, ataxia and altered consciousness, with
a good prognosis and frequent complete remission.

A 38-year-old male presented with slurred speech, ataxia, ophthalmoplegia, and
fever. He also reported difficulties in writing and playing the guitar.

IMAGING FINDINGS

Contrast-enhanced MRI revealed a small central lesion located in the midbrain:
- Hyperintensity on FLAIR and T2-weighted images
- Hypointensity on T1-weighted images
- Restriction of diffusion
- Peripheral contrast enhancement

No additional brain lesions identified. Cervical spine MRI: no spinal cord
abnormalities.

LABORATORY AND DIAGNOSTIC STUDIES

CSF analysis and EEG both normal. Serum antibody testing negative for IgG and
IgM against gangliosides GM1, GM2, GD1a, GD1b, and GQ1b.

CLINICAL COURSE AND TREATMENT

IV corticosteroids administered. Progressive improvement with disappearance of
symptoms in about 2 weeks from onset. Follow-up MRI at 1-month, 5-months, and
1-year showed gradual regression of lesion. Patient completely asymptomatic with
no successive relapses.

DISCUSSION

The pathophysiology of BBE is not fully understood; however an immune-mediated
process triggered by an infective agent (Campylobacter jejuni, Mycoplasma
pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae) seems to be at the base of this condition.

Odaka et al. analysis of 62 BBE cases - most frequent manifestations:
- Ataxia: 100%
- Ophthalmoplegia: 100%
- Disturbed consciousness: 74%
- Limb weakness: 60%
- Facial diplegia: 45%
- Babinski's sign: 40%
- Superficial sense impairment: 31%

DIAGNOSTIC CONSIDERATIONS

BBE diagnosis relies on clinical scenario, supported by MRI findings and
neurophysiological studies. BBE and Miller Fisher Syndrome (MFS) are considered
part of the same disease spectrum due to anti-GQ1b IgG antibodies (present in
66% of BBE cases - this patient was negative).

DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSIS

MFS is a variant of GBS with clinical presentation similar to BBE (acute
ophthalmoplegia and ataxia with areflexia/hyporeflexia) but with NO CENTRAL
INVOLVEMENT and no disturbance of consciousness. MRI in MFS is typically normal
as demonstrated in 99% of 353 patients (Ito et al.).

NMOSD excluded due to absence of optic nerve and spinal cord lesions.

KEY DISTINCTION: BBE vs MFS vs GBS
- BBE: Central brainstem lesions + altered consciousness + ophthalmoplegia
- MFS: No central lesions, no altered consciousness, ophthalmoplegia + ataxia
- GBS: Peripheral nerve/root enhancement, no central lesions typically

CONCLUSION

MRI may have a key role during the diagnostic process and neuroradiologists
should pay attention to isolated brainstem lesions considering BBE as a possible
condition.
