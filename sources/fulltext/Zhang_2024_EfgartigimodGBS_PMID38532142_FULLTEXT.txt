Efgartigimod in the treatment of Guillain–Barré syndrome

Abstract
Background Guillain–Barré Syndrome (GBS) is caused by immunoglobulin G (IgG) autoantibodies. Efgartigimod, a human 
IgG antibody Fc fragment that acts as a natural ligand for the FcRn, can increase IgG degradation, which thus may be a 
promising therapeutic drug for GBS.
Case presentation The two patients presented with postinfectious and acute flaccid paralysis. On admission, they were bed-
ridden. Nerve conduction studies indicated peripheral neuropathy. GBS was suspected and they are treated with two doses 
of efgartigimod (10 mg/kg) within 5 days. Their muscle strength improved gradually and 4 weeks after the initial dose, they 
could walk independently. Following the first dose, Patient 1 complaint of muscle soreness, which subsided the next morn-
ing. Patient 2 was intubated due to respiratory failure the day after the initial dose, and did not report other adverse effects.
Discussion In GBS patients, two doses of efgartigimod (10 mg/kg) were effective in rapidly improving muscle strength, 
with a satisfactory safety profile. The findings suggest a potential role for efgartigimod in modifying the disease process in 
GBS patients.
Conclusion Efgartigimod seems effective and safe in the treatment of GBS. This study indicates the potential role of efgar -
tigimod as a novel treatment option for GBS. Well-designed clinical trials should be conducted.
Keywords Guillain–Barré syndrome · Efgartigimod · FcRn inhibitor · IgG
Introduction
Guillain–Barré syndrome (GBS) is an immune-mediated 
polyneuropathy, with an annual global incidence of approxi-
mately 100,000 cases [1 ]. It typically presents as a mono-
phasic disease marked by progressive flaccid paralysis. GBS 
is caused by immunoglobulin G (IgG) autoantibodies and 
molecular mimicry is the mechanism by which infectious 
agents trigger an immune response against autoantigens 
[2–4].
Current immunotherapies for GBS mainly include 
intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg) and plasma exchange (PLEX) [5 ]. IVIg is a biologic agent obtained through the 
fractionation of blood from 2000 to 16,000 healthy donors 
and plasma exchange is a blood separation technique [6 ]. 
IVIg and plasma are not always available, especially given 
the growing global shortage of blood products. In addition, it 
has been documented IVIg takes two weeks to become effec-
tive [7 ]. And patients treated with IVIg commonly experi-
enced side effects (headache, tachycardia, nausea, vomiting, 
etc.), and 2–6% of patients had serious side effects (thrombo-
sis, aseptic meningitis, renal insufficiency, neutropenia, etc.) 
[8]. Plasma exchange can show clinical effects within the 
first week of treatment [7 ], but it may also remove immune 
complexes and cytokines and can induce arrhythmias and 
hypotension [9 ]. Furthermore, despite the application of con-
ventional immunotherapeutic approaches, around 3–7% of 
patients die, and 20% remain unable to walk after 6 months 
[10]. Therefore, there is an urgent need for effective, rap -
idly acting, and well-tolerated therapeutic drugs for GBS 
patients, independent of blood donations.

Case presentation
Patient 1
An 81-year-old female patient with a history of hypertension 
and diabetes mellitus presented with progressive limb weak -
ness and numbness for 8 days. She also had a diarrhea bout 
9 days prior to presentation. On admission, she was bedrid-
den. The cranial nerves were intact. The Medical Research 
Council (MRC) scale values were grade 3 for neck flexors 
and bilateral upper limb muscles, and grade 2 for lower limb 
muscles. Deep tendon reflexes were decreased diffusely. 
Hypoesthesia to temperature and pain in a stocking-and-
glove pattern was noted. Nerve conduction studies showed 
mixed demyelination and axonal sensory-motor abnormali-
ties (Table  1). Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis revealed 
albuminocytological dissociation with a normal cell count 
and an elevated protein level of 1.56 g/L (reference range 
0.2–0.45 g/L). She was diagnosed with GBS, even though 
serum anti-ganglioside antibodies (GM1, GM2, GM3, GM4, 
GD1a, GD1b, GD2, GD3, GT1a, GT1b, GQ1b, and sulfa -
tide) were negative.
The patient was immediately managed with efgartigi-
mod at a dose of 10 mg/kg, and the progression of her 
muscle weakness was halted. A second dose of efgartigi-
mod (10 mg/kg) was administered 4 days later. After the 
initial dose, the patient had complaint of muscle soreness, 
which was untreated and subsided the next morning. The 
patient’s condition improved gradually, as reflected by the 
GBS disability scale [19], Inflammatory Neuropathy Cause 
and Treatment (INCAT) disability score [20], MRC sum

ability Scale (I-RODS) [23], and grip strength measured 
with an electronic hand dynamometer (EH101, CAMRY, 
CHINA) (Fig.  1a, c; Supplementary Table 1). The MRC sum 
score is the sum of the MRC score of six muscles (deltoid,

Patient 2
A 65-year-old woman with a history of hypertension and

Discussion
GBS is considered an IgG antibody-mediated autoimmune 
disease [3 ]. Studies support that blockade of the FcRn may 
serve as a unique treatment approach for such diseases [24]. 
In the present study, we reported two GBS patients, who 
were treated with a neonatal Fc receptor antagonist efgar -
tigimod and experienced rapid improvement within 4 weeks.
The fundamental mechanism underlying FcRn-mediated 
IgG recycling is its pH-dependent affinity [25, 26]. When 
IgG undergoes endocytosis and enters cells within the acidic 
environment of endocytic vesicles (pH 5.0–6.5), FcRn binds 
to IgG, preventing its transport to lysosomes for enzymatic 
degradation. In the extracellular physiological environment 
(pH 7.4), their affinity diminishes, leading to the release of 
IgG and completing the recycling process. Efgartigimod has 
been designed with ABDEG-equipped molecules to increase 
affinity while preserving the pH-dependent characteristics of 
FcRn interaction to decrease pathogenic IgG [12]. A study 
suggested that efgartigimod has a more effective and rapid 
impact on clearing IgG in cynomolgus monkeys compared 
to standard IVIg therapy [13].
To date, there have been no published clinical studies on 
the treatment of GBS patients with efgartigimod. The Phase 
I clinical trial indicated that multiple injections of 10 mg/kg 
efgartigimod resulted in the maximum decrease in IgG lev -
els. Higher doses did not significantly lower IgG levels fur -
ther and increased the risk of adverse events [13]. Therefore, 
two doses of efgartigimod (10 mg/kg) were administered for 
our patients in consideration of the monophasic course of 
GBS, and they responded well to the treatment. Four weeks 
after the initial dose, the score of the GBS disability scale 
decreased by 2 and 3 in the two patients, respectively, and 
the mean change of the score was less than 2 in GBS patients 
treated with IVIg or PLEX [27]. However, the best regimen 
for efgartigimod should be investigated in the future.
Efgartigimod is well tolerated in our two patients. 
Patient 1 experienced muscle soreness after treatment. In 
fact, myalgia is common in GBS patients [28] and might 
be not associated with efgartigimod. Patient 2 did not report adverse effects, and respiratory failure following 
intravenous infusion of efgartigimod was considered as a 
manifestation of disease progression.
The FcRn plays a crucial role in mediating the recycling 
pathways of both IgG and albumin, yet IgG interacting at a 
distinct site from albumin [29]. Efgartigimod has selectiv -
ity for the IgG binding site of FcRn, and it did not reduce 
albumin levels in patients with myasthenia gravis [ 15]. 
However, Patient 2 exhibited a decrease in albumin lev -
els two weeks after the initial dose. As the median serum 
albumin level also decreased in IVIg-treated GBS patients 
[30], the causal link between efgartigimod and decreased 
albumin levels in GBS patients remains unclear and fur -
ther studies are needed.
This study pioneers in presenting case reports of GBS 
patients undergoing treatment with efgartigimod. It pro-
vides detailed documentation of drug dosage, drug usage, 
and changes in relevant scales post-treatment. Additionally, 
attention is given to the variations in IgG and albumin levels. 
Encouragingly, both patients achieved favorable outcomes, 
bolstering confidence in using efgartigimod for treating GBS 
and other IgG-mediated immune-related disorders.
Conclusion
In conclusion, efgartigimod with two doses of 10 mg/kg 
demonstrates rapid and significant neurological relief in 
the treatment of GBS, with good tolerability. The findings 
suggest that efgartigimod may be a promising new treat-
ment option for GBS, warranting further investigation in 
studies with larger sample sizes.
