Guillain-Barre Syndrome Complicated by Posterior Reversible Encephalopathy Syndrome

ABSTRACT

Introduction: Guillain-Barre syndrome is an immune-mediated inflammatory
polyneuritis characterised by rapidly progressive flaccid paralysis. Guillain-Barre
syndrome may present with posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome or reversible
cerebral vasoconstriction syndrome in rare cases.

Case Description: A woman in her 60s with a history of follicular lymphoma presented
with a one-week history of difficulty walking and thunderclap headaches. The patient
was diagnosed with Guillain-Barre syndrome based on neurological examination,
cerebrospinal fluid analysis and nerve conduction findings. Further diagnosis of
posterior reversible encephalopathy and reversible cerebral vasoconstriction syndromes
was based on imaging findings and headache history. The patient was treated with
intravenous immunoglobulin and amlodipine, and symptoms improved.

Discussion: We reviewed the literature on Guillain-Barre syndrome associated with
posterior reversible encephalopathy and/or reversible cerebral vasoconstriction
syndrome. The underlying pathophysiology may involve dysautonomia resulting in
unstable blood pressure, and hyponatraemia causing endothelial dysfunction. The SNOOP
mnemonic highlights the 'red flags'. This SNOOP mnemonic suggests the possibility of
secondary headaches that require imaging studies. In this case, the patient exhibited
three SNOOP symptoms: S (history of malignancy: follicular lymphoma), O (sudden-onset
headache) and O (over 50 years old).

Conclusion: This case highlights the importance of considering coexisting central
neurological disorders in patients with Guillain-Barre syndrome.

Learning Points:
- Guillain-Barre syndrome (GBS) alone rarely causes headaches; therefore, when GBS
  patients complain of severe headaches, especially when the headache is associated
  with 'red flags', other complications and differential diagnosis should be
  considered.
- Posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome (PRES) and reversible cerebral
  vasoconstriction syndrome (RCVS) can be triggered by GBS.
- Hyponatraemia, age over 50 years and female gender may be risk factors for
  developing PRES and RCVS in GBS patients.

INTRODUCTION

Guillain-Barre syndrome (GBS) is an immune-mediated inflammatory polyneuritis, a
disease clinically characterised by rapidly progressive flaccid paralysis and various
sensory disturbances [1]. GBS is an independent risk factor for posterior reversible
encephalopathy syndrome (PRES) [2]. In addition, reversible cerebral vasoconstriction
syndrome (RCVS) has been reported in GBS patients [3].

PRES presents as visual disturbances, headaches, vomiting, seizures, and
disorientation and is characterised by its clinical manifestations plus imaging
findings on CT or MRI [4].

Patients with RCVS suffer recurrent thunderclap headaches, and seizures and focal
neuropathy may follow the headache. RCVS diagnosis requires clinical presentation
including thunderclap headache, vasogenic edema on head CT/MRI, cerebral arterial
vasoconstriction on MRA, and the absence of aneurysmal subarachnoid haemorrhage [5].

Here, we present a case of a patient with all three conditions: GBS, PRES, and RCVS.
We propose that this relationship is causal rather than coincidental, and represents a
continuum between GBS as a peripheral immune-mediated disease and the central
neurologic syndromes PRES and RCVS.

CASE DESCRIPTION

A woman in her 60s presented with a one-week history of difficulty walking following
fever. She reported recent thunderclap headaches with sudden, holocranial onset and
10/10 intensity.

Medical History:
- Follicular lymphoma (2-year history)
- Recent chemotherapy (5-month treatment course, currently in remission)
- Daily non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug use
- No history of hypertension
- No history of migraine
- No immunosuppressive medications for at least one year

Brain CT at an outside hospital revealed no acute intracranial infarction or
haemorrhage but showed hypoattenuation in the bilateral occipital lobes.

Vital Signs on Hospital Arrival:
- Heart rate: 95 beats/min (regular rhythm)
- Blood pressure: 168/93 mmHg
- Respiratory rate: 17/min
- Oxygen saturation: 95% (ambient air)
- Body temperature: 36.5 degrees C
- No papilloedema observed

Neurological Examination:
- Muscle weakness: Medical Research Council grade 4/5 in upper extremities, 3/5 in
  lower extremities
- Loss of tendon reflexes: bilateral upper and lower extremities
- Tactile sensation: reduced in right L5 region
- Nuchal rigidity: absent
- Kernig's sign: absent
- Brudzinski's sign: absent

Laboratory Findings:
- WBC count: 5.4 x 10^3/ul (reference: 4.0-9.0 x 10^3/ul)
- Sodium: 121 mmol/l (reference: 135-145 mmol/l) -- significant hyponatraemia
- C-reactive protein: 0.07 mg/dl (reference: 0.00-0.14 mg/dl)
- Erythrocyte sedimentation rate: 10 mm/h (reference: 1-20 mm/h)
- Other complete blood count and metabolic panels: unremarkable

Cerebrospinal Fluid Analysis:
- WBC count: 1/ul (reference: 0-3/ul)
- Protein level: 536.8 g/l (reference: 15-40 g/l) -- albuminocytologic dissociation
- Opening pressure: unremarkable
- Glucose level: unremarkable
- Gram staining and culture: unremarkable

Imaging Findings:
- Brain MRI T2-fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR): Hyperintensity in
  parieto-occipital regions more prominent than bilateral occipital areas
- MRA: Poor delineation of the cortical branches of the bilateral middle cerebral
  arteries

Nerve Conduction Study (Day 4 Post-admission):
- Decreased amplitude in motor nerves
- Motor conduction velocity largely retained
- Prolonged distal motor latency
- Sensory nerve conduction velocity relatively preserved
- Depressed sensory nerve amplitude
- Diagnosis: predominant acute motor sensory axonal neuropathy (AMSAN) with traces
  of distal motor demyelination

Antibody Testing:
- Anti-GM1 IgG: negative
- Anti-GQ1b IgG: negative

Final Diagnosis:
Based on neurological examination, CSF albuminocytologic dissociation, and NCS
findings: Guillain-Barre Syndrome confirmed.
Based on headache history and MRI findings: PRES and RCVS diagnosed.

Treatment:
- Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg): 0.4 g/kg administered for 5 days
- Amlodipine: 5 mg for blood pressure control

Outcome:
- Headaches and blood pressure stabilized within 9 days; amlodipine discontinued
- Transferred for rehabilitation
- One-month status: Neurological improvement; discharged
- Three-month imaging: Brain MRI showed improvement in frontal and occipital lobes
  with disappearance of hyperintensities and increased visualization of cortical
  middle cerebral artery branches

DISCUSSION

RCVS and PRES share precipitating factors, clinical and radiological features, and
are frequently associated with each other, implying similar pathophysiology related to
reversible dysregulation of the cerebral vasculature, endothelial dysfunction and
disruption of the blood-brain barrier [7].

Pathophysiology:
Endothelial dysfunction can affect cerebral arterial tone regulation, triggering
vasoconstriction with subsequent hypoperfusion, blood-brain barrier disruption, and
vasogenic edema. Approximately 30 cases of GBS complicated by PRES have been
reported, with identified triggers including hypertension and hyponatraemia -- both
present in this case.

Role of Dysautonomia:
Dysautonomia from GBS produces unstable blood pressure, potentially causing cerebral
edema and endothelial dysfunction contributing to PRES development.

Hyponatraemia Mechanism:
A prior report of normotensive PRES patient with hyponatraemia suggested rapid sodium
level changes disrupt cerebral autoregulation [9]. Hyponatraemia occurs in 21.5-48%
of GBS patients [10]. In the literature review of cases with documented sodium
values, all three overlapping GBS-PRES cases showed hyponatraemia.

Clinical Significance:
PRES and RCVS are typically reversible with early diagnosis and prompt treatment,
ensuring favourable prognosis. However, haemorrhagic or ischaemic brain lesions can
result in poor outcomes.

Diagnostic Approach:
GBS typically requires no central nervous system imaging; however, headache
represents an uncommon GBS symptom, warranting further investigation. In this case,
imaging disclosed vasogenic edema predominantly in the occipital lobe and cerebral
vascular vasoconstriction.

SNOOP Mnemonic for Secondary Headache Red Flags:
- S: Systemic symptoms (fever, weight loss); secondary risks (immunocompromised
  state, malignancy history)
- N: Neurologic symptoms/signs (altered consciousness, focal deficits)
- O: Onset (sudden-onset headache)
- O: Older (new headache in patients over 50 years)
- P: Progressive, positional, papilloedema

This patient exhibited three SNOOP red flags: malignancy history (S), sudden headache
onset (O), and age over 50 (O).

================================================================================

CONCLUSION

This case presents evidence that GBS and PRES/RCVS may belong to a continuum
syndrome involving underlying dysautonomia producing unstable blood pressure, with or
without hyponatraemia, causing endothelial dysfunction and disrupting cerebral
autoregulation. When GBS is associated with headache syndromes, cerebral imaging
facilitates diagnosis. Clinicians should systematically inquire about headache history
and obtain imaging when patients satisfy red flag criteria.
