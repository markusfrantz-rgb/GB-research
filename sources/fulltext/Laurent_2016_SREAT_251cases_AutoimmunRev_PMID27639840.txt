Available online 15 September 2016Background: Steroid-responsive encephalopathy and associated autoimmune thyroiditis (SREAT) is character-
ized by encephalopathy and the presence of antithyroid antibodies. We describe the clinical presentation, out-come and treatments for SREAT by a systematic review of the literature.
Methods: MEDLINE via PubMed, Web of Science and the Cochrane Library were searched for articles published
until 2015. Inclusion criteria were unexplained encephalopathy with antithyroid antibodies.Results: We found reports of 251 patients (median age 52 years [range 18 –86], 73% females, 80 [32%] with
preexisting thyroiditis). Patients presented encephalitis signs with convulsions (n = 117; 47%), confusion
(n = 115, 46%), speech disorder (n = 91, 37%), memory impairment (n = 107, 43%), gait disturbance (n =67, 27%) and persecutory delusions (n = 61, 25%). Twenty-eight patients (11%) presented progressive memoryimpairment and 26 (10%) isolated psychiatric disorders. In serum, 34% of patients were positive for anti-thyroid
peroxidase (TPO) antibodies, 7% for anti-thyroglobulin (TG) antibodies, and 69% both. Thyroid-stimulating
hormone levels were usually normal, at 2 UI/ml [0.001 –205]. Cerebrospinal ﬂuid from 10/53 patients (19%)
was positive for anti-TPO antibodies, 2/53 (4%) anti-TG antibodies and 28 (53%) both. Electroencephalography
ﬁndings were abnormal for 82% of patients, showing diffuse slowing consistent with encephalopathy (70%) or
epileptic activity (14%). The ﬁrst-line treatment was steroids in 193 patients and other immunosuppressive
drugs in 10 cases. At a median follow-up of 12 months [range 0.2 –110], 91% of patients showed complete or
partial neurological response, with anti-TPO and -TG antibody titers at 347 UI/ml [0 –825,000] and 110 UI/ml
[0–50,892], respectively. During follow-up, 40 patients (16%) experienced at least one relapse. Relapse was
more frequent in patients with initial coma (26% vs 13%, p = 0.08).Conclusion: The diagnosis of SREAT should be suspected in case of encephalopathy without obvious cause, to
quickly start corticosteroid treatment. The exact modalities of treatment must be de ﬁned.

1. Introduction
Steroid-responsive encephalopathy associated with autoimmune
thyroiditis (SREAT) is a rare disorder ﬁrst reported by Brain et al., in
1966, as Hashimoto's encephalopathy. SREAT is characterized by
encephalopathy associated with antithyroid (anti-thyroid peroxidase
[TPO]) and/or anti-thyroglobulin (TG) antibodies, in the absence ofalternative causes [1]. Whether this entity is considered encephalopa-
thy, de ﬁned as symptoms consistent with neurological and psychiatric
signs with the presence of antithyroid antibodies, or real encephalitis
related to pathogenic antibodies, is still controversial.
The prevalence of Hashimoto's encephalopathy is not well established
and remains underdiagnosed. The clinical presentation varies, from
minor cognitive impairment to status epilepticus, for a challenging diag-
nosis. Usually steroids alone are the ﬁrst-line treatment, but the initial
dose, treatment duration and factors associated with treatment failure re-
main to be established. Even though steroid dependence and a high rate
of relapse have been described, the place of steroid-sparing treatments
remains to be determined.
With this literature review, we aimed to describe the characteristics,
treatments and outcome of SREAT and analyze the factors associated
with treatment response and relapse.
2. Patients and methods
2.1. Literature search strategy
Two investigators (CL and AM) searched MEDLINE via PubMed, Web
of Science and the Cochrane Library for English-language articles pub-
lished up to January 2015 by using the keywords “encephalitis, encepha-
lopathy, steroid-respon sive encephalopathy, Hashimoto encephalopathy,
neurological signs, antithyroid (anti-TPO and/or anti-TG) antibodies, and
thyroiditis ”. All articles with suf ﬁcient data were included in the literature
review [7–35].
We included articles describing cases with (1) the presence of signs
compatible with encephalopathy, (2) positive antithyroid antibodies
(anti-TPO and/or anti-TG antibodies) in serum and/or cerebrospinal
ﬂuid; (3) age N18 years; and (4) absence of other etiologies for
encephalitis, considering brain imaging (MRI or tomodensitometry),
lumbar puncture, and the absence of autoimmune disease and
antinuclear antibodies, Sjögren's syndrome A antigen (anti-SSA) and/
or anti-SSB antibodies, or antiphospholipid antibodies.
Clinical, radiological, electroencephalography (EEG) and biological
data were extracted from reports. Data on treatments concerned
thyroxine substitution, anticonvulsant and anxiolytic drugs, steroids
and other immunosuppressive agent doses, duration and treatment
indications. Neurological response was de ﬁned as complete (disappear-
ance of all symptoms as compared with baseline), partial (at least 50%
improvement), and no response for the remaining cases. The data
were extracted for cases at diagnosis and at 1, 3, 6 months and the last
visit if available.
2.2. Statistical analysis
Data are expressed as median (range) for continuous variables and
frequency (%) for qualitative variables. To consider missing data in the
analysis, results were expressed relative to the total number of available
data. Fisher's exact test was used to compare categorical variables and
non-parametric Mann –Whitney or Wilcoxon test to compare continuousvariables. P b0.05 was considered statistically signi ﬁcant. Statistical
analyses involved use of GraphPad Prism 5.1 (GraphPad Software, San
Diego, USA).
3. Results
Overall, 321 articles were published during the study period; 33
articles were non-English publications and 37 cases concerned patients
b18 years old. We included reports of 251 cases in the review.
3.1. Patient characteristics
The median age of patients was 52 years [range 18 –86]; 73% were
female ( Table 1 ). The initial clinical presentation consisted of convul-
sions (n = 117; 47%), confusion (n = 115, 46%), speech disorder
(n = 91, 37%), memory impairment (n = 107, 43%), gait disturbance
(n = 67, 27%), persecutory delusions (n = 61, 25%), myoclonus (n =
50, 22%), headaches (n = 38,16%), coma (n = 38, 15%) and depression
(n = 27, 12%). Overall, 28 patients (11%) had isolated progressivememory impairment, with median Mini-Mental State Examination
score 21 [3 –30], and 26 (10%) had isolated psychiatric disorder
(paranoid delusions, depression).
The median thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) level was normal, 2
UI/ml [0.001 –205]. In all, 80/248 patients ( 32%%) had preexisting disease,
62 with hypothyroidism (Hashimoto's thyroiditis) and 18 hyperthyroid-
ism (Grave's disease).
Serum titers of antithyroid antibodies were increased in all patients.
In serum, 119/173 (69%) patients were positive for both anti-TPO andanti-TG antibodies; 70 (34%) only anti-TPO antibodies and 11 (7%)
only anti-TG antibodies. Median anti-TPO level at diagnosis was 900
UI/ml [0 –270,000] and anti-TG level 179 UI/ml [0 –36,569]. In

As compared with non-coma patients at diagnosis, those with coma
(n = 38) were more frequently female (89% vs 71%, p = 0.02) and had a
higher rate of convulsions (84% vs 40%, p b0.0001) ( Table 2 ). Patients
with anti-TPO and/or anti-TG antibodies had similar initial clinical pre-
sentation, EEG and MRI ﬁndings, and only the rate of relapse during the
follow-up seemed to differ (0 vs 13% and 18% for patients with anti-TPO
and/or anti-TG antibodies).
3.2. Treatments and outcome
Among 228 patients with suf ﬁcient data, 25 refused steroid treat-
ment, and ﬁnally 203 received treatment at SREAT diagnosis ( Fig. 1 );
193/203 (95%) received steroids alone as ﬁrst-line treatment, preceded
by intravenous infusion in 150 cases (1 g × 3 days in 65 cases), then oral
prednisone at a median of 60 mg/day [10 –150].
Ten patients (5%) received other immunosuppressive drugs at
SREAT diagnosis: intravenous immunoglobulins (n = 4), azathioprine
(n = 2), plasma exchanges (n = 2), rituximab (n = 1) orhydroxychloroquine (n = 1), combined with steroids in 5 cases.
Patients who received steroids or other immunosuppressive drugs at
diagnosis did not differ in clinical, biological, or radiological features,
except for presence of initial coma (16% for patients who received
steroids alone vs none).Thyroxine substitution was used in 35/132 (27%) patients for hypo-
thyroidism, at a median dose of 100 μg/day [25 –200]. Antiepileptic and
anxiolytic agents were used in 73/173 (42%) and 30/144 cases (21%),
respectively.
Overall, 59 patients (24%) received second-line treatment because of
insufﬁcient response (n = 14), relapse (n = 5), steroid dependence
(n = 11) or adverse events (n = 3). Treatments consisted of steroidsalone (n = 20), other immunosuppressive treatment without steroids
(n = 33), and steroids combined with other drugs (n = 6). Other
immunosuppressive treatments were azathioprine (n = 15), plasma
exchanges (n = 9), intravenous immunoglobulins (n = 6), methotrex-
ate (n = 3), rituximab (n = 2), cyclophosphamide (n = 2) and myco-
phenolate mofetil (n = 2).
A complete or partial neurological response to treatment occurred in
146/157 (93%) cases within the ﬁrst 3 months. Among 146 patients
evaluated within a median delay of 5 days [1 –20], 134/146 (92%)
showed neurological response (complete or partial); in this group,
TPO antibody titers decreased from 733 UI/ml [0 –25,600] at diagnosis
to 293 UI/ml [0 –4200] at the ﬁrst visit (p b0.05). Among 70 patients
evaluated within 30 days [10 –90], 81% had persistent neurological
response, with TPO and TG antibody titers at 150 UI/ml [1 –1924] and
374 UI/ml [1 –3000], respectively. Overall, 86% of patients were
evaluated at 90 days [30 –730] and 77% showed persistent neurological
response.
At a median follow-up of 12 months [0.2
–110], 168/184 patients
(91%) showed complete or partial neurological response, with anti-TPO
and anti-TG antibody titers at 347 UI/ml [0 –825,000] and 110 UI/ml
[0–50,892], respectively. Among 70 patients with suf ﬁcient data, 36
were still receiving steroids at the last visit, with median dose of
10 mg/day [2.5 –60].
Complete responders (n = 25) and partial or non-responders (n =
119) at the ﬁrst visit did not differ in baseline characteristics, time from
ﬁrst symptoms to treatment, clinical presentation or antithyroid anti-
body titers. The rate of complete response was similar between patients
with intravenous steroids and oral prednisone at SREAT diagnosis, at the
ﬁrst visit (20% and 17%, respectively) and at the second visit (both 38%)
and had similar relapse rates (16% and 14%, respectively).
During a median follow-up of 12 months [0.2 –110], 40 patients
(16%) experienced at least one relapse. The presence of initial coma
was more frequent in patients with relapse (26% vs 13%, p = 0.08)and the relapse was less associated with positivity for anti-TG than
anti-TPO antibodies or both (0 vs 13% and 18%, respectively).
Nine of 141 patients (6%) died during follow-up and the death was
due to infection (n = 2), cardiac infarction and myocarditis (n = 1
each) or remained unexplained.
4. Discussion
In our study of 251 cases, the largest series of SREAT as far as we
know, SREAT could have various presentations, from coma to
progressive cognitive impairment and isolated psychiatric disorder.
MRI and cerebrospinal ﬂuidﬁndings could be normal or show non-
speci ﬁc features, so antithyroid antibodies should be tested in serum
and cerebrospinal ﬂuid in all patients with encephalopathy without ob-
vious etiology. Treatment with steroids was highly effective: more than
90% of the cases showed persistent neurological improvement, and the
oral prednisone was as effective as intravenous steroids.
SREAT could present as encephalopathy and could feature psychiat-
ric disorder or progressive cognitive impairment. Castillo et al. [2]found
cognitive impairment in 20 cases of SREAT, seizures in 60%, and
psychiatric presentation, psychosis or paranoia in 25%. The initial
clinical diagnoses among these 20 cases varied: infectious encephalitis,
Creutzfeldt –Jacob disease, stroke transient ischemic attack or Alzheimer
disease. One of the challenging differential diagnoses is paraneoplastic
limbic encephalitis. For 24 patients with paraneoplastic limbic

Only 32% of our patients had known thyroid disease and most had
normal TSH levels, so antithyroid antibodies should be systematically
tested in all patients with unexplained encephalopathy. Because no
clinical features or EEG or MRI signs are speci ﬁc for SREAT, antithyroid
antibodies are one of the keys to diagnosis. Nevertheless, antithyroid
antibodies in serum are frequent: they could be present in 13% of
healthy individuals and 27% of woman N60 years old [6];w i t hm e d i a n
age in our study at 52 years and predominant female sex. The presenceof anti-thyroid antibodies in cerebrospinal ﬂuid could be a strong argu-
ment for the disease, but the prevalence of these antibodies in the gen-
eral population is unknown and no standardized assay is available.
Therefore, the term steroid-responsive encephalopathy and associated
autoimmune thyroiditis (SREAT) is appropriate and emphasizes that
the pathogenicity of antithyroid antibodies is not clearly demonstrated
and SREAT may include other steroid-responsive encephalitis. Hence,
the steroid response is another feature that should be included in the
ﬁnal diagnosis of SREAT, even if a few cases might not respond to
steroids and need other immunosuppressive agents.
In our study, 89% of the patients (203/228) received immunosup-
pressive treatment as ﬁrst-line therapy, mainly steroids alone. Steroid
treatment was highly effective, and 91% of patients showed partial or
complete neurological response. Less than 5% received therapy other
than steroids or associated immunosuppressive agents as ﬁrst-line
treatment, mainly for steroids contraindications. The bene ﬁt and the
best regimen maintenance treatment remains to be determined, and

5. Conclusion
SREAT is a rare syndrome with various clinical presentations that
should be considered in any patient with acute or subacute encephalop-
athy, with progressive cognitive impairment and psychiatric signs
without obvious etiology. SREAT belongs to steroid-responsive enceph-
alitis, so steroids remain the ﬁrst-line treatment and must be started
quickly. Most of the time, treatment leads to marked improvement,
but the bene ﬁt of other immunosuppressive drugs, in particular in
cases with insuf ﬁcient response to steroids or relapse, remains to be
determined.
Take-home messages
•SREAT could have various presentations, from coma to progressive
cognitive impairment and isolated psychiatric disorder.
•MRI and cerebrospinal ﬂuidﬁndings could be normal or show non-
speciﬁc features, so antithyroid antibodies should be tested in serum
and cerebrospinal ﬂuid in all patients with encephalopathy without
obvious etiology.
•Treatment with steroids is highly effective and the oral prednisone
seem to be as effective as intravenous steroids.
