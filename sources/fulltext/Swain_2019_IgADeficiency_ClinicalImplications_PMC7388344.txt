The clinical implications of selective IgA deficiency
Swain S, Selmi C, Gershwin ME, Teuber SS
Journal of Translational Autoimmunity. 2019;2:100025
PMID: 32743511 | PMCID: PMC7388344 | DOI: 10.1016/j.jtauto.2019.100025

OVERVIEW
Comprehensive review of selective IgA deficiency (SIgAD), the most common primary immunodeficiency.

DEFINITION
Serum IgA <7 mg/dl in patients >4 years old with normal IgG and IgM levels and normal vaccine responses.

PREVALENCE
Ranges from 1:163 (Spain) to 1:14,840 (Japan). Monozygotic twins: 31% concordance; dizygotic: 13%.

CLINICAL MANIFESTATIONS

Infections: Increased susceptibility to sinopulmonary and GI infections (especially Giardia lamblia).

Allergic Disease: Associated with allergic rhinitis, asthma, atopic dermatitis.

Autoimmunity: Significant association with:
- Autoimmune thyroiditis (including Hashimoto's)
- SLE
- Celiac disease
- Type 1 diabetes

Mechanism: Increased gastrointestinal permeability and greater antigen presentation, combined with reduced regulatory T cells, may facilitate autoimmune development. Specific HLA haplotypes (B8, DR3, DQ2) play crucial pathogenic role.

MANAGEMENT
Primarily supportive. Immunoglobulin replacement reserved for severe cases.

CRITICAL SAFETY NOTE
Patients require counseling regarding transfusion risks. Should wear medical alert identification due to potential anaphylactic reactions from anti-IgA antibodies.

RELEVANCE TO MADELEINE'S CASE
SIgAD with concurrent autoimmune thyroiditis is a documented association. The shared HLA haplotype risk and increased autoimmune susceptibility in IgA-deficient patients is directly relevant.
