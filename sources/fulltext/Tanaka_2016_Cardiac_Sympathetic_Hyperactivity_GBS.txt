CASE REPORT

Cardiac Sympathetic Hyperactivity in a Patient with
Guillain-Barré Syndrome: An Analysis Using Cardiac
[123I] Metaiodobenzylguanidine (MIBG) Scintigraphy

Yuji Tanaka and Kazuo Satomi

Department of Neurology, Gifu Municipal Hospital, Japan

Internal Medicine 55: 299-302, 2016
DOI: 10.2169/internalmedicine.55.3941
PMID: 26831028

Received for publication August 20, 2014; Accepted for publication May 13, 2015
Correspondence to Dr. Yuji Tanaka, yutanaka-gif@umin.net

Abstract

A 31-year-old woman gradually developed weakness in the lower extremities and gait
disturbance. Subsequently, the patient developed severe constipation and hypertension
with tachycardia. Nerve conduction studies revealed demyelinating polyneuropathy. Serum
anti-GQ1b IgG antibody was detected. The levels of plasma noradrenaline and dopamine
and urinary noradrenaline were elevated. Cardiac [123I] metaiodobenzylguanidine (MIBG)
scintigraphy showed a normal H/M rate at the early phase and an elevated washout rate.
According to these findings, the patient was diagnosed with Guillain-Barré syndrome with
cardiac sympathetic hyperactivity. During convalescence, the plasma and urine
catecholamine levels fell within the reference ranges, and MIBG scintigraphy showed a
decreased washout rate.

Key words: Guillain-Barré syndrome, cardiac sympathetic hyperactivity, cardiac [123I]
MIBG scintigraphy, autonomic dysfunction


Introduction

Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS) is an immune-mediated peripheral neuropathy. Patients
with GBS may present with autonomic dysfunction (1). Several studies found autonomic
dysfunction, mainly cardiovascular, manifested as tachycardia and orthostatic hypotension
alternating with hypertension to be very common in this disease, occurring in one-third to
two-thirds of the patients (2). Autonomic dysfunction occurred frequently, and occasionally
was severe, and it manifested in the form of either excessive or inadequate activity of the
sympathetic and/or parasympathetic systems (3).

Cardiac [123I] metaiodobenzylguanidine (MIBG) scintigraphy is a diagnostic test for
evaluating the cardiac sympathetic function (4). However, few reports have addressed the
cardiac sympathetic function in GBS patients using this technique. We herein report a GBS
patient with cardiac sympathetic hyperactivity evaluated using cardiac MIBG scintigraphy.


Case Report

The patient was a 31-year-old woman who was diagnosed with adenoiditis at 6 years of
age and right cholesteatoma at 11 years of age. At first, the patient developed acute upper
respiratory inflammation. After 2 weeks, the patient developed weakness in the lower
extremities, difficulty in standing up, and gradual gait disturbance. The patient also showed
numbness in her left face and difficulty in left eye closure, but did not have dysphagia and
dysarthria. She was admitted to our hospital. A neurological examination revealed bilateral
peripheral facial nerve palsy, no another cranial nerve symptoms, weakness of the four
extremities (dominant in the lower extremities), no cerebellar symptoms, absence of deep
tendon reflex in the extremities, negative Babinski response, and no Romberg sign. Her
functional grade was grade 4 (5). Subsequently, the patient developed severe constipation
(1 defecation per week with straining, lumpy and sensation of incomplete evacuation) and
hypertension with tachycardia. Her blood pressure was 200/134 (systolic/diastolic) mmHg
and heart rate was 130 beats per minute (bpm) at rest.

A magnetic resonance imaging scan of the brain and spinal cord showed normal findings.
A cerebrospinal fluid examination showed an elevated protein level of 292 mg/dL
(reference range, 10-40 mg/dL) and normal cell count. Nerve conduction studies were
performed in the median, ulnar, tibial and sural nerves. The motor nerve conduction
velocity was markedly decreased in the median (34 m/s), ulnar (36 m/s) and tibial nerves
(26 m/s). The distal motor latencies were prolonged in the median (4.9 ms), ulnar (5.6 ms)
and tibial nerves (6.9 ms). Each motor nerve showed temporal dispersion. The amplitudes
of the compound muscle action potentials were decreased in the tibial nerves (4.0 mV),
whereas they remained within the normal ranges in the median and ulnar nerves. The
F-wave was prolonged in latency in the tibial nerve (52.1 ms). The sensory nerve
conduction velocity was normal in the median, ulnar and sural nerves. The amplitudes of
the sensory nerve action potentials were normal in the median, ulnar and sural nerves.
These electrophysiological findings supported the diagnosis of acute inflammatory
demyelinating polyneuropathy (AIDP) (6). In addition, the patient tested negative for
anti-GM1-IgG antibody and positive for anti-GQ1b IgG antibody (antibody titer 1.403,
cut-off index <0.400). The level of plasma adrenaline was 44 pg/mL (reference range,
<100 pg/mL), plasma noradrenaline was 1,075 pg/mL (reference range, 100-450 pg/mL),
plasma dopamine was 28 pg/mL (reference range, <20 pg/mL), urinary adrenaline was
11.6 ug/day (reference range, 3.4-26.9 ug/day), urinary noradrenaline was 472.4 ug/day
(reference range, 48.6-168.4 ug/day) and urinary dopamine was 709.2 ug/day (reference
range, 365.0-961.5 ug/day). On the head-up tilt test in the morning after fasting, the blood
pressure in the supine position was 202/130 mmHg and heart rate was 120 bpm, blood
pressure at the tilt (elevate to 60 degrees) was 188/124 mmHg and heart rate was 122 bpm.
In the heart rate variation test, CVR-R was 3.46. A Holter electrocardiography (ECG)
showed a high heart rate (average 98 bpm, maximum 138 bpm) and loss of the circadian
rhythm. Twenty-four hour ambulatory blood pressure monitoring showed a non-dipper type.
Prior to treatment (functional grade 4), cardiac MIBG scintigraphy showed a normal H/M
rate at the early phase (2.10) (reference range, 1.9-3.0) and the delayed phase (1.92), and
an elevated washout rate (WR) (45.35%) (Figure A). According to these findings, a
diagnosis of GBS (acute inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy) with autonomic
dysfunction, such as severe constipation, hypertension and tachycardia, was made.

Intravenous immunoglobulin therapy (2,500 mg/day, 5 days) and rehabilitation improved
the patient's motor symptoms. Treatment with beta-blocker (propranolol 30 mg/day) for 3
weeks improved the cardiac symptoms, such as hypertension and tachycardia. At one month
after admission, the patient's motor function was functional grade 1, and her blood pressure
at rest was 116/76 mmHg and heart rate was 64 bpm, and the plasma and urine levels of
catecholamines were found to be within the reference ranges. After one year, the motor
function was functional grade 0, and her blood pressure was 120/74 mmHg and heart rate
was 68 bpm, and cardiac MIBG scintigraphy showed a normal H/M rate at the early phase
(2.50) and the delayed phase (2.72), and a normalized WR (32.68%) (Figure B).


Discussion

We herein report that a 31-year-old woman presented with GBS (acute inflammatory
demyelinating polyneuropathy) accompanied with autonomic dysfunction, such as severe
constipation, hypertension and tachycardia. Initially, the patient had cardiac sympathetic
hyperactivity based on the cardiac MIBG scintigraphy findings and plasma and urine levels
of catecholamines. During recovery, the cardiac sympathetic hyperactivity was alleviated.

Autonomic cardiovascular dysfunctions of GBS patients mainly involve hypertension
(27%), orthostatic hypotension (19%), tachycardia (23%), or bradycardia (5%) (7). These
cardiovascular symptoms may be serious or even fatal during the clinical course, as they
take the form of either excessive or inadequate activity of the sympathetic and/or
parasympathetic systems (3). Concerning autonomic cardiovascular dysfunction, according
to the findings of physiological and endocrinological examinations, GBS patients with
hypertension and tachycardia in the acute phase are considered to have cardiac sympathetic
hyperactivity (8-11). On the electrophysiological examination, it was confirmed that the
patients had cardiac sympathetic hyperactivity and overactivity (12). The underlying
mechanism involves baroreceptor deafferentation, resulting in resting tachycardia and
hypertension, and this may explain the sympathetic hyperactivity in GBS patients (13).
However, the underlying mechanism of the autonomic dysfunction observed in GBS
patients has not yet been elucidated.

Concerning the type of GBS, acute inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy is
characterized by cardiac sympathetic hyperactivity, while acute motor axonal neuropathy is
not necessarily associated with marked autonomic dysfunction, except for the sudomotor
hypofunction seen in patients with severe neurological deficits (14). Although associations
between autonomic dysfunction and antiganglioside antibody have not been previously
reported, recently it was found that patients with high scores in the quantitative sudomotor
axon reflex test tended to test positive for anti-GQ1b IgG antibodies, indicating the
association between postganglionic sudomotor dysfunction and anti-GQ1b antibodies (15).
In the present case, the electrophysiological feature of the GBS was acute inflammatory
demyelinating polyneuropathy and the autoimmunological feature was positive for
anti-GQ1b IgG antibody.

Cardiac MIBG scintigraphy is a diagnostic test for evaluating the cardiac sympathetic
function (4); the reduction of MIBG uptake in the early phase implies a certain degree of
damage to the cardiac sympathetic nerve endings (4, 16, 17), and an increased WR
indicates cardiac sympathetic hyperactivity (18-20). Because MIBG is a physiological
analogue of norepinephrine that is actively taken up into norepinephrine granules of
sympathetic nerve terminals by norepinephrine transporters (4), an increased WR indicates
peripheral sympathetic hyperactivity. In the present case, an elevated WR of cardiac MIBG
scintigraphy was shown in the acute phase, and in the convalescence phase, a decreased WR
was revealed. These findings indicated that a GBS patient had peripheral sympathetic
hyperactivity in the acute phase.

In conclusion, we herein highlighted the increased WR of cardiac MIBG scintigraphy in a
GBS patient with cardiac sympathetic hyperactivity, such as severe constipation and
hypertension with tachycardia. To the best of our knowledge, no previous report has
evaluated the cardiac sympathetic function in GBS patients using the WR by this technique.
Cardiac MIBG scintigraphy is a useful quantitative method for detecting cardiac
sympathetic dysfunction. Moreover, cardiac MIBG scintigraphy in the early phase may be
a predictive factor of the cardiac sympathetic function in GBS patients. Thus, physicians
should pay careful attention to cardiac autonomic dysfunction in GBS patients.
