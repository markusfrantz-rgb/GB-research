Redefining the Guillain-Barre Spectrum in Children: Neuroimaging Findings
of Cranial Nerve Involvement

Authors: G Zuccoli, A Panigrahy, A Bailey, C Fitz
Institution: Department of Radiology, University of Pittsburgh Medical Center,
Children's Hospital of Pittsburgh, Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania
Journal: AJNR Am J Neuroradiol. 2011 Apr;32(4):639-642
DOI: 10.3174/ajnr.A2358
PMID: 21292802 | PMCID: PMC7965877

ABSTRACT

GBS and its MFS variant are acute polyneuropathies that are considered to represent
a continuum rather than distinct entities due to overlapping clinical features.
The study evaluated MR imaging findings in 17 children with GBS and Miller Fisher
syndrome (MFS). Fourteen of 17 patients demonstrated cauda equina (CE) root
enhancement, predominantly affecting anterior roots. Five of six patients who
underwent brain MR imaging showed cranial nerve involvement. The findings suggest
that involvement of the CE roots may be considered part of a more extensive
autoimmune neuropathy.

INTRODUCTION

Guillain-Barre Syndrome (GBS) represents a rapidly progressing peripheral
inflammatory polyneuropathy that causes acute neuromuscular failure with incidence
of 1-2 per 100,000 in the general population. Mild respiratory or gastrointestinal
infection precedes symptom onset in approximately 75% of cases.

Clinical presentation includes progressive and ascending weakness of the
extremities and areflexia with potential progression to sensory disturbances,
facial nerve palsies, autonomic dysfunction, and respiratory failure.

MFS is considered the cranial nerve variant of GBS and diagnosed by the triad of
ophthalmoplegia, ataxia, and areflexia. While GBS demonstrates CE root involvement
on neuroimaging, in MFS, MR imaging findings are usually within normal limits.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study Population: 17 pediatric patients with GBS selected retrospectively from
2001-2010. Diagnostic criteria from van Doorn et al (2008) were applied.

Demographics: Males 59%, females 41%. Mean age 9.41 years (range 16 months to
18 years). 94% experienced viral/respiratory infection within 3 weeks before
symptom onset. Recovery time varied from 2 weeks to 3 years.

Treatments: All received IVIg. Additional: pain management (76.5%), physical
therapy (70.6%), antibiotics (35.3%), steroids (17.6%), dietary supplementation
(17.6%), respiratory support (17.6%).

Imaging Protocol: 1.5T magnet. After gadolinium (0.1 mL/kg), 3-mm sagittal and
axial images through spine. Brain imaging: 5-mm axial and coronal T1-weighted
images. Two experienced pediatric neuroradiologists independently evaluated.

RESULTS

Clinical and Laboratory Findings:
- Areflexia: 16 patients (94.1%)
- Progressive weakness: 16 patients (94.1%)
- Pain: 13 patients (76.5%)
- Relative symptom symmetry: 12 patients (70.6%)
- Autonomic dysfunction: 9 patients (52.9%)
- Sensory symptoms: 7 patients (41.2%)
- Facial nerve palsy: 5 patients (29.4%)
- Ophthalmoplegia: 4 patients (23.5%)
- Dysphagia: 3 patients (17.6%)

Elevated CSF protein in 12 patients (70.6%). Of 7 patients with electrophysiology,
6 (85.7%) positive for GBS.

Neuroimaging - Cauda Equina Root Involvement:
- CE root enhancement: 14/17 patients (82.3%)
- Simultaneous anterior and posterior CE roots: 11/17 (64.7%)
- Isolated anterior CE root enhancement: 3/17 (17.6%)
- No isolated posterior root enhancement
- Strong anterior CE root enhancement: 9/17 (52.9%)
- Strong posterior CE root enhancement: 3/17 (17.6%)
- All 3 patients with strong posterior enhancement also had strong anterior
  enhancement

Neuroimaging - Cranial Nerve Involvement:
Six patients underwent brain MRI:
- 5/6 (83.3%) demonstrated cranial nerve enhancement
- Most commonly affected: CN III (oculomotor) and CN VI (abducens)
- Also affected: CN V (trigeminal), CN VII (facial), CN XI (accessory),
  CN XII (hypoglossal)
- NO ABNORMAL BRAIN PARENCHYMAL SIGNAL on any pulse sequence

KEY FINDING - CLINICORADIOLOGICAL DISSOCIATION:
Enhancement does not necessarily correlate with clinical symptoms:
- 5 patients had clinical facial nerve palsy, but only 1 showed facial nerve
  enhancement on MRI
- 3 patients had oculomotor nerve enhancement, but only 1 had clinical
  ophthalmoplegia
- Several enhancing cranial nerves had NO clinical correlate

DISCUSSION

This study demonstrates a high incidence of cranial nerve abnormalities in
pediatric GBS and its MFS variant. Findings support that pediatric GBS and MFS
are acute polyneuropathies representing a continuum rather than distinct entities.

Cauda Equina Root Involvement:
Thickening and contrast enhancement in CE roots on MRI is characteristic of GBS,
present in approximately 95% of typical cases. Selective or relatively more intense
enhancement of anterior CE roots favors GBS diagnosis, though this finding is
nonspecific.

Cranial Nerve Involvement:
Cranial involvement in pediatric patients has been reported but frequency and
extent unclear. A recent study found normal brain MR findings; however, this study
identified 5 patients with involvement of at least one cranial nerve.

GBS commonly involves facial nerves clinically, yet facial nerve enhancement has
only been rarely reported on MRI. This study found facial nerve enhancement in
only 1 patient despite 5 having facial nerve palsy.

CONCLUSIONS

All 17 patients met criteria for both GBS and MFS using broader MFS definitions.
The neuroimaging features of GBS and MFS overlap. Brain MR imaging should be
considered in routine evaluation of pediatric patients with GBS-MFS for cranial
nerve evaluation. Cranial nerve enhancement may become part of clinical diagnostic
criteria for GBS and MFS if reproduced in a large prospective study.
