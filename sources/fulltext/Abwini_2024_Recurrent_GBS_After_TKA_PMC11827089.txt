# Recurrent Guillain-Barré Syndrome After Subsequent Total Knee Arthroplasties

## Abstract

Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS) is an acute immune-mediated inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy characterized by symmetrical limb weakness and areflexia. GBS is typically monophasic but may recur in 1%-7% of patients. Many etiologies exist for GBS; one rare associated etiology is surgery, most notably orthopaedic, abdominal, and cardiac surgery. Here, we present a 76-year-old male who developed postsurgical GBS after a right total knee arthroplasty (TKA), 11 years after developing GBS following a left TKA. The patient developed worsening ascending paralysis and, ultimately, respiratory failure requiring tracheostomy. This study reports a rare case of recurrent postsurgical GBS after TKA, possibly triggered by general anesthesia or tourniquet use. Early recognition of postsurgical GBS is imperative to avoid patient mortality.


## Introduction
Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS) was first described in 1916 and is a cause of flaccid paralysis worldwide, constituting a neurological emergency [1]. GBS causes peripheral nervous system dysfunction, which develops as ascending muscle weakness, paresthesia, and areflexia. Severe respiratory depression with the need for mechanical ventilation can be a devastating and fatal sequela of GBS. The development of GBS can stem from different factors. About two-thirds of patients report gastrointestinal or respiratory symptoms about 3 weeks before the onset of GBS [2,3]. In high-risk patients such as those with a history of GBS, it would be beneficial to screen for signs of any viral illnesses to avoid the recurrence of GBS before performing any procedure.
Surgery is a potential trigger of GBS, most notably orthopaedic, abdominal, and cardiac surgery [2,4,5]. Rudant et al. found that GBS was more strongly associated with bone and digestive organ surgery [6]. GBS in post-traumatic or postsurgical patients is theorized to result from an autoimmune insult to the nervous tissue, exposing myelin to the body’s immune system where antigen-antibody complexes can deposit in blood vessels of peripheral nerves [[6], [7], [8]]. When GBS occurs, postsurgical symptoms are documented as occurring anywhere from 1 to 8 weeks after the procedure. GBS is typically monophasic; however, it has been shown to recur in about 1%-7% of patients [1,9,10]. When GBS recurs, the symptoms at the nadir increase in severity, often leading to more serious respiratory compromise [11].
There is a paucity of literature on GBS in orthopaedics which is why clinical suspicion must be high when a patient is symptomatic, especially following a total knee arthroplasty (TKA) [8,12,13]. Furthermore, orthopaedic surgery has one of the highest rates of postsurgical GBS [5,6,8,[13], [14], [15]]. It is imperative to continue recognizing postsurgical GBS so that all potential triggers can be studied and identified. Here, we report a patient with recurrent GBS after subsequent TKAs about 11 years apart.

## Case history
Patient consent: The patient consented to the submission of his case for publication. Verbal and written consent was obtained from the patient.
The patient is a 76-year-old male with a past medical history of coronary artery disease status post 3 stents (2016), benign prostate hyperplasia, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and surgical history of C4-C7 anterior cervical discectomy and fusion, sigmoidoscopy with Hartmann’s procedure and reversal, and left cemented TKA. He developed postsurgical GBS 11 years prior, within a few weeks of his left TKA at an outside institution. The patient was scheduled for a routine right cemented TKA, including tourniquet use and the utilization of general anesthesia. He recovered uneventfully and was discharged the next day.
On postoperative day 11, the patient presented to the emergency department (ED) with paresthesia in the bilateral upper and lower extremities. Subjectively, the patient stated these are the same sensations felt when he had his left TKA 11 years prior. On motor examination, 4/5 finger abduction was appreciated, 5/5 elsewhere with diminished sensation up to the distal leg and forearms bilaterally and areflexia. The incision demonstrated no signs of infection. A lumbar puncture was suggested. However, the patient refused due to his history of previous lumbar injections. At this time, the patient was started on a 5-day course of 30 grams intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) and gabapentin. On day 3 of IVIG therapy, patients’ symptoms progressed. Bilateral lower extremity motor examination was significant for 4/5 to the iliopsoas, quadriceps, and hamstrings, 3/5 to the flexor hallucis longus and gastrocnemius, and 0/5 to the extensor hallucis longus and tibialis anterior. Sensory deficits had progressed up to the popliteal fossa. The upper extremity examination was unchanged. The patient’s respiratory status was also declining, needing supplemental oxygen while keeping the head of the bed elevated. On the fifth and final day of IVIG therapy, the patient’s physical examination demonstrated 3/5 motor to the bilateral iliopsoas, quadriceps, and hamstrings; the rest of the lower extremity examination was unchanged. The bilateral upper extremity examination was significant for 5/5 shoulder abduction, 4/5 elbow flexion and extension, 3/5 wrist flexion, extension, finger flexion, extension, and abduction. A diminished sensation in the C6-C8 distribution bilaterally was noted.
At this time, the decision was made to upgrade the patient to the intensive care unit due to respiratory deterioration. The patient was placed on bilevel-positive airway pressure (BiPAP) for 3 days. Due to further respiratory failure from fatigue on BiPAP, the patient was intubated. Once the patient was stable, computed tomographic angiography showed subsegmental acute pulmonary embolus in the left lower lobe. The patient was started on heparin and was not a candidate for intravenous thrombolysis or mechanical thrombectomy. The patient was planned for tracheostomy due to the inability to wean from the ventilator. Per the family’s wishes, the patient was discharged to long-term acute care (LTAC).
One month after being symptomatic for GBS, the patient was transferred to an LTAC with a tracheostomy and gastric feeding tube in place. The patient had a cardiac arrest with the return of spontaneous circulation 5 days after being admitted to the LTAC. The patient’s respiratory function improved within about 1 month. Before the cardiac arrest, the patient was on ventilation and respiratory support. After the cardiac arrest with return of spontaneous circulation, the patient’s respiration worsened with increasing ventilation demands. The patient was slowly weaned off synchronized intermittent mandatory ventilation to pressure support. Two months after discharge from the LTAC, the patient’s tracheostomy was removed.
Two months after admission to the LTAC, the patient was discharged to an acute rehabilitation facility. When the patient was admitted to the LTAC, he required maximum assistance for mobility, moving from supine to sitting and demonstrating no purposeful spontaneous movement in the bilaterally upper extremities. Upon discharge from the LTAC, the patient demonstrated improved mobility, being able to individually take himself from a supine position to a sitting position, and, with assistance, could bear weight on both lower extremities. In addition, the patient could engage in simple range of motion commands with assistance, such as marching, elbow extension, and shoulder flexion.
From the LTAC, the patient was discharged to a skilled nursing facility (SNF). The patient’s tracheostomy site healed, and he could speak fluently and tolerate oral intake. The patient’s motor strength in bilateral upper and lower extremities was 3+ to 4/5. At day 19 in the SNF, the patient was discharged home per the family’s request. Rehabilitation services recommended ambulation with a wheelchair at discharge. The patient spent approximately 3 months in the hospital, intensive care unit, LTAC, and SNF before being discharged home from his presentation to the ED on postoperative day 11.
Upon following up with the patient at 10 months postoperatively, he states his right knee is pain-free, and he is satisfied with the TKA. He is ambulating with a walker. His range of motion is from 0°-90° of knee flexion. The patient has 4/5 motor strength in the bilateral lower extremities except for bilateral foot drop due to the GBS. He notes diminished sensation in the right lateral femoral cutaneous nerve distribution and bilateral feet up to the ankle. He reports 10/10 pain in the bilateral feet and ankles. Motor strength in the bilateral upper extremities remained 3+ to 4/5. Regarding his respiratory status, his breathing has significantly improved, and he can eat without much difficulty.

## Discussion
As a mainly clinically diagnosed disorder, GBS was recognized and treated in this patient. His presentation was subjectively stated to be the same as when he was diagnosed with GBS 11 years prior, directly after his contralateral (left) TKA. The early recognition of GBS in this patient was critical as there is a reported 16% mortality rate in patients with postsurgical GBS [15]. In developed countries, in addition to respiratory depression, 5% of GBS patients can develop fatal medical complications such as sepsis and pulmonary embolus [1]. Similarly, our patient presented with symptoms of dyspnea requiring BiPAP and, eventually, intubation for airway protection. Our patient’s medical course was complicated with a pulmonary embolus, which was recognized and treated accordingly.
Current literature has no consensus on why GBS develops in postsurgical patients. Viral and gastrointestinal illnesses in the weeks preceding surgery have been suggested as potential triggers [[1], [2], [3]]. Typically, the most common infectious cause of GBS is Campylobacter jejuni, which has an incubation period that precedes the development of 2-5 days of diarrhea. Therefore, GBS rarely develops within a week of initial infection [1]. Our patient had no upper respiratory or gastrointestinal symptoms, including diarrhea, before or after surgery, making an infectious etiology of GBS less probable. There is currently no known form of prophylaxis for the development of GBS postoperatively. Corticosteroids have been investigated and have been shown not to significantly impact the long-term outcomes of GBS or hasten recovery [16]. This may be due to the effect corticosteroids have on inhibiting macrophage function [17]. Macrophage stripping of myelin is necessary for remyelination to occur, so the use of corticosteroids may delay or even prevent recovery from GBS [17].
Literature has shown evidence of GBS after long bone trauma, with an onset of GBS about 7-14 days after surgery [5,18,19]. Surgery-associated GBS occurs due to psychological stress, pain, and physical damage [8]. Huang et al. propose that psychological stress, pain, and trauma increase inflammatory factors that can be involved in the pathogenesis of GBS [8]. In our patient, the TKA induced trauma to his femur and tibia, and he returned to the ED 11 days postoperatively with symptoms suggestive of GBS. Literature suggests that possible trauma-induced GBS stems from heat shock protein 70-kDa and how it may play a role in developing GBS after trauma [8]. During surgical procedures, the expression of heat shock protein 70-kDa is altered, sharing the same pathogenesis of post-traumatic GBS, leading to antigen-antibody deposition impacting nervous tissue [8]. Other factors, such as pharmacologic agent chemical irritation during surgical procedures, should be investigated as a similar potential trigger for GBS.
One such factor could be the use of general anesthesia. A retrospective review examining clinical factors, including autoimmune conditions, reported 2 cases of GBS within 6 weeks of a TKA [13]. The same study found that general anesthesia accounted for the development of postsurgical GBS in 18 of 19 patients [13]. The last patient in the review developed GBS after conscious sedation [13]. None of the 208 reviewed patients developed GBS after spinal or local anesthesia [13]. Our patient has no history of autoimmune conditions and developed postsurgical GBS following general anesthesia, which coincides with the findings of this review.
However, our patient underwent various abdominal and spinal procedures without triggering GBS. Abdominal and spinal procedures typically use general anesthesia, which complicates the GBS trigger in our patient. The question begets why our patient only developed GBS after the TKAs. One possibility is the use of tourniquets during TKA. Tourniquet-induced compression leads to local nerve trauma, triggering a further cascade of immunologic events [5]. This can explain the development of GBS in our patient. Tourniquet use should be investigated as a trigger for GBS, especially since orthopaedic surgery is one of the most common surgeries that trigger GBS [2,4,5].
Since general anesthesia has been found to have a potential link to GBS, especially in orthopaedic surgeries, the use of spinal anesthetics should be considered as an alternative to prevent the recurrence of GBS [8]. However, epidural and spinal anesthesia have also been postulated to be potential triggers for GBS; this remains controversial [5,8,[19], [20], [21], [22]]. Further studies should investigate the role of general, epidural, and spinal anesthesia, as well as trauma, including tourniquet use, as potential triggers of GBS. The concomitant use of general anesthetics and tourniquets in TKA should be further investigated as potential triggers of GBS, as current literature has no definitive evidence of these factors as the cause of the development of postsurgical GBS. There may be a role for a more thorough screening process to identify respiratory and gastrointestinal symptoms in the weeks before a scheduled TKA for high-risk patients, which should be investigated further.

## Summary
GBS is a rare cause of flaccid paralysis, often occurring after viral or gastrointestinal illnesses. However, its recognition after surgical procedures is crucial to avoid its potentially fatal sequelae. Among all surgical procedures, GBS seems to occur most after orthopaedic surgery. A high clinical suspicion is needed to identify GBS early in its course so that it can be treated accordingly. The potential triggers of GBS, such as general anesthesia and tourniquet use, must be recognized to avoid postsurgical GBS and the recurrence of GBS.
