Natriuretic Peptide-Driven Fluid Management During Ventilator Weaning:
A Randomized Controlled Trial

Mekontso Dessap A, Roche-Campo F, Kouatchet A, Tomicic V, Beduneau G,
Sonneville R, Cabello B, Jaber S, Azoulay E, Castanares-Zapatero D, Devaquet J,
Lellouche F, Katsahian S, Brochard L.

Am J Respir Crit Care Med. 2012 Dec 15;186(12):1256-63.
DOI: 10.1164/rccm.201205-0939OC
PMID: 22997204

Source: Oxford Academic (free access), retrieved 2026-03-29

================================================================================

ABSTRACT

Rationale: Difficult weaning from mechanical ventilation is often associated
with fluid overload. B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) has been proposed as a
tool for predicting and detecting weaning failure of cardiovascular origin.

Objectives: To investigate whether fluid management guided by daily BNP plasma
concentrations improves weaning outcomes compared with empirical therapy
dictated by clinical acumen.

Methods: In a randomized controlled multicenter study, we allocated 304 patients
to either a BNP-driven or physician-driven strategy of fluid management during
ventilator weaning. To standardize the weaning process, patients in both groups
were ventilated with an automatic computer-driven weaning system. The primary
end point was time to successful extubation.

Measurements and Main Results: In the BNP-driven group, furosemide and
acetazolamide were given more often and in higher doses than in the control
group, resulting in a more negative median (interquartile range) fluid balance
during weaning (-2,320 [-4,735, 738] vs. -180 [-2,556, 2,832] ml; P < 0.0001).
Time to successful extubation was significantly shorter with the BNP-driven
strategy (58.6 [23.3, 139.8] vs. 42.4 [20.8, 107.5] h; P = 0.034). The
BNP-driven strategy increased the number of ventilator-free days but did not
change length of stay or mortality. The effect on weaning time was strongest in
patients with left ventricular systolic dysfunction. The two strategies did not
differ significantly regarding electrolyte imbalance, renal failure, or shock.

Conclusions: Our results suggest that a BNP-driven fluid management strategy
decreases the duration of weaning without increasing adverse events, especially
in patients with left ventricular systolic dysfunction.

Clinical trial registered with www.clinicaltrials.gov (NCT00473148).

================================================================================

INTRODUCTION

Mechanical ventilation complications escalate with prolonged duration. Since
weaning constitutes roughly 40% of total ventilation time, optimizing this
process represents the primary mechanism for shortening respiratory support
duration.

Multiple investigations suggest mechanically ventilated ICU patient outcomes
improve through reduced pulmonary capillary wedge pressure and minimized
positive fluid balance during weaning. Fluid overload demonstrably precipitates
cardiac-origin weaning failure.

BNP, secreted by ventricular cardiomyocytes responding to increased wall stress,
independently predicts weaning failure at baseline assessment. Natriuretic
peptides show documented utility for predicting and diagnosing cardiac-origin
weaning failure.

The authors hypothesized that daily BNP-guided fluid management would outperform
empirical clinician-directed approaches during weaning. They tested this using
automated weaning systems (AWS) across multiple international centers.

================================================================================

METHODS

Patient Selection
-----------------

Daily ICU screening identified eligible candidates. Inclusion criteria required
endotracheal mechanical ventilation >=24 hours, SpO2 >=90% with FiO2 <=50% and
PEEP <=8 cm H2O, hemodynamic stability without vasopressor therapy or recent
fluid boluses, decreased sedation over preceding 48 hours, stable neurological
status (Ramsay score <=5), and temperature 36-39 degrees C.

Permanent exclusions included pregnancy/lactation, age <18 years, documented
furosemide/sulfonamide allergy, tracheostomy, hepatic encephalopathy, cerebral
edema, acute hydrocephalus, myasthenia gravis, acute polyradiculoneuropathy, and
withdrawal-of-care decisions.

Temporary exclusions encompassed scheduled same-day extubation, acute right
ventricular failure, renal insufficiency, recent iodinated contrast exposure,
hypernatremia (>150 mEq/L), hypokalemia (<3.5 mEq/L), and metabolic alkalosis
(pH >7.50).

Study Protocol
--------------

Volume-assist or pressure-control ventilated patients underwent positive
pressure support (PS) testing before enrollment. Already PS-ventilated patients
required confirmation of positivity criteria. Spontaneous breathing trials were
not mandated; only PS testing was required.

Randomization
-------------

Eligible patients with positive PS tests underwent centralized web-based block
randomization via independent platforms, stratified by center and underlying
disease (COPD presence, left ventricular systolic dysfunction [LVD, ejection
fraction <45%], or neither). This ensured homogeneous COPD and LVD distribution
across treatment arms.

BNP Measurement
---------------

Morning blood samples underwent BNP analysis using rapid immunofluorescence
testing (Triage BNP test; Biosite). Intervention-group devices displayed
results; control-group devices concealed results until study conclusion.

Fluid Management Strategies
----------------------------

Control Group: Clinicians remained blinded to BNP values. All treatments,
including diuretics, followed standard institutional practices without explicit
protocols.

BNP-Guided Group: On days with BNP >=200 pg/ml, aggressive fluid restriction
was implemented (baseline infusion <=500 ml/24h, parenteral nutrition <=1,000
ml/24h, no saline solutions beyond nutrition and medications) with intravenous
furosemide boluses (10-30 mg every 3 hours targeting 4.5-9 ml/kg/3h urine
output). The 200 pg/ml threshold reflected prior evidence distinguishing weaning
success from failure. Fluid restriction and diuretics continued >=24 hours
post-extubation.

Daily monitoring included sodium, potassium, urea, creatinine, and arterial
blood gas analysis. Protocols provided recommendations preventing/treating
diuretic-related adverse effects.

Ventilatory Management
----------------------

Both groups used AWS, gradually reducing PS levels while maintaining respiratory
comfort. Once AWS declared patients "ready for separation," extubation proceeded
after confirming additional criteria. Assist-control ventilation resumed if
respiratory deterioration occurred (respiratory rate >40/min or hypoxemia
requiring FiO2 >60% and PEEP >8 cm H2O). Tracheostomy was deferred until Day 10
when possible.

Endpoints
---------

Primary: Time from randomization to successful extubation (alive without
reintubation 72 hours post-extubation).

Secondary: Time to first extubation, time to successful weaning from
invasive/noninvasive ventilation, ventilator-free days within 60 days, ICU/
hospital lengths of stay, ICU/hospital mortality, and Day-60 mortality.

Statistical Analysis
--------------------

Sample size calculations assumed >=40% weaning duration reduction in the BNP
group compared to controls (alpha=0.05, power=0.90). Previous AWS trials
reported 4.4 +/- 4.0 days median weaning. Assuming higher variability and
accounting for potential nonparametric testing requirements, 150 patients per
group were deemed necessary.

Categorical variables underwent chi-square or Fisher exact testing. Continuous
variables underwent Mann-Whitney testing. Kaplan-Meier methods assessed
successful extubation probability. Breslow-Gehan-Wilcoxon testing examined
differences between groups. Gray model analysis considered continuous sedation
requirements as competing events. Two-tailed P < 0.05 indicated significance.

================================================================================

RESULTS

Enrollment and Baseline Characteristics
----------------------------------------

Of 1,464 screened patients (May 2007-July 2009), 306 underwent randomization
(152 control, 154 BNP-guided). Two BNP-guided patients were excluded due to
withdrawn consent unrelated to protocol aspects.

Baseline similarities included demographics, intubation reasons, illness
severity, respiratory function, pre-study mechanical ventilation duration, and
urine output. BNP values at randomization and COPD/LVD proportions were
comparable. Overall, patients with LVD showed higher baseline BNP (552 pg/ml)
than COPD (263 pg/ml) or neither-disease groups (230 pg/ml).

Diuretics and Fluid Balance
----------------------------

Comparable proportions experienced >=1 daily BNP value >=200 pg/ml during
weaning. The BNP-guided cohort received diuretics more frequently at higher
doses:

- Furosemide use: 81.6% versus 71.1%
- Acetazolamide use: 42.8% versus 21.7%
- Median cumulative furosemide: 118 mg versus 70 mg

This produced significantly more negative median fluid balance during weaning
(-2,320 ml versus -180 ml; P < 0.0001) and greater average daily urine output
(2,836 ml versus 2,273 ml; P < 0.0001). Fluid intake was comparable.
Extubation-day and post-extubation fluid balances showed no significant
differences.

Primary Outcomes
----------------

Time to successful extubation was significantly shorter in the BNP-guided group
(median 42.4 hours versus 58.6 hours; P = 0.034). Time to first extubation also
favored intervention (39.8 hours versus 47.7 hours; P = 0.019).

Ventilator-free days increased substantially:
- Days 1-14: 12.0 versus 9.7 days (P = 0.026)
- Days 1-28: 25.9 versus 23.3 days (P = 0.038)
- Days 1-60: 57.9 versus 54.9 days (P = 0.015)

Successful weaning from invasive/noninvasive ventilation showed borderline
significance (49.3 hours versus 74.4 hours; P = 0.051).

Length of stay was comparable (ICU: 8.0 days both groups; Hospital: 20.0 days
both groups). Mortality rates showed no differences (ICU: 11.8% versus 12.5%;
Hospital: 13.2% versus 16.4%; Day-60: 13.8% versus 18.4%).

Subgroup Analysis: Strongest benefits appeared in LVD patients, with significant
differences in time to first extubation, successful extubation, and successful
weaning. COPD and neither-disease groups showed less pronounced effects.

Complications During Weaning
-----------------------------

Respiratory complications: BNP-guided patients experienced fewer clinical
worsenings requiring reventilation (27.6% versus 43.4%; P = 0.004) and less
ventilator-associated pneumonia (9.2% versus 17.8%; P = 0.029). Noninvasive
ventilation needs post-extubation, 72-hour reintubation rates, tracheostomy
rates, and prolonged ventilation (>14 days) were similar.

Cardiovascular complications: Supraventricular/ventricular arrhythmias,
hypotension, and catecholamine requirements were comparable. BNP-guided patients
required less fluid loading (23.7% versus 34.9%; P = 0.032).

Neurological: Fewer BNP-guided patients needed continuous sedation for worsening
(40.1% versus 52.6%; P = 0.029). Analgesia requirements were similar.

Renal/metabolic: No between-group differences in hypokalemia, hypernatremia,
metabolic alkalosis, or renal failure. No dialysis was required in either group.

================================================================================

DISCUSSION

This trial demonstrates that straightforward BNP-guided fluid management
reduces mechanical ventilation duration without increasing organ failures.

Mechanistic advantages of BNP guidance likely include preventive,
patient-tailored strategies enabling conservative fluid management in high-risk
populations. Acute heart failure represents a common unsuccessful-weaning cause.
Its complex pathophysiology involves intrathoracic pressure changes, cardiac
loading alterations, left ventricular systolic/diastolic dysfunction, and fluid
overload. Positive fluid balance associates with prolonged ventilation and
extubation failure. Previous open studies demonstrated successful weaning
achievement following diuretic treatment in weaning-induced heart failure. BNP
measurement incentivized negative fluid balance while predefined protocols
standardized management, minimizing bias.

Prior goal-directed fluid management studies in mechanically ventilated
critically ill patients demonstrated beneficial effects targeting lower fluid
balances. However, they differed in protocols, populations, and intervention
timing. This study used BNP in early-weaning-eligible patients, whereas prior
research typically employed complex algorithms targeting invasive parameters
(extravascular lung water, central venous pressure, pulmonary artery occlusion
pressure). BNP offers practical advantages: invasive measurements prove
difficult implementing during weaning's decreasing-invasiveness phase, and large
intrathoracic pressure swings complicate conventional cardiovascular dysfunction
diagnosis. These factors have generated cardiac biomarker interest during
weaning. Prior data suggest natriuretic peptides may predict weaning outcomes
and distinguish cardiovascular-origin weaning failure.

Superior outcomes with explicit BNP-guided pharmacotherapy versus empirical
clinician approaches have been documented elsewhere, particularly outpatient
chronic heart failure and emergency dyspnea presentations. This study's
strongest LVD benefits align with BNP physiology: natriuretic peptides are
left-ventricle-secreted responding to left-ventricular wall stretch, correlating
closely with filling pressures in LVD patients. LVD represents
cardiovascular-origin weaning-failure risk. High-pressure pulmonary edema and
inadequate cardiac output may significantly impede LVD weaning. Conversely,
other groups experience weaning difficulties from respiratory-mechanical,
elevated-work-of-breathing, or CO2-retention factors (particularly COPD).
Additionally, COPD-related elevated BNP may partly reflect pulmonary
hypertension and right-ventricular afterload increases -- cardiac dysfunction
potentially less responsive to diuretics.

Lower ventilator-associated pneumonia rates in BNP-guided cohorts may reflect
decreased ventilator-exposure risk (earlier separation) and fluid balance's
direct infectivity effects. Strict pneumonia-diagnosis criteria limited
respiratory-symptom-worsening misattribution to pulmonary edema.

Hemodynamic/renal safety: The BNP-guided strategy produced no adverse
hemodynamic or renal consequences. Arterial pressure, vasopressor requirements,
blood urea, and creatinine were comparable. Protocol design minimized risks;
diuretic therapy underwent patient-response titration and was withheld during
worsening renal function. Acetazolamide frequent use prevented alkalemia. Close
electrolyte monitoring maintained comparable levels.

Study limitations included testing specific management strategies with multiple
safeguards, precluding certainty regarding simplified zero-balance or
zero-weight-gain targets. Inclusion/exclusion criteria departures could produce
different outcomes. ICU fluid-balance culture may influence generalizability.
Although cardiac dysfunction predominantly drives BNP variations, sepsis and
renal failure also significantly contribute. Renal-failure patients were excluded
due to renal-function influences on BNP levels.

The weaning procedure's relative brevity in control groups may reflect selection
criteria and/or AWS use. AWS allowed optimal weaning standardization. Given lack
of blinding and participating physician awareness of study questions,
control-group diuretic utilization may have exceeded usual practice, potentially
minimizing between-group weaning-duration differences. Although greater clinician
involvement in BNP-guided groups cannot be excluded, strict diuresis protocols
and AWS use achieved relatively physician-independent weaning.

================================================================================

CONCLUSIONS

BNP-driven fluid management during weaning decreased fluid balance and weaning
duration without increasing adverse events compared with physician-guided
approaches, particularly in LVD patients. No significant mortality rate or
length-of-stay differences emerged.

================================================================================

KEY FINDINGS SUMMARY

- Intervention: BNP values >=200 pg/ml triggered fluid restriction and diuretic
  protocols
- Primary finding: 16-hour median extubation-time reduction (58.6 -> 42.4 hours)
- Safety profile: No increased electrolyte imbalances, renal failure, or
  mortality
- Strongest benefit: Left ventricular systolic dysfunction patients
- Mechanism: Reduced fluid overload facilitating cardiac-origin weaning success
- Study design: Multicenter RCT, n=304, May 2007-July 2009
- Registration: NCT00473148
