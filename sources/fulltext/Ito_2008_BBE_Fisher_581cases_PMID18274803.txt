Introduction 
Fisher [10] reported three cases of acute ophthalmoplegia, 
ataxia, and areflexia subsequent to upper respiratory infectious symptoms, and postulated that the disorder is a Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS) variant because of CSF albuminocytological dissociation in one. Some patients with Fisher syndrome (FS) progress to GBS [22], proving Fisher’s postulation. Bickerstaff [4] reported eight such cases and named the condition “brain-stem encephalitis”, a definitive syndrome. Seven of those eight patients had symmetrical ophthalmoplegia, ataxia, and impaired consciousness. The etiology of Bickerstaff’s brainstem encephalitis (BBE) was speculated to be similar to that of GBS because prodromal upper respiratory infection and CSF albuminocytological dissociation were present. One of Bickerstaff’s original patients had flaccid limb weakness. In a study that included 62 patients with BBE, 37 had limb weakness, considered the result of an overlapping axonal subtype of GBS [26]. T okyo 145-0064, Japan■ Abstract   Whether Bickerstaff’s 
brainstem encephalitis (BBE) is a distinct disease or a subtype of Fisher syndrome (FS) is unclear as there have been no clinical studies with sufficiently large numbers of patients with FS or BBE. Our aim was to clarify the nosological relationship. Medical records of patients suffering acute ophthalmoplegia and ataxia within four weeks of onset were reviewed. BBE was the diagnosis for patients with impaired consciousness, FS for those with clear consciousness and areflexia. Clinical features, neuroimages, and laboratory find-ings were analyzed. Patients were grouped as having BBE (n = 53), FS (n = 466), or as unclassified (n = 62). The BBE and FS groups had similar features; positive serum anti-GQ1b IgG antibody (68 % versus 83 %), antecedent Campylobacter jejuni  
infection (23 % versus 21 %), CSF 
albuminocytological dissociation (46 % versus 76 %), brain MRI 
abnormality (11 % versus 2 %), and abnormal EEG findings (57 % versus 25 %). BBE (n = 4) and FS (n = 28) subgroups underwent detailed electrophysiological testing. Both groups frequently showed absent soleus H-reflexes, but normal sensory nerve conduc-tion (75 % versus 74 %) and a 1-Hz power spectrum peak on postural body sway analysis (67 % versus 72 %). Common autoantibodies, antecedent infections, and MRI and neurophysiological results found in this large study offer conclusive ev-idence that Bickerstaff’s brainstem encephalitis and Fisher syndrome form a continuous spectrum with variable CNS and PNS involvement.

One of the three patients originally described by 
Fisher [10] experienced drowsiness, and four of the eight patients described by Bickerstaff [4] had hypo- or areflexia. Subsequently, Bickerstaff’s group reported 18 other patients who had “brainstem encephalitis and the syndrome of Miller Fisher” and argued for a central origin [1]. All 18 had ophthalmoplegia and ataxia. Eleven experienced drowsiness, and one was comatose. T endon reflexes were absent in 11, normal in three, and brisk in four. Based on radiological (three patients) and pathological (one patient) changes in the brainstem, as well as abnormal EEG findings (12 patients), they insisted that the responsible lesion is localized in the CNS. Ropper [30], who criticized their report, considered six of the 18 cases typical FS, and the other 12 obscure brainstem lesions without peripheral polyneuropathy. 
Serum IgG antibody to ganglioside GQ1b, reported 
as a possible FS marker [7], was also detected in BBE 
patients [35]. The immunological profile common 
to FS and BBE supports a common pathogenesis. A comprehensive, case-control study was made that identified serologically antecedent infectious agents in FS and GBS [16] but not in BBE. Because of the lack of studies of large numbers of patients with FS or BBE, it is unclear whether BBE is a distinct disease or FS subtype. For our study we therefore reviewed medical records of 581 patients who had acute ophthalmoplegia and ataxia but no significant limb weakness. We analyzed their clinical features and laboratory findings and obtained conclusive evidence that FS and BBE form a continuous spectrum with variable CNS and PNS involvement. 
Methods
■ Patients and diagnostic criteria 
Between 1999 and 2006, approximately 9200 patients were referred for 
serum anti-ganglioside antibody testing from hospitals throughout 
Japan, which excluded the BBE patients reported in our previous study [26]. The Ethical Committee at Dokkyo Medical University ap-
proved performance of this study. Clinical information was obtained 
from each primary physician. CSF albuminocytological dissociation 
was defined as a raised protein concentration (more than 45 mg/dl) 
associated with a count of 10 or fewer leukocytes/μl. CSF pleocytosis 
was defined as the increased count of 11 or more leukocytes/μl.
 Based on Fisher’s [10] and Bickerstaff’s [4] findings, “progressive, relatively symmetric external ophthalmoplegia and ataxia by four 
weeks” are the clinical features necessary for a diagnosis of both BBE 
and FS. Whereas “hypo- or areflexia” and clear consciousness were re-
quired for the diagnosis of FS, “impaired consciousness” is for that of 
BBE. Hypo- or areflexia was not an exclusion criterion for the diagno-sis of BBE because half of the original cases had hypo- or areflexia. In 
our study, “severe limb weakness” of 3 or less on the Medical Research Council (MRC) scale was an exclusion criterion for both conditions, although there was gross flaccid limb weakness in Bickerstaff’s Case 4 
[4]. In contrast, limb weakness of 4 on the MRC scale was an inclusion 
criterion for either condition because there was mild limb weakness in Fisher’s original Case 1 [10]. For the diagnosis of either condition, the 
following must be excluded: vascular disease involving the brainstem, Wernicke’s encephalopathy, botulism, myasthenia gravis, brainstem tumor, pituitary apoplexy, acute disseminated encephalomyelitis (disorders described by Bickerstaff and Cloake, Fisher, Bickerstaff, and Al-Din et al.) [1, 4, 5, 10], multiple sclerosis, neuro-Behçet disease, vasculitis, lymphoma, and Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease (our addition). Based on GBS criteria [2],
 extensor plantar reflexes do not exclude an 
FS diagnosis if other clinical features are typical.
■ Anti-ganglioside and infectious serology
Serum IgG antibodies to GQ1b, GT1a, GM1b, GalNAc-GD1a, and GD1b 
(Fig. 1) were measured by an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay 
[37]. Serum was considered positive for anti-ganglioside antibodies 
when the absorbance value was 0.1 or higher at a dilution 1:500. 
 Sera obtained from 34 patients with BBE, 73 with FS, and 44 
with unclassified conditions (see Pa tient selection in Results) were 
available for infection serology tests. Evidence of recent infections by Campylobacter jejuni , Haemophilus influenzae , cytomegalovirus, 
Epstein-Barr virus, and Mycoplasma pneumoniae  were assayed 
serologically [16]: Sera from 73 hospital control subjects were used. 
Frequency differences between groups were compared by the χ
2 or 
Fisher’s exact (two-tailed) test. A p value of less than 0.05 was consid-
ered significant.

Results
■ Patient selection
Based on medical records available to us, 896 patients 
who showed progressive, relatively symmetric external ophthalmoplegia and ataxia by four weeks were recruited. 
Insufficient information for clinical analysis excluded 280 patients and 35 others were excluded because of limb weakness of 3 or less on the MRC scale during the course of illness. The 581 selected patients were divided into three groups: 53 with typical BBE who had impaired consciousness; 466 with typical FS who had alert consciousness and hypo- or areflexia; 62 patients were unclassified because of clear consciousness and preserved muscle stretch reflexes during their illnesses.

Discussion
■ Clinical similarities
The findings of this large study provide significant 
information about the clinical features of BBE and FS and convincing evidence of their clinical similarity. In both BBE and FS, males predominated, and the age distribution showed two peaks. Symptoms of anteced-
ent infectious illnesses were most common for the upper respiratory system. Blepharoptosis and internal ophthalmoplegia were frequent during the illness. Deep sensation was relatively unimpaired despite profound ataxia. 
CSF albuminocytological dissociation occurred in 
1/4 of our BBE patients during the first week of illness, increasing to 1/2 in the second week, but each dissocia-tion frequency was lower than in our FS patients. The presence of CSF albuminocytological dissociation in 
BBE, as well as in FS, suggests that they have a common etiology. In addition, 1/3 of the BBE patients had CSF pleocytosis, compared to 1/20 of the FS patients. These findings show that CSF studies alone cannot discrimi-nate BBE from FS. Breakdown of the blood-CSF barrier may be more severe in BBE, thereby producing CSF pleocytosis more often than in FS. 
■ Both CNS and PNS involvement
Although the presence of brainstem lesions in BBE was 
doubted [30], postmortem examinations of three BBE patients found definite inflammatory changes in the brainstem [1, 4, 26]. Our BBE patients also had definite CNS involvement because all had impaired conscious-ness. Hyperreflexia and Babinski’s sign were present in 1/3 of the BBE patients. MRI detected CNS lesions in 1/10 of the BBE patients, and EEG abnormalities in 3/5. In contrast, EEG recordings showed diffuse slow activities in 1/4 of the fully conscious FS patients tested. In the three FS patients, T
2-weighted MRI showed high 
intensity abnormalities in the brainstem and cerebel-lum. In an alert FS patient, T
1-weighted MRI showed 
areas with increased signal intensity in the cerebral 
white matter, as well as in the brainstem and cerebellum [8]. Supranuclear, nuclear, and internuclear ocular mo-tor signs have been reported in several FS patients [23]. These observations indicate that central components are 
occasionally associated with FS. 
Some investigators have suggested that lesions 
scattered in the brainstem tegmentum and cerebel-lar peduncles are responsible for ataxia and areflexia [24], but neurophysiological study results suggest that peripheral abnormalities occur in some FS patients [11, 14, 31]. Results showed that major abnormalities were the absence of soleus H-reflexes and a 1-Hz peak in body sway analyses. Body sway analysis is the classic method used to evaluate the proprioceptive afferent system. Postural sway with a 1-Hz frequency has been reported in patients with tabes dorsalis or ataxic polyneuropathy [3], and a previous FS study found nine of 10 patients had a 1-Hz peak [20]. Our study confirmed these findings and showed that BBE patients also had this peak, indicating that ataxia is caused by a mecha-nism, dysfunction of the proprioceptive afferent system, common to FS and BBE. Patients with FS or BBE showed both CNS and PNS involvement [8, 27], supporting the hypothesis of continuity between those conditions. The coexistence of central and peripheral components refutes the idea of a simple relationship between BBE and purely central involvement, as well as between FS and a simple peripheral neuropathy.  
■ Immunopathogenesis
Most patients with BBE or FS who had been studied had 
had infectious symptoms before the onset of neurologi-cal symptoms. A history of antecedent upper respiratory infection was frequent for both conditions, but most of the causative microorganisms were not identified. Our 
comprehensive study, however, found H. influenzae  to be 
the causative agent in some BBE patients, as reported in FS patients [16]. It is interesting that in Fisher’s original publication he reported isolation of H. influenzae  from 
the sputum of one of his three patients [10]. Our BBE patients with serologic evidence of recent H. influenzae  
infection all had had upper respiratory infection symp-toms. H. influenzae  was isolated both from those with 
BBE and with FS, but bacterial isolation is not always indicative of the causative agent, as discussed elsewhere [17]. In both conditions, a history of preceding diarrhea was less common than one of preceding upper respira-tory infection symptoms, as reported in GBS [33]. Of previously identified microorganisms, our study showed C. jejuni  to be the most frequent agent in BBE, as well as 
in FS [16]. The presence of common, antecedent infec-tious agents such as C. jejuni and H. influenzae in both 
conditions supports the hypothesis that BBE and FS have similar etiologies, as is the case for GBS and FS [34]. 
Anti-GQ1b IgG antibody was positive in 8/10 of the 
FS and 7/10 of the BBE patients, but found to be negative in healthy and disease control participants, including those with brainstem infarctions and Wernicke’s en-cephalopathy [7, 35]. The fact that both conditions have this autoantibody in common is strong support for FS and BBE representing a single autoimmune disease. As stated, C. jejuni  was the most frequent microbial 
agent identified in FS and BBE, and was isolated from both groups of patients. Lipo-oligosaccharide (LOS) is a major cell-surface structure expressed by C. jejuni . 
The bacterial sialyltransferase Cst-II functions in the 
biosynthesis of ganglioside-like LOS, the 51
st amino acid 
determining its enzymatic activity [12]. Cst-II (Thr51) produces GM1-like and GD1a-like LOSs, and cst-II  
(Thr51) strains are associated with GBS [18]. In contrast, Cst-II (Asn51) produces GT1a-like and GD1c-like LOSs (Fig. 1), in which each terminal trisaccharide is identical to that of GQ1b [15, 18]; moreover, cst-II (Asn51) strains 
are associated with FS [18]. We recently showed that the characteristics of a C. jejuni  isolate from a BBE patient 
were those of FS not GBS isolates [15]. That isolate had cst-II (Asn51) and the GQ1b epitope on LOS, evidence 
that BBE and FS are part of a continuous spectrum and that BBE is closer to FS than to GBS. 
Human immunohistochemical studies showed that 
GQ1b is highly expressed in extramedullary regions of the ocular motor nerves and some large neurons of dorsal root ganglia [6, 19]. Patients with mild BBE show drowsi-ness but are easily roused by stimulation, suggesting that 
the brainstem’s reticular formation is involved in BBE. The blood-brain barrier (BBB) protects the brain from deleterious effects of large molecules circulating in the 
blood, but there are several areas where the BBB is defi-cient. Studies on the permeability [9] and ultrastructure [32] of the area postrema have demonstrated that the BBB of this region is relatively permeable, allowing large 
molecules to penetrate into the brainstem parenchyma at this site. It is possible that in BBE the anti-GQ1b an-tibody reaches the brainstem via this route, and attacks the brainstem reticular formation, but this has yet to be demonstrated experimentally. Anti-GQ1b monoclonal antibody reacts with LOSs from FS-related H. influ-
enzae strains [16], indicative that those LOSs have the 
GQ1b epitope. The possible sequence of events in the pathogenesis of BBE and FS therefore is the following. (1) Infection by a microorganism bearing the GQ1b epitope induces production of anti-GQ1b IgG antibody in certain patients. (2) The anti-GQ1b antibody binds to GQ1b, which is expressed on the oculomotor nerves and primary sensory neurons, inducing FS. (3) In some cases anti-GQ1b antibody also enters the brainstem through areas where the BBB is deficient, e.g. area postrema, and (4) binds to GQ1b which may be expressed on the 
brainstem reticular formation, thus inducing BBE. 
Several patients with FS and ataxic GBS have been 
reported to have anti-GM1b, anti-GD1b, or anti-Gal-NAc-GD1a IgG antibody but not anti-GQ1b antibody [25, 36]. Our study adds patients who have either FS or BBE. Although some humans may express GM1b, GD1b, or GalNAc-GD1a in their oculomotor nerves, primary sensory neurons, and brainstems, further immunohisto-chemical studies are required to test our hypothesis.

■ Proposal of “Fisher-Bickerstaff syndrome” and its 
treatment
As discussed above, BBE is not distinct from FS clini-
cally, anatomically, or etiologically. These two conditions 
therefore represent a single autoimmune disease that variably involves the PNS and CNS. Clinical findings, anatomical lesions, and immunological findings for our unclassified condition group were similar to those for BBE and FS. A nosological position is needed for un-classified conditions. FS is familiar to most neurologists, whereas BBE is not. Fisher [10] reported the original cases in 1956, and Bickerstaff and Cloake [5] their first cases in 1951, which they classified as BBE. We propose the new eponymic terminology “Fisher-Bickerstaff syn-drome” which includes unclassified conditions as well as FS and BBE. 
Randomized controlled trials have established the 
efficacy of plasma change and IVIG for GBS [13, 29]. Their efficacy for use in Fisher-Bickerstaff syndrome has yet to be shown because there have been no ran-
domized controlled trials [28]. This retrospective study could also not show whether immunotherapy is effec-tive for Fisher-Bickerstaff syndrome, and whether each therapeutic response differs between FS and BBE. Ret-rospective analysis of 92 consecutive FS cases, however, showed that IVIG somewhat lessened ophthalmoplegia and ataxia but had no effect on outcomes, presumably because of good natural recoveries [21]. Most of our FS patients had received IVIG or plasmapheresis, which may have been unnecessary. Although BBE outcomes generally are good, several BBE patients have died [1, 4, 26]. We therefore suggest that patients with BBE should be treated with IVIG or plasmapheresis, which should be confirmed in future studies.
