REVIEW ARTICLE
Differentiating recurrent Guillain–Barre syndrome and acute‑onset 
chronic inflammatory polyneuropathy: literature review
Berin Inan1,2  · Can Ebru Bekircan‑Kurt1  · Mehmet Demirci3  · Sevim Erdem‑Ozdamar1  · Ersin Tan1 
Received: 6 December 2023 / Accepted: 7 April 2024 / Published online: 25 April 2024 
© The Author(s) under exclusive licence to Belgian Neurological Society 2024
Abstract
Guillain–Barre syndrome (GBS) is an acute-onset immune-mediated polyneuropathy characterized by ascending symmetrical 
muscle weakness, diminished reflexes, and sensory symptoms. While GBS typically follows a monophasic course, some 
patients experience treatment-related fluctuations or recurrences, posing diagnostic challenges in distinguishing GBS from 
acute-onset chronic inflammatory polyneuropathy (A-CIDP). A-CIDP, may present acutely, simulating GBS, with a nadir 
in less than 8 weeks, subsequently evolving into a chronic or relapsing course. The distinction between recurrent GBS and 
A-CIDP is crucial, as A-CIDP necessitates long-term immunosuppression. A PubMed search was conducted using the search 
terms ‘recurrent Guillain Barre syndrome’ and ‘acute onset CIDP’ focusing on studies in the English language, published 
between January 1, 2004 and April 30, 2023. Overlapping clinical features, particularly in the early stages, complicate dif-
ferentiation between recurrent GBS and CIDP. Electrophysiological studies, ultrasonography, and immunological markers 
have been explored for discrimination; however, definitive criteria for differentiation remain elusive. Recent follow-up studies 
have further blurred the boundaries between recurrent GBS and A-CIDP, suggesting the persistence of underlying immune 
processes even in GBS patients without clinical deterioration. This emphasizes the necessity of reevaluating diagnostic 
criteria and treatment strategies. In conclusion, distinguishing recurrent GBS from A-CIDP remains an ongoing challenge. 
Existing evidence questions the categorization of recurrent GBS as a distinct entity, challenging its very existence. Continued 
research is necessary to refine diagnostic criteria and deepen our understanding of these conditions, ultimately advancing 
patient care. This review delves into the intricacies of recurrent GBS and A-CIDP differentiation and highlights the need for 
a reevaluation of the recurrent GBS concept.

Introduction
Guillain–Barre syndrome (GBS) is an acute-onset immune-
mediated polyneuropathy with an incidence of 1–2/1,00,000 
people per year [1 –5]. GBS is typically characterized by 
ascending symmetrical muscle weakness and absent or 
decreased deep tendon reflexes. Patients may also have sensory and autonomic symptoms [2 ]. GBS has several vari-
ants, with acute inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy 
(AIDP) constituting approximately 85–90% of all GBS cases 
in the United States and Europe [6 ]. In contrast, the pri-
mary axonal forms of GBS, acute motor axonal neuropathy 
(AMAN) and acute motor and sensory axonal neuropathy 
(AMSAN), are more prevalent in Asia [7 ]. Miller Fisher syn-
drome (MFS) and Bickerstaff brainstem encephalitis (BBE) 
are other common variants [3 ]. GBS is often triggered by 
an immune response to a preceding infection or other event 
that cross-reacts with peripheral nerve components because 
of molecular mimicry [3 ]. Campylobacter jejuni infection is 
the most common antecedent event in GBS [8 ]. Cytomegalo-
virus (CMV), Influenza A and B, human immunodeficiency 
virus (HIV), COVID-19 virus, and Zika virus are some of 
the other viruses which have been associated with GBS

[8–14]. GBS has also been linked to vaccinations, trauma,

GBS has a monophasic course and reaches its peak sever -
ity within 4 weeks in most cases [1 , 2]. However, approxi-
mately 5–6% of patients with GBS exhibit treatment-related 
fluctuations (TRF) [1 , 17–19], defined as worsening of at 
least one grade on the GBS disability scale or by at least 
five points on the Medical Research Council (MRC) sum 
score after improvement or stabilization is achieved by 
immunotherapy within the first 8 weeks after the disease 
onset, not occurring more than two times [3 , 17, 18, 20]. In 
addition, recurrences have been reported in 2–5% of patients 
with GBS [21]. Recurrent GBS is defined as the presence 
of at least two GBS episodes with a nadir within 4 weeks 
and with an interval of ≥ 4 months if the recovery is incom-
plete after the first episode or an interval of ≥ 2 months if 
the recovery is complete / near-complete [22]. On the other 
hand, chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy 
(CIDP) is a chronic acquired immune-mediated neuropathy 
[23], typically characterized by clinical worsening over the 
course of at least 8 weeks [23, 24]. Yet, 16% of patients 
with CIDP, classified as acute-onset CIDP (A-CIDP), may 
present acutely with a nadir in less than 8 weeks simulat-
ing GBS, followed by a chronic or relapsing course beyond 
8 weeks [20, 25, 26]. Patients with three or more TRFs are 
also included in this definition [25]. However, boundaries 
between diagnoses are not crystal-clear in all cases. There 
are overlapping clinical presentations, especially in the early 
stages, and 2–5% of patients initially diagnosed with GBS 
turn out to have A-CIDP subsequently [20].
Distinguishing the patients with GBS and A-CIDP cor -
rectly is crucial because patients with A-CIDP require long-
term immunosuppression. Hence, we aim to provide a nar -
rative review of literature on recurrent GBS (AIDP, AMAN 
and AMSAN variants) and A-CIDP.
Materials and methods
We conducted a PubMed search using the search terms 
‘recurrent Guillain Barre syndrome’ and ‘acute onset CIDP’ 
to reach all article types written in English language. All rel-
evant articles published between January 1, 2004 and April 
30, 2023 were examined.
Results
Studies about recurrent GBS
Recurrent GBS is a contentious diagnosis reported to be 
present in 2–5% of patients with GBS in the literature [21, 
27]. Several studies and case series have provided insights into the common characteristics and distinctions among 
recurrent GBS cases. Various studies have indicated that 
younger patients are more likely to experience recurrences 
[22, 27–29], particularly those under 30 years of age [29]. 
However, there have been cases of recurrent GBS presented 
with first episode after the age of 60 [30– 32]. Recurrences 
are more frequent in AIDP when compared to axonal vari-
ants of GBS [22, 27, 33]. Antecedent illness serves an inde -
pendent risk factor for recurrent GBS [22]. The preceding 
events may vary [9 , 12, 29–31, 34–39] or remain consistent 
[10, 32, 40–42] in recurrent episodes. Furthermore, a recent 
study has demonstrated that a high level of thyroid-stimu-
lating hormone (TSH) is another independent risk factor for 
recurrent GBS [22].
Clinical features during episodes are usually similar with 
varying severity [29, 32, 36, 39, 43, 44]. Some cases have 
shown milder symptoms in recurrent episodes compared to 
the initial episode [12, 37, 41], while others have experi-
enced exacerbations of symptoms or more severe presen-
tations in following episodes [9 , 30, 31, 40, 45, 46]. The 
time interval between recurrent episodes varies considerably 
in the literature. In addition to cases of recurrence after as 
short as 2 months, cases with long periods of up to 43 years 
between attacks have also been reported [34, 35, 37, 41–43, 
46]. Moreover, previous studies have shown that time from 
onset to nadir of the recurrent episodes slightly diminishes 
during the course, and time from the triggering infection to 
the onset of recurrence tends to shorten in successive epi-
sodes [31, 33, 34].
The presence of anti-ganglioside antibodies (i.e. GM1, 
GM2, GM3, GM4, GD1a, GD1b, GD2, GD3, GT1a, GT1b, 
GQ1b, GalNAc-GD1a), especially IgG type, is known to be 
pathogenic in GBS patients [ 35, 38, 42], while the role of 
anti-ganglioside IgM antibodies is still a subject of debate 
[43]. Moreover, the pattern of antibody positivity may differ 
from one episode to another in the same patient. The posi-
tivity of same [32, 36] or distinct [12, 40] anti-ganglioside 
antibodies in the recurrent episodes have been documented.
The literature also presents a limited number of cases 
with recurrent axonal GBS [30, 32, 33, 36]. In a retrospec-
tive study from Sweden, one of the fifteen recurrent GBS 
cases was the axonal variant [33]. In another case report, a 
61-year-old male patient with a history of AMAN experienc-
ing a recurrence following the second dose of the shingles 
vaccine has been reported [30]. Jo et al. reported a 75-year-
old female who underwent two episodes of AMAN after 
a urinary tract infection, 8 months apart. Anti-GM1 and 
anti-GD1a IgG antibodies were positive in serum in both 
episodes [32]. Another case was a 37-year-old male with 
three episodes of acute axonal motor neuropathy. First and 
second episodes were 54 days apart, with full recovery, both 
were following diarrhea. The third episode was 3 years later. 
Anti-GM1, anti-GM2, anti-GD1b, and anti-GalNAc-GD1a

antibodies were positive in the first and third episodes. The 
authors initially interpreted the case as TRF after the second 
episode and later diagnosed it as recurrent GBS [36].
Recurrent GBS may also manifest as a part of combined 
central and peripheral demyelination (CCPD). We had a 
young male patient with recurrent simultaneous central and 
peripheral nervous system demyelination, 12 years apart. 
The peripheral nervous system involvement was compatible 
with two episodes of recurrent GBS. Anti-neurofascin-155 
and anti-neurofascin-186 antibodies were negative. The 
prognosis was excellent with pulse methylprednisolone, 
intravenous immunoglobulin, and plasmapheresis [47].
Studies about A‑CIDP
A-CIDP represents a unique subset of CIDP with acute onset 
of symptoms followed by a chronic course lasting beyond 
8 weeks and associations with various antecedent events. 
Herein, we have examined the literature, encompassing stud-
ies and case reports, to present the properties and clinical 
characteristics of A-CIDP patients.
In CIDP, the frequency of antecedent events, includ-
ing infections and vaccinations, has been reported to range 
between 10 and 33% [48]. On the other hand, chronic inflam-
matory diseases such as systemic lupus erythematosus and 
sarcoidosis are predominantly associated with CIDP rather 
than GBS [49– 52]. Notably, CIDP cases associated with 
antecedent events are more likely to be classified as A-CIDP 
[48, 53], occur at a younger age [54], and exhibit cranial 
nerve involvement [48, 54, 55] than those without anteced-
ent events. Recently, A-CIDP cases following COVID-19 
infection or vaccination have been reported [ 11, 13, 56]. 
Some reported significant clinical improvement over a 
2-month follow-up period [11].
Anti-ganglioside antibody testing in chronic immune-
mediated neuropathies is not as common as in acute 
immune-mediated neuropathies, and IgM type anti-gan-
glioside antibodies are more common in chronic immune-
mediated neuropathies than acute ones [43, 57]. An atypical 
case of A-CIDP has been reported with three episodes in 
a 3-year period [58]. The first episode was 2 weeks after 
an upper respiratory infection, presenting with progressive 
paraparesis. Electroneuromyography (ENMG) revealed pro-
longed F-wave latencies and the absence of sensory nerve 
action potentials, suggesting a demyelinating pathology. 
Recovery was complete after the first episode. The second 
episode was 1 year later, following a diarrheal illness, pre-
senting with recurrence of paraparesis. ENMG was not per -
formed, and the patient was treated with supportive care. 
Recovery was incomplete, reaching approximately 80% of 
the patient’s baseline. The last episode occurred 3 years after 
the initial episode, with worsening quadriparesis. ENMG 
revealed pure axonal features which has been interpreted as axonal loss due to progressive untreated demyelination or 
primary axonal neuropathy by the authors. Anti-GM1, anti-
GD1a, and anti-GD1b antibodies have been positive which 
are known to be prominent in AMAN but have also been 
seen in CIDP variants. Eight months later, the recovery was 
partial [ 58]. Other intriguing cases include A-CIDP evolv -
ing from post-partum GBS [59] and VEXAS (vacuoles, E1 
enzyme, X-linked, autoinflammation, somatic) syndrome 
[60], Zika virus-associated A-CIDP [14], A-CIDP follow -
ing Hantavirus infection in a hepatitis B virus carrier [61], 
and after Influenza vaccination [26].
Over the past few decades, several antibodies (anti-con-
tactin-1, anti-neurofascin-155, anti-neurofascin-186, anti-
contactin-associated protein-1) against nodal/paranodal 
proteins have been identified in a subset of patients with 
peripheral neuropathies [62]. Initially classified as CIDP 
according to the European Federation of Neurological Sci-
ences / Peripheral Nerve Society (PNS) 2010 guideline [24], 
these cases have recently been redefined as a distinct cat -
egory known as autoimmune nodopathies. The European 
Academy of Neurology / PNS 2021 guideline [63] now rec -
ognizes them as separate entities, distinct from CIDP, due 
to their specific clinical features and responses to treatment. 
It is imperative to bear in mind that certain patients with 
clinical presentations resembling A-CIDP may indeed be 
afflicted by autoimmune nodopathies. This is particularly 
relevant for anti-neurofascin-186 antibody positive ones, as 
they can present symptoms indicative of acute or subacute 
onset acquired demyelinating polyneuropathies [64].
Studies about differentiating A‑CIDP from GBS
The differentiation between A-CIDP and GBS with or with-
out recurrences poses a significant clinical challenge due 
to their overlapping clinical features and distinct treatment 
strategies. Moreover, in some instances, CIDP may have 
a monophasic course and enter complete remission [ 65], 
making the differential diagnosis from GBS more challeng-
ing. Some clinical clues have been suggested to distinguish 
between GBS and A-CIDP [20, 25, 66]. Recently, several 
ultrasonographic and immunological markers have also been 
proposed [67– 70]. Nonetheless, how to differentiate A-CIDP 
from GBS electrophysiologically remains unclear [66].
Patients with A-CIDP are more likely to have an older age 
at disease onset [25], exhibit prominent sensory symptoms, 
impaired vibration sensation, sensory ataxia [66] and less 
likely to have cranial, and autonomic involvement, and loss 
of pinprick sensation compared to patients with GBS [20, 
25, 66]. Additionally, a history of type 2 diabetes mellitus 
is more frequent in patients with A-CIDP [25]. On the other 
hand, there are discrepancies in the literature regarding the 
need for mechanical ventilation in patients with A-CIDP 
and GBS. While one study has been found similar rates for

mechanical ventilation in both patient groups [25], other 
studies have been stated that the need for mechanical ven-
tilation is more common in patients with GBS [20, 66]. In 
addition, patients with GBS can be differentiated from CIDP 
by long asymptomatic periods, rapid onset of symptoms, 
normal cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) proteins in the early phase, 
and more frequent antecedent infections [29, 66]. However, 
in a study comparing patients with A-CIDP and fluctuat -
ing GBS, the frequency of antecedent infections has been 
similar in both groups of patients [20]. Another distinc-
tion between these diseases is the time from neurological 
symptoms to maximum disability, which is often less than 
4 weeks in patients with GBS, while by definition, it is more 
than 8 weeks in patients with CIDP [24].
Distinguishing between A-CIDP and AIDP through 
electrophysiological means remains challenging. Currently, 
no specific electrophysiological markers, including sural-
sparing pattern, sensory ratio, or presence of A waves have 
been identified to definitely distinguish between these two 
neuropathies [66]. There are conflicting results regarding 
sural-sparing pattern and the presence of A waves. While 
one study indicates that these parameters are more com-
monly seen in AIDP [68], another study suggests that these 
parameters do not differ significantly in A-CIDP and AIDP 
[66]. Nevertheless, in a prospective study, they speculated 
that nerve excitability studies may assist in differentiating 
A-CIDP from AIDP in the early stages. They found abnor -
malities in the recovery cycle of excitability, including sig-
nificantly reduced superexcitability and prolonged relative 
refractory period without changes in threshold electroto-
nus in AIDP. On the other hand, different pattern changes 
observed in the recovery cycle, suggesting early repolariza-
tion in A-CIDP [71]. Therefore, it is difficult to differenti-
ate A-CIDP from AIDP electrophysiologically in the early 
stages, and the usefulness of electromyographic follow-up 
in differentiation of those pathologies is also debatable. It 
has been stated that as the disease progresses, the sural nerve 
might become affected in CIDP and sural-sparing pattern 
might be used for differential diagnosis [66]. On the other 
hand, in a recent follow-up study of patients with AIDP, 
it was reported that approximately 39% of patients contin-
ued to meet the definite CIDP criteria 1 year after the acute 
AIDP episode, and approximately 10% of the patients did 
so more than 18 months after. Conduction blocks were pre-
sent in three patients on nerve conduction studies at 1 year, 
suggesting active demyelination [72]. So, nerve conduction 
study results should be interpreted cautiously with symp -
toms and clinical examination findings during follow-up, 
before reaching a diagnosis.
Ultrasonography is another modality that has been stud-
ied for A-CIDP and GBS discrimination. The Bochum 
ultrasound score (BUS), based on cross-sectional areas of 
ulnar, radial, and sural nerves, has emerged as a potentially valuable tool. Given the higher Bochum scores indicating 
increased thickness of the nerve; presence of sensory symp-
toms and a sum BUS of ≥ 2 points were more frequent in 
A-CIDP and had the highest sensitivity and specificity in 
distinguishing A-CIDP from GBS [67, 73]. Similarly, in a 
recent study they demonstrated that patients with CIDP had 
significantly enlarged nerves, except for the roots and vagus, 
which were enlarged in both CIDP and GBS. Moreover, pure 
sensory nerves were mostly enlarged in CIDP, while remain-
ing normal in GBS. This ultrasonic sensory-sparing pattern 
combined with enlarged roots / vagus has been defined as a 
hallmark of GBS [68].
Immunological differences between A-CIDP and GBS 
have also been explored. Studies have indicated that Fas-
mediated T-cell apoptosis is impaired in A-CIDP but not in 
GBS. This suggests that evaluating Fas function may aid in 
the early identification of GBS and A-CIDP [70, 74]. Addi-
tionally, CSF markers have also been investigated. In a ret-
rospective study, they have found higher CSF interleukin-8 
(IL-8) levels in patients with GBS compared to those with 
CIDP, and proposed an optimal cut-off value of 70 pg/mL 
[69].

Discussion
The differential diagnosis between A-CIDP and GBS, or 
whether recurrent GBS is a real entity or not, remains the 
subject of ongoing debate and poses several challenges. 
While there is some consensus on distinguishing these 
two entities, it is crucial to critically analyze existing 
studies to question the existence of recurrent GBS. The 
traditional definition of recurrent GBS includes episodes 
with a time to nadir of no more than 4 weeks [33]. How -
ever, it is worth noting that some authors include relaps-
ing–remitting cases into the CIDP category, such as those 
with intermittent episodes associated with respiratory 
failure [75] or recurrence after 18 months of full recovery 
[66]. CIDP progresses gradually over more than 8 weeks. 
However, patients with A-CIDP present with more rap -
idly progressive symptoms. Some authors have described 
A-CIDP with a time to nadir of less than 4 weeks [20]. 
GBS is often caused by an immune response to a preced-
ing infection, or other event that cross-reacts with periph-
eral nerve components [ 3, 8]. Although the exact cause 
of CIDP is unknown, CIDP is also an immunologically 
mediated neuropathy with multiple possible triggers. Both 
cellular and humoral immunity are involved in the patho-
genesis of CIDP [48– 51]. Although the pathophysiology 
of CIDP and GBS is quite different, there is not enough 
data whether recurrent GBS is a unique entity or another

variant of CIDP. Some authors speculates that instead of 
being distinct entities, recurrent GBS and CIDP may rep-
resent a spectrum of inflammatory demyelinating polyra-
diculopathies [ 65, 76]. Drawing an analogy with the dif -
ferentiation between clinical isolated syndrome (CIS) and 
multiple sclerosis (MS) can further support the idea that 
recurrent GBS may actually be part of the CIDP spectrum. 
While CIS refers to a single clinical demyelination episode 
resembling GBS, CIDP shares similarities with the chronic 
and relapsing nature of MS. On the other hand, differenti-
ating A-CIDP from GBS in the early stages is challenging 
but crucial as they require different treatment strategies. 
Unfortunately, despite some clinical clues [20, 25, 66], 
ultrasonographic and immunological markers [67– 70], 
and electrophysiological studies [66, 68] differentiation 
of A-CIDP from GBS remains elusive.In a retrospective study, it was showed that nerve conduc-
tion studies continued to worsen up to 18 months after the 
onset of symptoms in AIDP, and CIDP-like abnormalities 
suggestive of demyelination were identified [72]. These find-
ings suggest that the underlying autoimmune process may 
persist in a subset of AIDP patients, even in the absence 
of clinical deterioration. The findings may also potentially 
explain residual symptoms such as paresthesia, severe pain, 
or fatigue [77] that are observed in some individuals after 
a single acute GBS attack. Based on this study, it is impor -
tant to reconsider treatment strategies for patients with 
recurrent GBS and A-CIDP. Patients classified as having 
recurrent GBS after a second attack do not receive long-
term immunotherapy, while those diagnosed with A-CIDP

GBS due to the underlying, subtly ongoing immune pro -

recurrent episodes, evaluate the initiation of immunotherapy, 
and even reevaluate the diagnosis. By doing so, we can pro-
vide appropriate treatment strategies and potentially improve

Reconsidering certain aspects, including cases that are 
positive for antiganglioside antibodies, cases with a relaps-
ing axonal presentation (AMAN, AMSAN), and cases with 
antecedent events, is essential when suggesting that patients 
with recurrent GBS might be acute-onset axonal or demy -
elinating CIDP cases. Although less common than in GBS, 
anti-ganglioside antibody positivity and antecedent events 
such as vaccinations and infections can also be observed 
in patients with CIDP [35, 38, 42, 48, 57]. In recent years, 
the term of “chronic inflammatory axonal polyneuropa-
thy (CIAP)” has been proposed instead of axonal CIDP. It 
has been stated that CIAP, by definition, progresses over a 
period of > 8 weeks, and the distinction between CIAP and 
axonal CIDP lies in the assumption that in axonal CIDP, 
axonal changes are secondary to CIDP. Moreover, among 
the patients presented, there are two cases that initially pre-
sented as axonal GBS but were later diagnosed with CIAP 
/ axonal CIDP [78], suggesting the presence of acute-onset 
axonal CIDP cases.
Conclusion
The differentiation between A-CIDP and GBS, especially 
recurrent cases, presents a significant challenge within the 
field of neurology. Existing evidence raises doubts regarding 
the distinct classification of recurrent GBS as a separate clin-
ical entity. We argue that the arbitrary distinction between 
recurrent GBS and CIDP, the overlapping clinical presenta-
tions, and the presence of ongoing autoimmune processes 
all underscore the need for a reevaluation of diagnostic cri-
teria for CIDP and recurrent GBS. Moreover, the presence 
of axonal cases further complicates the clinical landscape. 
Recurrent GBS may represent another variant of CIDP 
that has not been fully described yet. Therefore, additional 
research and clinical investigations are warranted to enhance 
our understanding of these conditions, ultimately leading to 
the development of more effective treatment strategies and 
advanced patient care.
