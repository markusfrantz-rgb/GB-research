Altered mental status in "Guillain-Barre syndrome" - a noteworthy clinical clue

Authors: Eoin Mulroy, Neil E Anderson
Journal: Annals of Clinical and Translational Neurology
Year: 2020
PMID: 33136342
PMCID: PMC7732251
DOI: 10.1002/acn3.51226

OVERVIEW

This landmark review addresses central nervous system involvement in GBS, noting that "despite being common in clinical practice, there is no guidance in the literature as to how to approach such scenarios." Almost one third of critically ill GBS patients have mental status abnormalities.

INTRINSIC GBS-RELATED MENTAL STATUS CHANGES

Mental status abnormalities can include hallucinations, delusions, and vivid dreams, representing intrinsic disease features appearing primarily during week 1-2.

DISEASE COMPLICATIONS CAUSING CNS DYSFUNCTION

1. SIADH/Hyponatremia: Affects roughly 50% of GBS patients, causing altered mental status and seizures.
2. CO2 Narcosis: From respiratory muscle weakness requiring ventilation.
3. PRES (Posterior Reversible Encephalopathy Syndrome): Classically manifesting with headache, nausea, vomiting, visual changes, seizures, encephalopathy with characteristic posterior brain edema. Blood pressure variability can trigger PRES, particularly in women over 55 years.

TREATMENT-RELATED COMPLICATIONS

IVIG-Associated Adverse Effects:
- Thrombotic events: Variable incidence (1-16%), occurring within 24 hours of infusion
- Aseptic meningitis: Affects 0.5-1% of IVIG-treated patients; presents with fever, headache, meningism
- PRES: Can occur secondary to treatment

Medication Effects: Opioids, gabapentin, pregabalin, and sedative medications commonly cause mental status alterations in ventilated patients.

GBS VARIANTS WITH CNS INVOLVEMENT

Anti-GQ1b Antibody Disease Spectrum:
- Bickerstaff brainstem encephalitis: external ophthalmoplegia AND either altered level of consciousness or hyper-reflexia
- 25-30% display MRI abnormalities: T2-weighted hyperintensities in brainstem, thalamus, cerebellum, or cerebral white matter
- Up to 50% have coexisting GBS
- Serum anti-GQ1b antibody positivity confirms diagnosis

GBS MIMICS WITH CNS INVOLVEMENT - "MIND" FRAMEWORK

M = Metabolic + Malignancy
I = Infectious/Immune/Inherited
N = Nutritional
D = Drugs/Toxins

METABOLIC/MALIGNANCY DISORDERS

Porphyrias: Present with abdominal pain followed by neuropsychiatric symptoms, confusion, delirium and seizures. Up to 40% develop motor-predominant axonal neuropathy. Porphyric neuropathy characteristically begins in arms (unlike GBS). Diagnostic clue: Elevated urinary aminolevulinic acid and porphobilinogen.

Leptomeningeal metastases: Affect 5-8% of solid tumors, 15% of hematologic malignancies. Present with rapidly progressive weakness, radicular pain, sphincter involvement. MRI shows meningeal contrast uptake; CSF contains malignant cells. Clues: asymmetric weakness, abnormal CSF.

Paraneoplastic syndromes: Anti-Hu antibody syndrome (sensory neuronopathy with brainstem/limbic encephalitis), anti-CRMP5 antibodies.

INFECTIOUS DISORDERS

Enteroviruses: Anterior horn cell involvement causing paralysis.
Flaviviruses: Asymmetric paralysis, brainstem/diencephalic involvement with "double doughnut" sign.
Rabies (paralytic variant): 20-30% present as paralytic form, clinically indistinguishable from GBS.
Herpesviruses: CMV, VZV can cause GBS-like illness.
Neuroborreliosis: Painful spinal polyradiculitis.

IMMUNE DISORDERS

Vasculitides: SLE and EGPA with symmetric sensorimotor polyneuropathy mimicking GBS.
Autoimmune encephalitides: NMDA/AMPA receptor antibodies, CASPR2 antibodies.
Inherited: X-linked CMT 1X with transient ADEM-like white matter abnormalities.

NUTRITIONAL DISORDERS

Thiamine deficiency: Classic Wernicke's triad of confusion, ataxia, ophthalmoplegia resembles anti-GQ1b disorders. MRI: Symmetrical thalami, mammillary bodies, periaqueductal hyperintensities.

TOXINS AND DRUGS

Arsenic, thallium, glycol ingestion, ciguatera poisoning, TNF-alpha antagonists.

CLINICAL DECISION-MAKING ALGORITHM

1. Are mental status changes intrinsic to GBS? (MRI brain normal, CSF albuminocytologic dissociation, symptoms peak week 1-2)
2. Could complications of GBS be responsible? (hyponatremia, hypercarbia, infections, PRES, autonomic dysfunction)
3. Could GBS treatment be responsible? (IVIG-related: thrombotic events, aseptic meningitis, PRES; medication effects)
4. Could this be a GBS variant? (Test anti-GQ1b antibodies, assess for Bickerstaff features)
5. Is this actually not GBS? (Pursue MIND differential systematically)

MRI FINDINGS BY DIAGNOSIS

- PRES: T2/FLAIR hyperintensities in posterior brain regions
- BBE: T2 hyperintensities in brainstem, thalamus, cerebellum, cerebral white matter
- Wernicke encephalopathy: Symmetric thalamic/mammillary/periaqueductal enhancement
- Flavivirus encephalitis: "Double doughnut" sign (thalamic involvement)
- CMT 1X: ADEM-like white matter abnormalities
- Autoimmune encephalitis: Medial temporal lobe hyperintensities

CSF ANALYSIS

- Albuminocytologic dissociation: Suggestive of GBS
- Pleocytosis: Supports infectious/immune etiology
- Aseptic meningitis (IVIG-related): Neutrophilic pleocytosis, elevated protein, high opening pressure

CONCLUSION

GBS itself can occasionally produce mental status alterations, but this should remain a diagnosis of exclusion. Significant depression in level of consciousness, seizures, movement disorders should trigger exhaustive searches for alternative explanations.
