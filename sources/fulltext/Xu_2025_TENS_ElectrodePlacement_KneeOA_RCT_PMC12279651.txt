TITLE: Electrode Placement Affects Clinical Outcome in Transcutaneous Electrical Nerve Stimulation for Knee Osteoarthritis: A Combined Cadaver Study and Randomized Controlled Trial
ABSTRACT: Transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS) is a widely used physical therapy for knee osteoarthritis (OA) pain management. However, the optimal electrode placement for TENS in knee OA remains unclear. Given that TENS delivers stimulation via electrodes to cutaneous nerves, placing electrodes in areas with high nerve density should be the precondition to exert its therapeutic effects. However, high-density nerve areas around the knee and corresponding electrode placement strategies have yet to be investigated. An anatomic study was conducted on 20 adult formalin-fixed cadavers to identify the high-density nerve areas around the knee. Then, to standardize electrode placement, the patellar width was used as a reference to determine distances from the patellar borders. Subsequently, 80 patients with Kellgren–Lawrence grade 2 or 3 knee OA were enrolled in a randomized, double-blinded trial. Participants received TENS therapy with electrodes placed either on the identified high-density nerve areas (study cohort, n = 40) or on traditional pain areas (control cohort, n = 40). Four high-density nerve areas were identified: the quadriceps tendon, patellar ligament, medial joint line area, and superior medial area of the knee. Over the follow-up period, patients in the study cohort showed significantly greater improvements in VAS score and WOMAC index total score, as well as in pain, stiffness, and function subscales compared with those in the control cohort (all P < 0.05). These findings suggest that positioning electrodes over high-density nerve areas allows TENS more effectively activate sensory afferents, thereby enhancing pain relief. ## Introduction
Pain is the most common symptom of knee OA [1]. Relieving knee pain is a cornerstone of OA management, typically achieved through nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and complemented by physical therapy [2]. Transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS), a noninvasive and nonpharmacological treatment, is widely used for knee OA pain management and is recommended by several guidelines, including those from National Institute of Health and Care Excellence and American College of Rheumatology Subcommittee [3, 4].
Despite the clinical popularity of TENS, the optimal electrode placement for knee OA remains largely undetermined. Electrode placement varies greatly from one study to another, with some recommending placement at pain site [5], while others suggest positioning electrodes along dermatome or at corresponding acupoint [6]. In clinical practice, physiotherapists often rely on a trial-and-error approach to determine electrode placement, which is not only time-consuming but may also compromise therapeutic efficacy [7]. Walsh et al. identified ineffective electrode placement as a key factor for poor patient response [8]. Indeed, many researchers acknowledge that electrode placement is the least investigated parameter of TENS [9].
TENS works by delivering electrical stimulation via electrodes to activate cutaneous nerves, thereby inducing a hypoalgesic effect [10]. Studies have shown that areas with a higher density of cutaneous nerves have lower impedance, making it easier to activate sensory afferents and enhance pain relief [11, 12]. Hence, placing electrodes in areas with a high density of nerves should be the precondition to exert TENS’ biological effects. However, the high-density nerve areas around the knee, and the strategies for electrode placement in these areas, have yet to be investigated.
On the basis of the mechanism of TENS, we hypothesize that different electrode placements result in varying clinical outcomes and that placing electrodes on high-density nerve areas may enhance efficacy. To test this, we first conducted a cadaver study to identify high-density nerve areas around the knee. A randomized and double-blinded trial was then carried out to evaluate whether different electrode placements (on high-density nerve areas versus pain areas) influence the clinical effectiveness of TENS for Knee OA.
Accordingly, the primary objective of this study was to determine whether electrode placement over high-density nerve areas enhances the analgesic efficacy of TENS in patients with knee OA.

## The Cadaver Study

## Identifying High-Density Nerve Areas Around the Knee
At first, a systematic anatomical dissection was conducted on 20 specimens to examine the course, distribution, and density of the cutaneous nerves around the knee (Supplementary Fig. 1). Subsequently, following the method described by T. Peker et al. [13], the high-density nerve area was identified based on the number of cutaneous nerve branches. Electrodes were placed on the high-density nerve area, and their horizontal and vertical distances from the key anatomic landmarks (patella, medial joint line, and patellar ligament) were measured (Fig. 2). ## Preclinical Verification of Electrode Placement
On the basis of the findings from the section “Identifying High-Density Nerve Areas Around The Knee”, strategies for electrode placement in high-density nerve areas were developed. First, patellar width was measured. Then, electrode was positioned at specific vertical and horizontal distances proportional to patellar width, a method termed “patella-informed electrode placement.”
To verify the accuracy of this approach, preclinical verification was performed on two specimens. First, the electrodes were placed according to the patella-informed method. Then, pins were inserted around the electrodes to mark their locations (Fig. 3A, B). ## The Clinical Study

## Trial Design and Patients
The clinical study was designed as a prospective, double-blind (patient and assessor), randomized, and controlled trial (Fig. 4). Patients with knee OA were eligible if they met the American Rheumatism Association criteria [14] and the following standards: (1) aged between 50 and 75 years; (2) radiographic evidence of Kellgren-Lawrence grade 2 or 3 knee OA; (3) a score of at least 20% on one or more pain dimensions of Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis (WOMAC) index; and (4) treated at a single institution.Fig. 4CONSORT flow diagram. The whole flow diagram included enrollment, allocation, follow-up, and analysis. CONSORT: Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials. The exclusion criteria were as follows: patients had a history of epilepsy, knee joint trauma or diabetic neuropathy; diagnosed with lower extremity neuropathy; had electrical implants (e.g., defibrillators, deep brain stimulator, or pacemakers); took oral NSAIDs in the past 2 weeks; received TENS treatment on the target knee in the past 4 weeks; had infectious or inflammatory skin diseases in the target knee area; were pregnant or planning to become pregnant during the trial period, and had active liver, kidney, cardiovascular, or cerebrovascular diseases.

## Randomization
The sample size was calculated with NCSS-PASS (V23.0.2), assuming a standard deviation [SD] of 1.78 (on a 0–10 visual analog scale (VAS)) on the basis of previous studies [15, 16]. To achieve 80% power at a 0.05 significance level, while accounting for a 10% predicted dropout rate, 40 patients per group were required. Randomization was conducted on an individual basis with consecutively numbered sealed envelopes (1–80), which were opened after patient recruitment. After obtaining informed consent, patients were assigned as follows: odd-numbered patients were allocated to the patella-informed electrode placement (electrode on high-density nerve area, study cohort); even-numbered patients were assigned to the traditional electrode placement group (electrode in pain area, control cohort). In total, 80 patients were enrolled in the study, with 40 in the study cohort and 40 in the control cohort.

## TENS Interventions
According to the patient allocation, electrodes were placed on the high-density nerve areas for patients in the study cohort. In contrast, for the control cohort, electrodes were positioned directly in the pain areas (Fig. 5A, B). The physiotherapist set the TENS stimulation parameters and intensity using XY-K-SJD-A TENS device (Sunnyou Co., Ltd., China). TENS modality was individualized using standard recommended stimulation parameters (low-frequency, high-frequency, or burst TENS) and biphasic pulse waveform, according to symptom presentation. The frequency was initially set from 60 to 100 Hz and the pulse duration was gradually increased from 100 µsec to 250 µsec. TENS treatment sections were conducted five times per week, with each session lasting up to 30 min.Fig. 5Electrode placements in the clinical trial. ## Outcome Measurement
Clinical outcomes were evaluated by an assessor who was blind to the patient allocation. WOMAC and VAS indexes, the most widely used assessment for knee OA pain, were used as the assessment tools. The WOMAC index and VAS index questionnaires were collected at baseline and at one week and two weeks post the first TENS intervention. The WOMAC index includes 5 pain-related questions, 2 stiffness-related questions, and 17 questions on difficulty in daily activities. Each subscale is graded on a 5-point scale (0 = none, 1 = mild, 2 = moderate, 3 = severe, 4 = extreme).
Adverse events were defined as any new or worsening signs, symptoms, or undesirable occurrences during the study. Referring to the report by Reichenbach et al.[17], adverse events include local skin reactions. Serious adverse events were classified as those resulting in hospitalization, prolonged hospitalization, significant or persistent disability, life-threatening events, or death.

## Statistical Analysis
Statistical analysis was performed with IBM SPSS Statistics 20.0 (Armonk, New York, USA) and GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software Inc.). Categorical variables were presented as numbers and percentages and continuous variables as median values. Friedman test was used for the comparison of repeated non-parametric measurements, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for the paired comparison. Differences at a P level of < 0.05 were identified as statistically significant.

## Results

## High-Density Nerve Areas Around the Knee
There are four high-density nerve areas around the knee: the quadriceps tendon, patellar ligament, medial joint line area, and superior medial region (Fig. 2A, B). The electrode placement in these high-density nerve areas was as follows:

## Quadriceps Tendon Area
Vertical distance (A): from the base of the patella to the inferior margin of the electrode, representing 26.31% of patellar width (~ 1/4) (Fig. 2C, D).
Horizontal distance (B): from the medial margin of the patella to the medial margin of the electrode, representing 17.96% of patellar width (~ 1/6) (Fig. 2E).

## Patellar Ligament Area
Vertical distance (C): from the inferior pole of the patella to the superior border of the electrode, representing 24.73% of patellar width (~ 1/4) (Fig. 2F, G).
Horizontal distance (D): from the medial border of the patellar ligament to the medial edge of the electrode, representing 16.06% of patellar width (~ 1/6) (Fig. 2H).

## Superior Medial Region
Vertical distance (E): from the medial joint line to the inferior margin of the electrode, representing 36.19% of patellar width (~ 1/3) (Fig. 2I–J).
Horizontal distance (F): from the medial margin of the patella to the lateral border of the electrode, representing 21.76% of patellar width (~ 1/5) (Fig. 2K).

## Medial Joint Line Area
Vertical distance (G): from the medial joint line to the superior border of the electrode, representing 18.91% of patellar width (~ 1/5) (Fig. 2L, M).
Horizontal distance (H): from the medial margin of the patella to the lateral border of the electrode, representing 21.25% of patellar width (~ 1/5) (Fig. 2N).
On the basis of these findings, the “patella-informed electrode placement” was developed (Detailed information on each electrode placement is provided in Supplementary Table 1.). In clinical, the patellar width is measured at first, and then the vertical and horizontal electrode distances are calculated accordingly. The electrodes are then positioned at the targeted nerve areas (Supplementary Fig. 2).

## Preclinical Verification
According to the patella-informed electrode placement methodology, electrodes were positioned at the two lower extremities. Dissection confirmed that the electrodes were accurately positioned on the high-density nerve areas (Fig. 3C), with detailed placements shown in Fig. 3C1–C4. The results demonstrated that the patella-informed electrode placement methodology is reliable and practical.

## Safety Outcomes
No serious adverse event was reported in the study. However, three patients discontinued their therapy due to adverse events—one (2.5%) from the study cohort and two (5%) from the control cohort. All adverse events were minor and transient local skin reactions related to TENS treatment.

## Discussion
Alternative treatments for knee OA, including non-surgical treatment or minimally invasive surgery prior to total knee arthroplasty, have been extensively studied, but no definitive consensus has been reached so far [1]. The effectiveness of TENS in relieving knee OA pain remains controversial [18]. Some studies suggested that it may be placebo effect. In a randomized, controlled and double-blinded trial, Reichenbach et al. [17] found that TENS was not superior to placebo TENS. However, numerous other researches supported its beneficial effect for knee OA pain management [4, 19]. Recently, a systematic review of 29 randomized controlled trials involving 1398 patients confirmed the effectiveness of TENS in terms of pain [18]. The reported discrepancy may result from the diverse nature of the pathologies for which TENS is prescribed, the wide range of stimulation variables, and inconsistencies in electrode placement.
In clinical practice, TENS electrode placement is often determined by the physiotherapist’s subjective judgment rather than established guidelines [20]. In fact, there is no consensus in literature regarding optimal TENS electrode placement. To identify optimal stimulation sites, Berlant et al. used a TENS unit and manual probing to locate superficial cutaneous nerves points in painful areas. However, this technique caused discomfort for both the patient and the therapist [21]. Previous studies have also explored how TENS electrode placement impacts on clinical efficacy [22, 23]. They emphasized that the key to choose TENS electrode sites should be based on anatomical and physiological principles [24].

## Inspiration and Theoretical Basis for Electrode Placement on High-Density Nerve Area
Mechanistically, TENS works through either activating the gate control or stimulating superficial nerve fibers to release endorphins [21, 25]. Ishimaru et al. showed that using TENS with skin electrodes can significantly raise the pain threshold of the skin and fascia—especially around the knee, where many cutaneous nerves are located [26]. These nerves run between the skin and fascia and branch out to supply nerve endings [27]. This suggests that the clinical effects seen in areas with low skin resistance may be due, at least partly, to better activation of superficial cutaneous nerves along these paths.
Inspired by these pioneering studies, we hypothesize that placing electrodes over high-density nerve areas would achieve more optimal pain relief. Actually, Prokhorov et al. provided preliminary results showing that electrodes placed over the median nerve at the wrist were positioned on skin with lower impedance than other areas [28]. Since skin impedance appears to influence the analgesic effect of electrical stimulation, this factor could be crucial in optimizing TENS therapy. Furthermore, Kolen et al. suggested that sensory fibers in these areas were more responsive to stimulation [20]. Similarly, Vance et al. identified a high density of nerve branches in areas with lower skin impedance [11].
Despite these findings, few studies have specifically explored the high-density nerve areas around the knee, and even fewer have integrated this knowledge into TENS electrode placement in clinical practice. This study evaluated the high-density nerve areas and developed strategies for electrode placement in these areas. Furthermore, a randomized and double-blinded trial was then carried out to evaluate the different TENS electrode placement in treating knee OA.
The definition of high-density nerve areas remains unclear, particularly regarding their range and nerve branch density. Peker et al. investigated nerve distribution patterns by counting extramuscular and intramuscular nerve branches [13]. In the study, we quantified high-density nerve area by counting cutaneous nerve branches, with two independent researchers to minimize any potential bias.
Furthermore, we observed significant individual variations in measurement data. To mitigate this heterogeneity, we categorized patellar width into grades to standardize electrode placement. This method enables personalized treatment while maintaining a simple, quick and accurate TENS application for knee OA. Additionally, both patients and physiotherapists can be trained in patella-informed TENS electrode placement, making it accessible for both clinical and at-home use. Of course, this study was conducted in a hospital setting, where therapists evaluated the effectiveness of patella-informed TENS electrode placement for knee OA. Further research is needed to assess its efficacy in home settings.

## Effectiveness of TENS Electrode on High-Density Nerve Area in Knee OA
Our protocol for patella-informed TENS electrode placement in knee OA was found safe and effective. Only one patient in the study cohort suffered from local skin reaction, while no other participants reported adverse events. We observed that placing electrodes on high-density nerve areas provided superior pain relief compared with electrodes on pain areas. Similarly, Brown et al. and Dean et al. also suggested that TENS is most effective when electrodes are placed on the main nerve bundles originating from the painful areas [5, 29]. These findings indicate that proper TENS electrode placement is related to the cutaneous nerve target areas. A clinical study by Kolen et al. found that stimulating the most superficial cutaneous nerve innervating the pain area significantly reduced knee OA pain, more so than stimulation at other sites [20].
The medial joint line and anterior knee are commonly identified as the most painful regions in knee OA [30]. Nian et al. highlighted that pain in these areas plays a crucial role in functional decline among patients with knee OA [31]. Sluka et al. [6] and Johnson et al. [32] suggested that TENS applied to pain areas can activate the receptive fields of nerve fibers, thereby improving knee pain. Based on the results of the cadaver study, the high-density nerve areas around the knee coincide with the anterior and medial pain areas for knee OA. This provides an anatomical explanation for why electrodes in the pain area can still relieve knee OA pain, but not as effectively as electrodes in high-density nerve areas. Positioning electrodes on high-density nerve areas allows TENS more effectively activate sensory afferents, thereby enhancing pain relief.

## Limitations
Placing TENS electrodes on high-density nerve areas can enhance treatment efficacy for knee OA and is especially recommended for mild to moderate knee OA. However, certain limitations should be considered. First, while this study focused on the impact of electrode placement, other stimulation parameters—such as frequency, pulse width, and intensity—may also influence the therapeutic effectiveness of TENS. These variables were not systematically explored in the current study and warrant further investigation to better understand their interaction with electrode positioning. Second, while our findings suggest that placing electrodes over high-density nerve areas offers better pain relief, the exact reasons remain unclear. For example, it is not yet known whether lower skin resistance in these areas plays a role. More studies using high-resolution imaging, electrophysiological mapping, and real-time impedance measurements are needed to clarify the connection between nerve distribution and local skin impedance. This could be a valuable direction for future research in this area.

## Conclusions
This study provides new insights into the relationship between TENS electrode placement and the clinical effectiveness of TENS therapy for knee OA. On the basis of how TENS works—delivering stimulation to cutaneous nerves through electrodes—we hypothesized that placing electrodes over high-density nerve areas could improve treatment results. First, using a cadaver study, we identified the high-density nerve areas around the knee and developed strategies for electrode placement in these regions. Then, in a randomized, double-blinded clinical trial, we confirmed that placing electrodes on these areas resulted in better pain relief and improved joint function compared with placing them over pain areas. On the basis of these findings, we recommend positioning electrodes on high-density nerve areas to enhance TENS therapy for knee OA.
