Abstract
Background & purpose In this retrospective study, we aimed at defining the clinical, paraclinical and outcome features of
acute neurological syndromes associated with anti-GQ1b antibodies.
Results We identified 166 patients with neurological symptoms appearing in less than 1 month and anti-GQ1b antibodies
in serum between 2012 and 2022. Half were female (51%), mean age was 50 years (4–90), and the most frequent clinical
features were areflexia (80% of patients), distal upper and lower limbs sensory symptoms (78%), ophthalmoplegia (68%),
sensory ataxia (67%), limb muscle weakness (45%) and bulbar weakness (45%). Fifty-three patients (32%) presented with
complete (21%) and incomplete (11%) Miller Fisher syndrome (MFS), thirty-six (22%) with Guillain–Barre syndrome (GBS),
one (0.6%) with Bickerstaff encephalitis (BE), and seventy-three (44%) with mixed MFS, GBS & BE clinical features. Nerve
conduction studies were normal in 46% of cases, showed demyelination in 28%, and axonal loss in 23%. Anti-GT1a antibodies were found in 56% of cases, increased cerebrospinal fluid protein content in 24%, and Campylobacter jejuni infection
in 7%. Most patients (83%) were treated with intravenous immunoglobulins, and neurological recovery was complete in
69% of cases at 1 year follow-up. One patient died, and 15% of patients relapsed. Age > 70 years, initial Intensive Care Unit
(ICU) admission, and absent anti-GQ1b IgG antibodies were predictors of incomplete recovery at 12 months. No predictors
of relapse were identified.
Conclusion This study from Western Europe shows acute anti-GQ1b antibody syndrome presents with a large clinical phenotype, a good outcome in 2/3 of cases, and frequent relapses.

Introduction
Gangliosides, including GQ1b, are glycosphingolipids
with a structure in which a hydrophobic ceramide and a
hydrophilic sugar chain portion are bound, and sialic acid
is bound to the sugar chain portion [1, 2]. Gangliosides are
abundant in the nervous system, and their distribution is
heterogeneous between different nerve tissues [2]. GQ1b
represents approximately 5–6% of the total gangliosides
in human peripheral nervous system (PNS), and 11–13%

in oculomotor nerves [2]. In oculomotor nerves, GQ1b are
mostly localized in the paranodal region of the node of Ranvier [2]. In addition to oculomotor nerves, the GQ1b sugar
chain antigen is also highly expressed in human dorsal root
ganglion cells and Ia sensory fibers in muscle spindles, often
in association with GT1a sugar chains [2]. GQ1b includes
two chemical groups composed of two sialic acids, i.e., a
disialosyl group, that are bound to galactose, and anti-GQ1b
antibodies recognize the sugar chain portion to which sialic
acid is bound [2].
Miller Fisher syndrome (MFS) is a clinical syndrome
associating ophthalmoplegia, ataxia and areflexia [3].

Journal of Neurology (2024) 271:4982–4990

Bickerstaff’s encephalitis (BE) is a clinical syndrome associating ophthalmoplegia, ataxia and areflexia, i.e., MFS,
and a reduced level of consciousness, i.e. drowsiness [4].
BE and MFS have a good outcome and usually occur after
an infection, with Campylobacter jejuni and Haemophilus
influenzae being the most frequently identified pathogens
[5–7]. As such, both BE and MFS are considered as Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS) variants [5].
Before anti-GQ1b antibodies were found in both conditions in 1992, MFS and BE were considered separate
clinical entities. Today, as MFS and BE share a common
serological profile and pathophysiology, both conditions
are referred as being part of the “anti-GQ1b antibody
syndrome” [6, 7]. Large series of MFS and BE patients
with anti-GQ1b antibodies have been described, mainly in
Asian countries where both conditions are common [8, 9].
Intriguingly, in these series, 10–30% of patient are GQ1bseronegative using conventional assays [2, 10]. However,
calcium-dependent anti-GQ1b antibodies have been
recently found in a majority of these “GQ1b seronegative”
patients, thus demonstrating anti-GQ1b antibodies are a
major component of MFS and BE pathophysiology [2, 10]
In this retrospective study, we analyzed the clinical,
electrophysiological, laboratory and treatment response
characteristics of patients with acute anti-GQ1b antibody
syndrome originating from 19 French and Swiss University Hospitals. Our aim was to define the clinical and
paraclinical features of acute anti-GQ1b-associated neurological syndromes, and to provide clues as to how better
manage and treat these patients.

Patients & methods
Patients
We performed an observational retrospective study including patients with: (1) neurological symptoms appearing in
less than 1 month; (2) anti-GQ1b antibodies in serum; and
(3) diagnosed between 2012 and 2022. Neurologists from
19 University Hospitals in France and Switzerland were
contacted, and clinical, paraclinical & treatment data were
gathered from medical records by the referring physician.
Treatment response was evaluated by the referring neurologist according to each center practice. This study is in
conformity with the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki 1964 and its later amendments. Given that
our study was conducted retrospectively on documented
patient records with no specific intervention of any kind,
local ethical approval and specific informed consent were
not required.

Neurological assessment & diagnostic classification
Data gathering was performed between February and September 2022. In addition to neurological symptoms and
signs, data on treatment and outcome were also gathered.
Patients were classified according to Shahrizaila's clinical
classification [11, 12]: (1) GBS spectrum, including classic
GBS and other GBS variant, i.e., paraparetic GBS, pharyngeal cervical brachial weakness (PCBW), facial diplegia and
paresthesia; (2) MFS spectrum, including classic MFS and
other MFS subgroups, i.e., acute ophthalmoplegia or acute
ataxic neuropathy; and (3) BE spectrum, including classic
BE & acute ataxic hypersomnolence. If patients had overlapping features between these groups, they were designated as
MFS/GBS or GBS/BE.

Biological assessment
Anti-GQ1b antibodies were investigated using different
methods in each University Hospital, including commercially available immunodots (GA Generic Assays GmbH,
Germany) and ELISA (Enzyme Linked Immunosorbent
Assay) (Gangliocombi dosing kit, Bühlman, Switzerland)
[13, 14]. Calcium-dependent anti-GQ1b antibodies were not
investigated [10]. Serum bacteria and virus serology were
investigated using standard methods, including Campylobacter jejuni, Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), Cytomegalovirus
(CMV) and Mycoplasma pneumoniae.

Other paraclinical investigations
Electrodiagnostic studies (EDX) were performed using
standard methods. Demyelination was defined using standard criteria, including motor distal latency prolongation,
reduction of motor conduction velocity, prolongation of
F-wave latency, absent F-waves, motor conduction block,
abnormal temporal dispersion, and distal Compound Muscle Action Potential (CMAP) duration prolongation [11].
Brain and spinal cord magnetic resonance imaging (MRI),
and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis were performed using
standard methods.

Statistical analysis
We analyzed the clinical and paraclinical characteristics
of our patients, including age, sex, clinical subtype (MFS,
MFS/GBS, GBS, GBS/BE, and BE), initial clinical severity
(as measured with Intensive Care Unit [ICU] admission),
anti-GQ1b antibodies serological profile (IgM, IgG, and
IgM + IgG), and treatment (PE, IVIg, and CTX) to identify
predictors of full recovery at 12 months and predictors of

relapse. Quantitative variables were investigated using Wilcoxon-Mann–Whitney test and Student’s t-test, and qualitative variables were investigated using chi-squared and Fisher
tests. Two-tailed tests were used with significance level set
to 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS,
Microsoft Excel Software and BiostaTgv.

Results
Neurological characteristics of 166 patients
with acute anti‑GQ1b antibody syndrome
A hundred and sixty-six (166) patients with acute anti-GQ1b
syndrome were identified in the 2012–2022 timeframe in
19 French and Swiss University Hospitals. All patients presented with a monophasic illness with sensory and/or motor
symptoms, altered tendon reflexes, reaching nadir within
28 days, in the absence of alternative cause, consistent with
GBS. Clinical characteristics of this group of 166 patients
are detailed in Table 1. Mean age was 50 years (4–90) and
51% of cases were female. The most frequent clinical signs
were abolished tendon reflexes (80% of cases), distal upper
and lower limbs sensory symptoms (78%), ophthalmoplegia
(68%), sensory ataxia (67%), limb muscle weakness (45%)
and bulbar weakness (45%). Facial weakness was observed
in 27% of cases, and Babinski sign in 3%. Most patients
(69%) had a preceding event before disease onset, including
respiratory infection (34% of cases), gastroenteritis (15%),
flu-like syndrome (10%), and vaccination (4%). Twentyeight percent (28%) of patients presented with headache
when admitted, and 22% had central nervous system (CNS)
involvement, including mainly cerebellar ataxia and more
rarely hypersomnolence. One patient (0.6%) died of respiratory failure in ICU.
Clinical classification of patients is detailed in Table 2.
Fifty-three patients (32%) had MFS spectrum clinical features, thirty-six patients (22%) had GBS spectrum clinical
features, and only one patient (0.6%) had BE spectrum features. Seventy-three patients (44%) presented with mixed
clinical features, including sixty-five patients (39%) with
MFS/GBS features and eight patients (5%) with GBS/BE
features. Three patients were unclassified because of insufficient clinical data.

Other paraclinical investigations
Paraclinical characteristics of our patients are detailed in
Table 3. CSF analysis was performed on average 5 days after
disease onset and showed raised protein content in 24% of
cases. In our group of 166 patients, mean CSF protein content was 0.6 g/L. EDX studies were performed on average
9 days after disease onset and were normal in 46% of cases.
Demyelinating neuropathy was observed in 28% of cases
and axonal neuropathy in 23%. Brain MRI was normal in
106/122 patients (87%). Cranial nerve abnormalities, including hyperintense signals and/or contrast enhancement after

gadolinium injection involving oculomotor, trigeminal, and
facial nerve, were observed in 11% of cases. Spinal cord
MRI showed myelitis in two patients, including 1 cervical
and 1 thoracic. Serological proof of recent infection was
found in 17% of cases, including Campylobacter jejuni (7%
of cases), Mycoplasma pneumoniae (4%), Epstein Barr virus
(2%) and Cytomegalovirus (2%).

Treatment and outcome of 166 patients with acute
anti‑GQ1b antibody syndrome
Fifty-three patients (32%) were admitted in ICU, and almost
half of them (26; 16%) required mechanical ventilation. One
patient died in ICU from respiratory failure. Most patients
(148 patients; 89%) were treated, including with intravenous
immunoglobulins (IVIg) (138 patients; 83%), corticosteroids (CTX) (17 patients; 10%) and plasma exchange (PE) (9
patients; 5%). Amongst the 138 patients treated with IVIg,
124 were treated with one dose of 2 g/kg administered over
5 days, 13 with 2 doses of 2 g/kg, and 1 with 3 doses of 2 g/
kg. The 17 patients treated with CTX were given IV methylprednisolone 1 g/day over 1 to 3 days in all cases. Five
PE sessions were done in the 9 patients treated with PE. No
maintenance therapy was given in the cases described here.
After a mean 12 months follow-up, 69% of patients presented with complete neurological recovery and 29% with
incomplete neurological recovery. Thirteen patients (8%)
had presented with a previous acute neurological episode
before being included in our series, and 12 patients (7%) presented with an acute anti-GQ1b antibody syndrome relapse
after the episode described here. Overall, 25/166 patients
(15%) from our series had a relapsing history.

Predictors of full recovery at 12 months
and predictors of relapse
We identified several predictors of complete recovery at
12 months, including age < 70 years (74% of patients with
age < 70 years recovered completely vs. 45% of patients
with age > 70 years, p < 0.001), no initial ICU admission
(50% of patients who had not been admitted in ICU recovered completely vs 27% of patients who had been admitted in ICU, p < 0.008) and the presence of anti-GQ1b IgG
antibodies, whether associated or not with IgM antibodies
(73% of patients with anti-GQ1b IgG antibodies recovered
completely vs 28% of patients with only anti-GQ1b IgM
antibodies, p = 0.02). Patients with MFS tended to more frequently fully recover than patients with MFS/GBS, GBS and
GBS/BE, but the comparison between the MFS subgroup
and other subgroups did not reach statistical significance
(80% of patients with MFS recovered completely vs 64%
of patients with MFS/GBS, GBS and GBS/BE, p = 0.08).
Importantly, treatment was not a predictor of full recovery.

Discussion
In this retrospective study we analyzed the clinical, EDX,
biological, treatment response and outcome characteristics
of 166 patients with acute anti-GQ1b antibody syndrome.
To our knowledge, this is the largest cohort from Western
Europe described until now.
The clinical spectrum of acute anti-GQ1b antibody syndrome is not well known. Indeed, in contrast with our study,
a majority of previous studies analyzed patients on the basis
of clinical presentation, i.e., MFS and/or BE, and not on the
basis of the presence of anti-GQ1b antibodies in serum [9].

with typical BE, highlighting the fact that BE is an extremely
rare condition. Our study also shows that acute anti-GQ1b
syndrome tends to recur, with almost 1 out of 7 patients
(15%) presenting with relapses. Relapsing acute anti-GQ1b
syndrome has already been described, and no predisposing
factor has been identified until now [15–17]. Our study confirms the latter, as we failed to identify predictors of relapse,

In our study, 56% of patients presented with anti-GT1a
antibodies in addition to anti-GQ1b antibodies. This result
is in accordance with previous results demonstrating that
almost all patients with anti-GQ1b IgG antibodies also present with an anti-GT1a IgG activity [18, 19]. Absorption
studies suggest that the same antibody reacts with GQ1b
and GT1a [18, 19].
In our study, 46% of patients had normal EDX investigations, 28% had demyelinating neuropathy, and 23% had
axonal neuropathy. It is interesting to note that EDX investigations in patients with MFS and BE usually show a pattern of axonal sensory neuropathy with no demyelination
[20, 21]. This apparent discrepancy is likely explained by
the fact that we studied a group of patients with acute antiGQ1b antibody syndrome and not a group of patients with
MFS. Most patients in our study (66% of cases) presented
with GBS-like clinical phenotypes, and EDX findings in our
study likely reflect this clinical heterogeneity. This discrepancy may also be in part explained by the fact that EDX
investigations were performed early in our study, i.e., after
a mean delay of 9 days after symptoms onset, before significant axonal loss and/or demyelination is likely to appear.
MFS and BE are usually self-limiting disorders with
good spontaneous prognosis [7]. In this context, whether
patients with MFS and BE require immunomodulating treatment remains unclear. A 2007 Cochrane review found no
randomized data for the treatment of MFS and BE [22].
However, the authors proposed to extrapolate the evidence

for treating GBS to both MFS and BE [22]. Interestingly,
a recent retrospective study found treated and untreated
MFS patients had similar outcome [23]. In this context,
one may hypothesize treatment may be useful in patients
with a severe GBS-like clinical phenotype [24], and perhaps less relevant in patients with a typical MFS phenotype
[25]. In our series, 83% of patients were treated with IVIg,
and outcome was excellent in most cases. However, when
considering predictors of full recovery at 12 months in our
series, we observed patients with age < 70 years, MFS clinical presentation, no ICU admission and anti-GQ1b IgG antibodies more frequently presented with complete recovery
at 1 year in comparison with other patients, i.e., patients
with age > 70 years, GBS clinical presentation, initial ICU
admission and no anti-GQ1b IgG antibodies. Interestingly,
treatment was not a predictor of full recovery. In this context,
whether IVIg treatment was relevant in our series remains
unclear. Additional studies are needed to determine whether
immunomodulating therapies are useful in all patients or
only a subset of patients with acute anti-GQ1b antibody
syndrome.
Pathological data suggest MFS is a nodo-paranodopathy
likely involving a complement-mediated disruption of the
node of Ranvier [26]. When considering CNS involvement,
it has been shown that sera from BE patients destroyed the
blood–brain-barrier (BBB), which was not the case with sera
from patients with MFS. BE serum-related BBB disruption
may be the result of autocrine secretion of metalloproteinase
MMP-9 from BBB-composing endothelial cells [27].
Our series has limits. First, the retrospective design
led to missing data, including detailed clinical and EDX
follow-up. Second, the multicentric design led to absent

4988

standardized data collection and clinical evaluation. Third,
we did not have data detailing how many patients with no
polyneuropathy presented with anti-GQ1b antibodies in
our 19 recruiting centers. Nonetheless, it is known that
the incidence of anti-ganglioside antibodies in healthy
individuals ranges from 1 to 9% [28–30]. Fourth, the fact
that anti-GQ1b antibodies were investigated using different methods in each University Hospital did not allow us
to investigate correlations between antibodies titers and
other variables such as outcome, relapse, and clinical
presentation.

Conclusion
To summarize, our study demonstrates acute anti-GQ1b
antibody syndrome presents with a wide GBS-like clinical
spectrum mostly involving PNS and rarely CNS. Mortality
is low, outcome is good in 2/3 of cases, and relapses are
frequent. Most patients in our study were treated with IVIg,
but whether this treatment is useful in all cases of acute antiGQ1b antibody syndrome remains to be evaluated.
