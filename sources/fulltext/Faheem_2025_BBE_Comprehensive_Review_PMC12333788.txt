Bickerstaff Encephalitis: A Comprehensive Narrative Review of Pathophysiology, Clinical Features, and Global Health Considerations

Authors: Muhammad Shaheer Bin Faheem, Nafila Zeeshan, Syed Atta Ur Rafe, Sumaya Samadi
Affiliations: Department of Medicine and Surgery, Karachi Institute of Medical Sciences; Department of Internal Medicine, Dow University of Health Sciences; Department of Internal Medicine, Jinnah Sindh Medical University; Department of Internal Medicine, Kabul University of Medical Sciences
Journal: Annals of Medicine and Surgery, 2025 Jul 16;87(8):5122–5131
DOI: 10.1097/MS9.0000000000003587
PMCID: PMC12333788

ABSTRACT

Bickerstaff brainstem encephalitis (BBE) represents a rare autoimmune neurological disorder initially described in 1951, sharing immunological and clinical characteristics with both Guillain–Barré syndrome and Miller–Fisher syndrome. The condition emerges following infection or vaccination and involves anti-GQ1b IgG antibodies as critical biomarkers. This narrative review synthesized literature from PubMed, Cochrane, Global Health Library, and Google Scholar through 2025, applying SANRA quality standards.

Key Findings: Most BBE cases demonstrate positive anti-GQ1b IgG antibodies, typically presenting with ophthalmoplegia, ataxia, and altered consciousness. Cerebrospinal fluid examination frequently reveals elevated protein levels; MRI may show midbrain lesions. IVIG and plasmapheresis represent primary therapeutic interventions, with documented effectiveness in symptom reduction and recovery acceleration. Pediatric patients demonstrate faster healing; prognosis remains generally favorable, though severe presentations may necessitate intensive care management.

Conclusion: BBE requires early recognition and immunotherapy initiation. Anti-GQ1b IgG detection distinguishes it from other neurological conditions. Further research remains essential for standardization and enhanced outcomes.

Keywords: anti-GQ1b IgG antibodies, Bickerstaff brainstem encephalitis, Guillain–Barré syndrome, immunotherapy, Miller–Fisher syndrome

INTRODUCTION

Bickerstaff and Cloake initially described a condition resembling BBE in 1951 as "Mesencephalitis and Rhombencephalitis," with Bickerstaff providing formal characterization in 1957. This extremely rare disorder demonstrates notable clinical and immunological overlap with both Guillain–Barré syndrome and Miller–Fisher syndrome.

The condition manifests as sudden brainstem dysfunction, typically emerging days following infection or vaccination. Campylobacter jejuni, a Gram-negative bacterium causing enteritis, has been identified as a preceding infectious agent in multiple related syndromes. Molecular mimicry between C. jejuni lipo-oligosaccharide and nervous system gangliosides represents a key mechanism underlying both GBS and FS pathogenesis.

BBE characteristically presents with acute or subacute progressive ophthalmoplegia and ataxia alongside impaired consciousness or pyramidal signs. The presence of anti-GQ1b IgG antibodies provides diagnostic confirmation. With an estimated prevalence of approximately 0.78 cases per 100,000 individuals annually, BBE presents significant diagnostic challenges for clinicians.

Although historically considered distinct, BBE is now recognized as part of a disease spectrum including GBS and MFS due to shared anti-GQ1b IgG antibody presence. However, BBE differs fundamentally by affecting the central nervous system and frequently presenting with consciousness impairment. Early treatment with IVIG or plasma exchange has demonstrated effectiveness in symptom improvement and disease progression prevention.

METHODOLOGY

A comprehensive database search included PubMed, Cochrane, Global Health Library (incorporating WHO's Global Index), and Google Scholar, targeting literature through 2025. The search strategy employed specific terminology: "Bickerstaff encephalitis," "BBE syndrome," "Miller Fisher syndrome," "GBS syndrome," "anti-GQ1b antibody syndrome," and "autoimmune encephalitis."

Inclusion Criteria:
- Studies discussing BBE pathophysiology, clinical features, diagnosis, and management
- Case reports
- Case series

Exclusion Criteria:
- Studies not reporting desired outcomes
- Conference abstracts
- Gray literature
- Non-English language articles

Quality was assessed using the Scale for the Assessment of Narrative Review Articles (SANRA), JBI critical appraisal tools, Newcastle Ottawa Scale, and narrative GRADE approach.

RESULTS: STUDY CHARACTERISTICS AND SUMMARY OF FINDINGS

Twenty-three studies were included, comprising observational studies, case reports, case series, and narrative reviews.

Key studies included:
- Zhuo et al. (2019): 19 pediatric BBE patients receiving immunoglobulin, corticosteroids, and supportive care
- Schubert et al. (2019): 40 adult autoimmune encephalitis patients requiring ICU treatment
- Yoshikawa et al. (2020): 7 BBE patients (4 anti-GQ1b antibody positive, 3 negative)
- Ito et al. (2008): 53 patients with BBE and Fisher syndrome
- Odaka et al. (2001): 20 BBE patients and 29 Fisher syndrome patients

Key Findings Across Studies:
- Anti-GQ1b IgG antibody detection occurred in over 50% of pediatric BBE cases
- BBE and Fisher syndrome demonstrated continuous clinical spectrum characteristics
- Treatment with IVIG or corticosteroids produced shorter recovery times compared to supportive care alone
- Pediatric patients demonstrated faster recovery trajectories

PATHOPHYSIOLOGICAL MECHANISMS

Molecular mimicry constitutes a critical BBE pathogenesis mechanism. Specific microorganisms, including Campylobacter jejuni and Haemophilus influenzae, express GQ1b epitopes that trigger anti-GQ1b antibody production during infection. These antibodies subsequently mistakenly target host tissues expressing GQ1b, precipitating neurological manifestations.

A 2020 case documented BBE development in a 72-year-old woman following SARS-CoV-2 infection, further reinforcing post-infectious autoimmunity's role in disease initiation. Anti-GM1 antibodies appeared in approximately 40% of patients, though their specific pathogenic contribution remains unclear, suggesting additional antibody profiles may contribute to disease heterogeneity beyond anti-GQ1b involvement.

CLINICAL PRESENTATION

BBE manifests as an immune-mediated disorder presenting with sudden external ophthalmoplegia onset, ataxia, and altered consciousness, frequently following preceding infection. IgG anti-GQ1b antibodies appear commonly detectable in acute-phase serum.

The characteristic clinical triad includes:
1. Ophthalmoplegia – external eye movement impairment
2. Ataxia – incoordination and balance disturbance
3. Altered consciousness – ranging from mild confusion to deep coma

DIAGNOSIS

BBE diagnosis relies upon the clinical triad of ataxia, bilateral external ophthalmoplegia, and impaired consciousness. Brain MRI occasionally reveals small central midbrain lesions.

Diagnostic Significance of Anti-GQ1b Antibodies:

Koga and colleagues established definite and probable BBE diagnostic criteria. Definitive diagnosis requires acute ataxia, ophthalmoplegia, and altered consciousness onset within four weeks, followed by remission within twelve weeks, accompanied by anti-GQ1b antibody presence. Exclusion of overlapping neurological conditions strengthens diagnostic certainty.

Anti-GQ1b antibodies demonstrate high expression in cranial nerves III, IV, and VI, limb muscle spindles, and the brainstem reticular formation, explaining hallmark symptoms.

Ito et al. examination of 53 BBE cases revealed 68% (36/53) tested positive for serum IgG anti-GQ1b antibodies. However, a retrospective 19-pediatric-patient study reported significantly lower detection rates. Antibody absence does not exclude BBE, as alternative immune mechanisms may contribute.

SHARED PATHOGENIC SPECTRUM WITH GBS AND MFS

Anti-GQ1b antibody presence is not exclusive to BBE; they appear in both GBS and MFS. Some researchers propose that BBE, GBS, and MFS constitute a continuum of post-infectious encephalitis and polyneuritis, with anti-GQ1b antibodies serving as common pathogenic hallmarks. Multiple disorder antibody detection supports the notion that BBE represents a GBS variant or broader anti-ganglioside antibody spectrum component.

TREATMENT AND MANAGEMENT

Currently, no established BBE treatment guidelines exist; different therapeutic regimens apply based on patient clinical status. However, BBE responds favorably to IVIG and plasmapheresis.

Corticosteroids:
Methylprednisolone is the most commonly employed corticosteroid: 1 gram daily for 2–5 days, followed by oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg/day for 1–4 weeks.

Intravenous Immunoglobulin (IVIG):
Administered at 400 mg/kg/day for five days or as rapid course of 1–2 g/kg over 1–2 days.

Plasmapheresis:
When corticosteroids or IVIG fail, plasmapheresis is considered. Standard protocol involves one session every other day for 5–7 cycles.

Emerging Therapies:

Rituximab targets CD20 receptors on B-cells, suppressing antibody-mediated pathways. A recent meta-analysis demonstrated rituximab improved outcomes in BBE patients unresponsive to first-line treatment.

Eculizumab, a humanized monoclonal antibody inhibiting complement pathways, shows promising rat model results, though human efficacy evidence remains inconclusive.

CLINICAL COURSE AND PROGNOSIS

Average BBE hospitalization duration approximates 24 days. Over 20% required ICU admission (averaging 13 days), with all requiring mechanical ventilation.

BBE demonstrates generally favorable prognosis, with faster pediatric recovery—typically within 4 to 6 weeks post-treatment initiation. 70% experienced no lasting neurological deficits. BBE-related death occurs extremely rarely, appearing in only 2.7% of studied cases.

LIMITATIONS

1. BBE remains rare and under-recognized; clinical trials are extremely scarce
2. Insufficient high-quality evidence regarding therapeutic efficacy and safety
3. Lacking randomized trials necessitated case report and small-sample case series inclusion
4. Included studies demonstrate proneness to sampling and reporting biases

CONCLUSION

Bickerstaff brainstem encephalitis represents a rare, immune-mediated neurological disorder characterized by progressive ophthalmoplegia, ataxia, and impaired consciousness, frequently overlapping with Miller–Fisher syndrome and Guillain–Barré syndrome.

Diagnosis remains largely clinical, supported by CSF analysis, MRI findings, and nerve conduction studies. Most patients demonstrate favorable prognosis with recovery within weeks to months. Severe cases may precipitate life-threatening complications including respiratory failure and autonomic dysfunction.

While IVIG, corticosteroids, and plasmapheresis demonstrate clinical benefits, standardized treatment protocols remain absent. Future research should prioritize identifying additional BBE biomarkers and establishing evidence-based guidelines.
