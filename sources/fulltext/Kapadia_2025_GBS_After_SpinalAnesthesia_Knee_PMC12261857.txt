# A rapid onset of Guillain–Barre syndrome following spinal anesthesia for knee arthroscopy: a case report

## Abstract

BackgroundGuillain–Barré syndrome, a rare autoimmune polyneuropathy, typically follows infections and rarely surgical procedures. Post-surgical Guillain–Barré syndrome is an uncommon subset with significant clinical implications.Case presentationWe report the case of a 76-year-old Middle Eastern (Bahrainian) male who developed Guillain–Barré syndrome immediately after undergoing routine knee arthroscopy under spinal anesthesia. Shortly postoperatively the patient developed urinary incontinence, followed by progressive bilateral lower limb weakness and sensory deficits. Neurological symptoms rapidly deteriorated by day two and urgent neurological evaluation was taken. Initial and repeat spinal magnetic resonance imaging showed no structural abnormalities, and cerebrospinal fluid analysis lacked albuminocytological dissociation, complicating diagnosis. Electrodiagnostic studies confirmed Guillain–Barré syndrome with demyelinating patterns. Treatment with intravenous immunoglobulin was initiated promptly, resulting in remarkable recovery. By discharge, the patient had regained full motor and sensory function and achieved complete recovery at 6 months follow-up.ConclusionThis case highlights the challenges of diagnosing early post-surgical Guillain–Barré syndrome, which can present atypically and without classical cerebrospinal fluid findings. However, early recognition, multidisciplinary collaboration, and timely intravenous immunoglobulin therapy facilitated a favorable outcome. This report highlights the challenges of diagnosing early post-surgical Guillain–Barré syndrome and demonstrates the potential for complete recovery with prompt intervention, emphasizing the need for awareness and timely management in similar cases.


## Introduction
Guillain–Barré syndrome (GBS), a rare but serious autoimmune disorder, is characterized by acute flaccid paralysis. It has a global incidence ranging from 0.38 to 2.53 cases per 100,000 individuals annually, with most studies reporting 1.1 to 1.8 per 100,000 [1]. The condition often follows bacterial or viral infections, with Campylobacter jejuni being the most implicated pathogen. Other infections, including Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae, cytomegalovirus, and Epstein-Barr virus, have also been associated with GBS. However, approximately one-third of cases remain idiopathic, with no clear infectious cause.
Postsurgical GBS, a rare subset of the disorder, is defined by the onset of typical GBS symptoms within six weeks of a surgical procedure [2]. Though uncommon, it carries significant clinical implications, with a reported mortality rate as high as 16% [3]. Orthopedic and gastrointestinal surgeries have shown the highest associations with postsurgical GBS, though the exact mechanism underlying this relationship remains unclear [3]. Early recognition is critical, as delays in diagnosis and treatment can lead to severe complications, including respiratory failure requiring mechanical ventilation.
This report describes a rare case of a 76-year-old male who developed GBS following routine left knee arthroscopy. The patient’s unique clinical presentation and diagnostic findings establish this case as an uncommon but noteworthy occurrence. It further highlights the challenges in diagnosing postsurgical GBS, particularly in differentiating it from other postoperative complications, such as anesthetic-related neuropathies or alternative neurological disorders. Furthermore, the case underscores the importance of timely recognition and intervention, which were pivotal in achieving a favorable outcome for the patient.

## Case presentation
Our patient was a 76-year-old Middle Eastern (Bahraini) male with a medical history of asthma, hypertension, and ischemic heart disease underwent left knee arthroscopy under spinal anesthesia for a meniscal injury. The patient was not on anticoagulant therapy at the time of surgery. The patient had no recent history of vaccination, preceding infections (respiratory or gastrointestinal), or prior surgical interventions. Spinal anesthesia with intrathecal fentanyl was uneventfully administered at a dose of 25 mcg along with 2.5 mL of 0.5% hyperbaric bupivacaine via a 26-gauge Whitacre needle. The intraoperative and immediate postoperative course was stable, and the patient was transferred to the ward without complications.
On the evening of surgery, the patient developed concerning neurological symptoms, initially presenting with an inability to sense the urge to void, followed by urinary incontinence. A urology consultation was obtained, and a urinary catheter was placed. The persistence of symptoms, coupled with evolving neurological signs including reduced sensation in the lower limbs, prompted a neurological consultation. The initial neurological assessment prompted further evaluation to rule out spinal or neurological complications, particularly given the recent spinal anesthesia.
On the first postoperative day, the patient reported progressive bilateral lower limb weakness. Physical examination revealed muscle strength graded 2/5 in both lower limbs and patchy sensory loss in a stocking distribution extending to the T10 dermatome, but with intact reflexes. The neurology team initially suspected cauda equina syndrome owing to the recent spinal anesthesia and recommended an urgent magnetic resonance image (MRI) of the spine. The patient and his family expressed significant anxiety over the possibility of a severe spinal complication, necessitating counseling from the anesthesia and orthopedic teams. An MRI under general anesthesia returned normal, with no evidence of cauda equina syndrome. Other differential diagnoses, including spinal trauma and transverse myelitis were discussed and ruled out. Spinal trauma was deemed unlikely given the atraumatic nature of spinal anesthesia and normal MRI scan, and transverse myelitis was excluded due to the absence of inflammatory changes on MRI.
The patient’s neurological status deteriorated further on the second postoperative day, with the development of fecal incontinence. Rectal examination revealed a lax anal sphincter and absent sensation around the anus. Motor function continued to decline, with muscle strength decreasing to 1/5 in the left lower limb. Neurological examination now showed asymmetric reflexes, preserved on the right but absent on the left, though Babinski’s sign remained negative bilaterally. During a multidisciplinary discussion involving anesthesia, orthopedic, and neurology teams, the possibility of Guillain–Barré syndrome (GBS) was raised, given the rapid progression of symptoms, particularly the pattern of weakness, sensory changes, and sphincter involvement.
A comprehensive diagnostic workup was initiated on the third postoperative day. A second MRI of the spine revealed mild peri-conus enhancement and nonthickened, mildly enhanced anterior nerve roots in the lumbar spine, findings consistent with inflammatory or demyelinating processes. Computed tomography of the brain showed only age-related changes. Cerebrospinal fluid analysis was performed, showing no evidence of infection, inflammation, or albuminocytological dissociation—a finding sometimes absent in early GBS cases. Despite the worsening neurological symptoms, the patient’s respiratory and bulbar function remained intact. Given the confirmed diagnosis and the risk of progression to respiratory failure, treatment with intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was initiated on postoperative day three, at a dose of 30 grams daily for five days. Supportive measures, including physiotherapy, were implemented to preserve joint mobility and prevent complications from immobility. The patient showed gradual improvement after starting IVIG therapy. By the fifth day of treatment, motor strength began to improve, and reflexes returned in the right lower limb. A remarkable recovery was noted by the ninth postoperative day, with the patient regaining full motor function in both lower limbs, complete resolution of sensory deficits, and recovery of normal bowel and bladder control. Given the patient’s clinical improvement following IVIG therapy initiation, a repeat CSF analysis was not pursued. After improvement in symptoms the patient was discharged in stable condition with no residual neurological deficits. Despite the comorbid burden associated, the patient's recovery seemed to be unaffected by the comorbidities. The patient was evaluated at one-month follow-up, at which point he had resumed his normal daily activities. A subsequent assessment at six-months confirmed sustained recovery and return to baseline functioning.

## Discussion
Guillain-Barré Syndrome (GBS) is a rare, acute immune-mediated polyneuropathy characterized by progressive motor weakness, areflexia, and varying degrees of sensory, autonomic, and cranial nerve involvement. Postsurgical GBS, accounting for ~ 4.5–9.1% of all GBS cases, typically manifests within 6 weeks of surgery [4]. The exact mechanism of postsurgical GBS remains unclear, though it is hypothesized to involve an autoimmune attack on peripheral nerves. A dysregulated immune response, potentially triggered by lipid-based pharmacological agents, has been also suggested as a contributing factor in the development of postsurgical GBS [5].
Our case presents several unique features that contribute significantly to the existing literature. First, the remarkably early onset of symptoms—beginning on the day of surgery—stands in stark contrast to the typical presentation timeline and posed a significant diagnostic challenge. Unlike the more common gradual progression of post-surgical GBS, the symptoms emerged abruptly, initially raising concerns about anesthesia-related complications. The absence of typical GBS triggers, such as preceding respiratory or gastrointestinal infections, combined with normal initial cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis, created a diagnostic challenge.
While Hocker et al.’s 20-year retrospective review documented the earliest onset at postoperative day seven, with an average onset of 15 days, our patient developed symptoms immediately after surgery [6]. This aligns more closely with the exceptional case reported by Bamberger et al., where GBS manifested within 2 hours postsurgery, representing one of the earliest documented onsets in the literature [7].
Initial and repeat spinal MRI ruled out structural abnormalities, redirecting the diagnostic focus to alternative etiologies, such as GBS. CSF analysis did not reveal albuminocytological dissociation, a hallmark of GBS, complicating the diagnosis. However, it is important to note that the classical albuminocytological dissociation in CSF may be absent in early GBS, taking up to 2 weeks to develop, and should not delay treatment if clinical suspicion is high [8]. Diagnostic confirmation was ultimately achieved through electrodiagnostic studies, which demonstrated demyelinating patterns consistent with GBS.
Upon confirmation of the diagnosis, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) therapy was promptly initiated on post-operative day 3 due to its proven efficacy and ease of administration. Both IVIG and plasmapheresis remain the mainstay treatments for GBS. In a similar case reported by Sayin et al., IVIG administration resulted in significant symptomatic improvement, a finding that aligns with the outcomes observed in our patient [9]. The patient’s clinical improvement following IVIG initiation did not prompt for further investigations including a repeat CSF analysis. However, had symptoms persisted or worsened, repeat lumbar puncture would have been considered to assess for delayed albuminocytological dissociation. At discharge, the patient had minimal residual weakness and was in stable condition. The patient achieved complete recovery without any complications and a return to baseline functioning.
The favorable outcome in our case contrasts with the typically worse prognosis associated with postsurgical GBS compared to idiopathic cases. Literature suggests that post-surgical GBS, particularly axonal variants, often results in prolonged recovery periods and persistent deficits [10]. The rapid and complete recovery observed in our patient may be attributed to several factors: early recognition despite atypical presentation, timely initiation of appropriate therapy with IVIG, and comprehensive supportive care through a multidisciplinary approach.
The experience gained from this case emphasizes the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for GBS in postsurgical patients presenting with neurological symptoms, regardless of the timing of onset. It also highlights the value of prompt neurophysiological studies in confirming the diagnosis when clinical findings and initial investigations are equivocal. Moreover, the timely initiation of treatment can significantly enhance patient recovery, as evidenced in this case.

## Conclusion
This case report highlights the importance of early recognition and prompt treatment of post-surgical Guillain–Barré Syndrome (GBS), a rare but potentially devastating complication. The atypical presentation and rapid onset of symptoms in our patient underscore the need for a high index of suspicion in postsurgical patients with neurological symptoms. Timely initiation of intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) therapy and comprehensive supportive care enabled our patient to achieve complete recovery, contrasting with the typically poorer prognosis associated with postsurgical GBS. This case reinforces the value of multidisciplinary collaboration and prompt neurophysiological studies in diagnosing and managing postsurgical GBS, ultimately optimizing patient outcomes.
