Chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy (CIDP) is an immune mediated demyelinating neuropathy 
that can lead to secondary axonal degeneration and irreversible weakness and disability. Early effective treat-
ment is therefore necessary to minimize the degree of axonal degeneration. Prior to 2024 the only FDA approved 
therapy for CIDP was intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg). In 2024, efgartigimod (Vyvgart-Hytrulo), a FCRn 
inhibiting therapy (FIT) was approved for treatment of CIDP based on the phase II Adhere study. In the controlled 
setting of the phase II clinical study patients who were stable on IVIg were taken off treatment to ensure that their 
disease was active, and patients who worsened were then treated with efgartigimod. The responders were then 
randomized (in phase B) to either placebo or continued efgartigimod treatment. In the real world setting it is not 
feasible to stop IVIg and let patients worsen before starting a FIT, thus the transition from IVIG to efgartigimod in 
a real world setting was not studied in the pivotal trial. We have treated nine patients with FIT in our practice and 
report findings of four of those patients who had severe relapse of CIDP after treatment. Five of the other patients 
neither improved nor declined with FIT. This raises questions about the issues related to transitioning patients 
from IVIG to efgartigimod.
1.Introduction
Chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy (CIDP) is an 
immune mediated demyelinating neuropathy that can lead to secondary 
axonal degeneration and irreversible weakness and disability [1]. Early 
effective treatment is therefore necessary to minimize the degree of 
axonal degeneration. Prior to 2024 the only FDA approved therapy for 
CIDP was intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg) [2]. In 2024, efgartigimod 
(Vyvgart-Hytrulo), a FCRn inhibiting therapy (FIT) was approved for 
treatment of CIDP based on the phase II Adhere study [3]. In the 
controlled setting of the phase II clinical study patients who were stable 
on IVIg were taken off treatment to ensure that their disease was active, 
and patients who worsened were then treated with efgartigimod. The 
responders were then randomized (in phase B) to either placebo or 
continued efgartigimod treatment. In the real world setting it is not 
feasible to stop IVIg and let patients worsen before starting a FIT, thus 
the transition from IVIG to efgartigimod in a real world setting was not 
studied in the pivotal trial. We have treated nine patients with FIT in our 
practice and report findings of four of those patients who had severe 
relapse of CIDP after treatment. Five of the other patients neither improved nor declined with FIT. This raises questions about the issues 
related to transitioning patients from IVIG to efgartigimod.
2.Materials and methods
The case series was collected as part of our clinical practices at the 
neuroscience Institute. The information is related to care that patient has 
received as part of their standard of care (See Table 1).

been stable on IVIg therapy for the last one year.

was 5 and she needed a walker for ambulation and had severe difficulty 
using her arms and hands in daily activities.

Given her continuing mild distal leg weakness with ankle dorsi -

She received her first injection 12 days after her last dose of IVIG. 
Two days after infusion she noticed an increase in sensory dysesthesias 
in her feet extending to the mid-calf level. The day after her second dose 
of efgartigimod (day 8 of initiation of therapy) she noticed increased 
weakness proximally and distally in her legs which progressed to the

no longer able to ambulate normally and required assistance for her 
ADLs. The leg weakness and imbalance led to a tibial fracture after a fall. 
She was admitted to the hospital and at this point her MRC scores in all 
four limbs were 2–3/5 and INCAT score was 7. She received 10 cycles of 
plasmapheresis and began to have some improvement but still required 
being discharged to a rehab facility where she was started back on IVIG. 
Eight weeks after the treatment with efgartigimod had been initiated she 
remained with an INCAT score of 5 due to the need to use a walker and 
difficulty performing routine activities with her hands. 
Case 2.A 74-year-old man with distal variant of CIDP (satisfying 
EFNS/PNS clinical and electrophysiological criteria) had been on a 
stable dose of IVIG for the last seven years.
At initial presentation he had distal arm and leg weakness and 
numbness with INCAT disability score of 4.
He responded to IVIg and INCAT score improved to 1 over the initial 
six months of treatment and he had remained stable since. He had 
weakness in ankle dorsiflexion (MRC 4) but was able to walk indepen -
dently and he had no residual symptoms in his arms.
He requested a switch to efgartigimod to look for further improve -
ment and efgartigimod was started six weeks after his last IVIg dose. 
During this delay between therapies, he noticed increased numbness in 
his legs, but he remained able to walk independently. Three days after 
his first dose of efgartigimod he noticed weakness in his hands and 
proximal arms. After the second dose (8 days after efgartigimod initia -
tion) his arms worsened, and he could not lift them over his shoulders 
(should abductors MRC 2), and he began to have worsening of his ankle 
dorsiflexors. Two days after his third dose (17 days after efgartigimod 
initiation) he required a walker to walk and could not independently 
care for himself due to his arm weakness (INCAT score of 6). Efgartigi -
mod was stopped and he was placed urgently back on IVIg. Within 2 
weeks of IVIg he started improving and at 6 weeks he has regained near 
baseline strength in his arms except for mild impairment of dexterity in 
his hands. He remains with residual leg weakness requiring a walker for 
ambulation (INCAT 4). 
Case 3.A 58-year-old female with a 15-year-history of typical CIDP 
(satisfying EFNS clinical and electrophysiological criteria) had been 
stable on IVIg therapy for the last ten years.
At the onset of her disease 15 years ago she was hospitalized with 
profound quadriplegia and INCAT score of 8. She was treated with 
plasma exchange and improved. She then started IVIG. She improved on IVIG and remained stable. Five years previous, she had participated in a 
research study for CIDP during which she had worsening of strength and 
withdrew and went back to IVIG. Her INCAT score remained stable at 2, 
on IVIG 1 g/kg/day every three weeks. When the IVIG dosing interval 
was tapered to more than 4 weeks she had worsening of weakness.
Due to fatigue from the IVIG infusions she chose to switch to efgar -
tigimod. Efgartigimod was started 20 days after her last IVIG infusion. 
Within 3 days of her first dose, she had an increase in her sensory 
symptoms and worsening gait ataxia. After the second dose (day 9) she 
had a dramatic increase in her weakness to the point that she had 
proximal arm and leg weakness that limited her from doing her ADLs 
and she required a cane for ambulation (INCAT score of 4). FIT was 
stopped and she was placed back on IVIG and within two weeks she no 
longer needed the cane but still had limitations in arms and legs prox-
imally and distally. Two weeks after the IVIG she had an increase in her 
weakness (INCAT of 7) that necessitated being admitted into the hos-
pital for urgent plasmapheresis. This therapy is ongoing. 
Case 4.A 47-year-old female with a five-year history of multifocal 
CIDP (satisfying EFNS clinical and electrophysiological criteria) was 
stable on IVIG for the last four years.
Her initial presentation five years ago was of progressive asymmetric 
upper extremity predominant weakness and numbness along with 
hyporeflexia. Her INCAT score was 5 and her initial treatment was with 
IVIG and within 3 months she had improvement of left hand strength. 
Prednisone was added and she had significant improvement in strength 
over the next 6 months. She developed adverse effects to prednisone and 
mycophenolate was started and prednisone was tapered off. She 
continued on a combination of IVIG and mycophenolate and has 
remained on that regimen for the last 4 years, with an INCAT of 2. She 
did have one period where, due to insurance reasons the IVIG interval 
increased beyond six weeks, and she noticed increased hand weakness.
Due to the persistent weakness in her hands despite long term IVIG 
therapy she was switched to efgartigmod. Within three days of the first 
dose, she had increased weakness and numbness in her right arm and 
both legs. After the second dose (9 days after initiation) she could no 
longer independently drive or walk and unfortunately, she had a fall 
leading to a fracture at the ankle. Her INCAT score was 6. She was taken 
off efgartigimod and restarted on IVIG. After two weeks she had not 
noticed improvement and prednisone was added and follow up was 
scheduled for four weeks but has not yet occurred.
3.Discussion
In summary, four CIDP patients who were transitioned from IVIG to 
FIT worsened precipitously. At the time of this report, the patients have 
not yet returned to baseline with reinstatement of IVIg therapy and in 
some cases the addition of prednisone. In CIDP patients who relapse, 
there is no guarantee that they will return to baseline and therefore a 
relapse is always of concern given that some patients may have wors-
ening of permanent disability as was seen in previous studies in CIDP 
[4].
Our cases here highlight a potential risk of switching patients from 
IVIG to FIT. In all four cases the patients had definite CIDP, and recent 
evidence of disease activity as evidenced by wearing off symptoms

between IVIG infusions and/or worsening with interruption of therapy. 
In addition, worsening of patients when IVIg was stopped provided 
retrospective evidence of active disease. The rapid worsening that 
coincided with initiation of FIT suggests a withdrawal effect from the 
IVIG given that FIT decreases serum IgG levels by blocking its salvage 
mechanism in the endothelial cells. Another concern is that the decline 
seen in these patients is faster and more severe than the wearing off or 
relapse seen in patients where IVIg is tapered in dose or frequency. 
Therefore, in addition to rapid serum IgG reduction, this also raises the 
possibility of other humoral factors or downstream cytokines that may 
be affected with FIT in patients with active CIDP.
In contrast to what we have observed in this small number of patients 
with CIDP, we are unaware of any reports, nor have we seen in our 
practices, patients with myasthenia gravis (MG) worsening when tran-
sitioned from IVIg to FIT. This suggests that the mechanism of action of 
efgartigimod in CIDP may be different than that for MG, or it could be 
related to the differing immunopathogenesis of these diseases. Further 
study of CIDP patients who experience dramatic worsening with FIT 
initiation with more detailed analysis of serum biomarkers, especially Ig 
and neurofilament levels, and nerve conduction studies may be benefi -
cial [5].
We recommend that once a decline is noticed after starting FIT, 
therapy should be discontinued, and rescue therapy should be instituted 
as early as possible. In general, some strategies that could be considered 
in switching real world CIDP patients from IVIg to efgartigimod, include 
bridging with corticosteroids until the positive effect of FCRn inhibition 
on the disease process can potentially negate the effects of IVIG with-
drawal or continue IVIg treatment during the initiation of efgartigimod 
therapy. There is a critical need to study this further.
4.Conclusion
In conclusion the transition of patients with CIDP who are stable on 
IVIG therapy to FIT can lead to severe worsening of disease. Further 
research to understand the immunopathogenesis of this worsening, may 
shed light as to the appropriate strategy for initiating this switch or may 
identify unique patient and disease characteristics that can predict this decline. Recognition of the potential for severe worsening during this 
switch is important to investigate strategies that may prevent severe 
relapse and irreversible disability.
