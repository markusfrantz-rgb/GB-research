BurstDR spinal cord stimulation for chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy

ABSTRACT
Background: Chronic Inflammatory Demyelinating Polyneuropathy (CIDP) is a neurological disorder in which the immune system attacks the myelin sheath, leading to progressive muscle weakness and sensory loss. Neuropathic pain is a common and often debilitating symptom in patients with CIDP. Treatment options include immunoglobulin therapy and, in refractory cases, spinal cord stimulation (SCS).Case Description: We present the case of a 70-year-old male with CIDP who experienced severe neuropathic pain in a stocking distribution affecting both lower extremities. He had previously been treated with intravenous immunoglobulin with limited relief of his pain symptoms. Due to persistent, debilitating neuropathic pain despite ongoing immunotherapy, he underwent implantation of a thoracic paddle spinal cord stimulator. At 3-month postoperative follow-up, he reported a 75% reduction in pain intensity.Conclusion: BurstDR paddle lead SCS may offer significant pain relief in patients with CIDP-associated neuropathic pain and should be considered as part of a multidisciplinary treatment.



## INTRODUCTION
Chronic Inflammatory Demyelinating Polyneuropathy (CIDP) is an immune-mediated disorder in which the immune system attacks the myelin sheath of peripheral nerves, leading to progressive muscle weakness and sensory loss. The estimated prevalence of CIDP ranges from 0.8 to 8.9 cases/100,000 people.[7] Common symptoms include muscle weakness, sensory deficits, and neuropathic pain, which affect approximately 36–57% of patients.[3,9] First-line treatment typically targets the underlying immune dysfunction and includes intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), corticosteroids, or plasmapheresis. While these therapies can be effective, they carry a risk of adverse events, reported in up to 3.9% of cases.[4,6] For patients with refractory neuropathic pain, spinal cord stimulation (SCS) has emerged as a viable adjunctive therapy. The analgesic mechanism of SCS is based on the gate control theory proposed by Wall and Melzack, which emphasizes that activation of large-diameter Aβ fibers in the dorsal columns inhibits nociceptive transmission at the level of the dorsal horn through activation of inhibitory interneurons.[8]
Here, we present the case of a 70-year-old male with CIDP and severe neuropathic pain who experienced significant symptom relief following implantation of a thoracic paddle lead spinal cord stimulator.

## CASE PRESENTATION
A 70-year-old man with a 20-year history of CIDP presented with progressive neuropathic pain. He had initially responded to first-line immunomodulatory therapies but later experienced diminishing benefit. Re-initiation of IVIG was attempted and later discontinued due to concerns related to his history of deep vein thrombosis and the associated risk of pulmonary embolism. Subsequent pharmacologic management included gabapentin (up to 1200 mg daily) and multiple opioid and non-opioid analgesics, all of which provided minimal symptomatic relief. Given the refractory nature of his pain, a burstDR SCS trial was performed, resulting in approximately 70% pain relief. ## DISCUSSION
CIDP is a rare autoimmune disorder of the peripheral nervous system, characterized by progressive motor and sensory deficits. First-line treatments focus on immune modulation, including corticosteroids (e.g., prednisone and dexamethasone) and plasma exchange.[2] In cases that are refractory to these therapies, second-line immunosuppressive agents such as azathioprine, methotrexate, cyclosporine, cyclophosphamide, rituximab, interferons, and biologics such as alemtuzumab and natalizumab may be considered.[2] Management of CIDP-related neuropathic pain typically includes tricyclic antidepressants, serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors, and calcium channel ligands such as gabapentin and pregabalin. In our case, the patient’s neuropathic pain remained refractory despite high-dose gabapentin and multiple opioid analgesics, prompting consideration of SCS as a next-line intervention. Mostofi et al. reported a case in which a patient with CIDP underwent bilateral cervical and lumbar SCS lead placement, resulting in a 50% reduction in average pain severity.[5] In contrast, our patient received a surgically implanted paddle lead SCS system through open laminotomy, which allows for more stable lead placement and broader stimulation coverage.
We specifically selected burstDR stimulation for this patient to target both the sensory-discriminative and affective-emotional components of pain.[1] BurstDR is a novel waveform that delivers intermittent high-frequency pulse trains designed to mimic natural neuronal burst firing, thereby modulating both the lateral and medial pain pathways.[1] In this case, burstDR stimulation resulted in substantial pain reduction, significant functional improvement, and discontinuation of multiple systemic therapies, including opioids, IVIG, and plasmapheresis.

## CONCLUSION
A 70-year-old man with treatment-resistant CIDP experienced a 75% reduction in pain 3 months after burstDR thoracic paddle spinal cord stimulator implantation.
