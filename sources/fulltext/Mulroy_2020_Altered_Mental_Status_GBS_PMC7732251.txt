Altered Mental Status in "Guillain-Barre Syndrome" -- A Noteworthy Clinical Clue

Authors: Eoin Mulroy, Neil E Anderson
Journal: Annals of Clinical and Translational Neurology
Year: 2020 (November 2), Volume 7, Issue 12, Pages 2489-2507
DOI: 10.1002/acn3.51226
PMID: 33136342
PMCID: PMC7732251

=== FULL TEXT ===

ABSTRACT

The authors challenge the conventional view of GBS as purely a peripheral nervous system disorder. They note that "central nervous system involvement, often manifesting as derangements in mental status can occur as a complication" of GBS, its variants, and related mimic conditions. The review provides clinicians with a systematic approach to evaluating cases where altered mental status accompanies GBS presentation.

INTRODUCTION

Guillain-Barre syndrome represents "the commonest cause of acute flaccid quadriparesis in the developed world." The authors observe that mental status changes frequently accompany GBS presentations: "central nervous system dysfunction is classically considered not to be a part of GBS. However, in our experience, it is not uncommon for alterations in mental status to be present."

FRAMEWORK: FOUR CENTRAL CLINICAL QUESTIONS

1. Could mental status changes be intrinsic to GBS itself?
2. Could treatment be responsible?
3. Could this represent a GBS variant?
4. Could this indicate a different diagnosis entirely?

SECTION 1: MENTAL STATUS CHANGES AS PART OF GBS

Intrinsic Neuropsychiatric Manifestations:
- Almost one third of GBS patients have mental status abnormalities
- Manifestations include "hypnagogic or hypnopompic vivid dreams," visual and tactile hallucinations, and delusions
- Symptoms typically peak around one week after disease onset

Metabolic Disturbances:
- SIADH affects approximately 50% of GBS patients
- CO2 narcosis from hypoventilation (25% require ventilation)
- Symptomatic hyponatremia produces altered mental status, seizures, and occasionally coma

Autonomic Dysfunction:
- Present in at least two thirds of cases
- PRES: "headache, nausea, vomiting, visual changes, seizures, encephalopathy, and focal neurologic deficits alongside radiologic evidence of vasogenic edema predominating in posterior brain regions"
- Mechanism: "rapidly developing hypertension with failure of cerebral autoregulation"
- Most cases of GBS-related PRES have occurred in women aged > 55 years

SECTION 2: TREATMENT-RELATED COMPLICATIONS

IVIg Adverse Effects:
- Thrombotic events (1-16% incidence) within 24 hours of administration
- Aseptic meningitis (0.5-1%) generally within 48 hours of infusion start
- PRES through hemodynamic mechanisms
- CSF shows neutrophilic pleocytosis, elevated protein, high opening pressure

Analgesic and Sedative Medications:
- Pregabalin, gabapentin may alter mental status
- Sedative withdrawal in ventilated patients

SECTION 3: GBS VARIANTS -- ANTI-GQ1B DISEASE

The anti-GQ1b antibody spectrum:
- Miller-Fisher Syndrome: ataxia, areflexia, ophthalmoplegia
- Bickerstaff's Brainstem Encephalitis: external ophthalmoplegia AND either altered consciousness or hyperreflexia
- Up to 50% of BBE patients have coexisting GBS
- 25-30% display nonspecific MRI abnormalities: T2 hyperintensities in brainstem, thalamus, cerebellum, or cerebral white matter

SECTION 4: GBS MIMICS -- THE MIND CLASSIFICATION

M: Metabolic + Malignancy (porphyria, thyrotoxicosis, leptomeningeal metastases, paraneoplastic)
I: Infectious/Immune/Inherited (enteroviruses, flaviviruses, rabies, HIV, CMV, VZV, Lyme, vasculitides, autoimmune encephalitides, CMT 1X)
N: Nutritional (thiamine deficiency/Wernicke's)
D: Drugs/toxins (arsenic, thallium, glycol, iatrogenic)

KEY CLINICAL PEARLS

- PRES is an important but underrecognized complication of GBS
- Altered mental status in GBS should NOT be assumed to be part of the peripheral disease
- SIADH is very common in GBS (50%) and can cause altered consciousness
- Anti-GQ1b spectrum (BBE) should be considered when GBS presents with altered consciousness
- A structured approach (MIND framework) helps distinguish GBS from mimics
- Many GBS + CNS disorders are treatable, with outcomes dependent on early recognition
- IVIg-induced aseptic meningitis occurs in 0.5-1% of patients
- Thrombotic complications of IVIg (1-16%) can cause CNS symptoms

CONCLUSION

"While GBS itself can occasionally produce mental status alterations, this should remain a diagnosis of exclusion. Significant depression in level of consciousness, seizures, movement disorders as well as immunosuppression and at-risk travel histories should prompt exhaustive searches for alternative explanations."

"Many GBS + CNS disorders are treatable, with patient outcomes being critically dependent on early recognition. A structured approach such as that presented here is therefore paramount."
