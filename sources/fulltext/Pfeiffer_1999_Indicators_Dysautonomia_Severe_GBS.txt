Indicators of dysautonomia
in severe Guillain-Barré syndrome

Abstract This study sought to establish quantitative criteria for dysautonomia in artificially ventilated
patients with Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS). Such criteria would
help to identify patients at risk for
cardiovascular complications. This
retrospective controlled clinical study
compared hourly cardiovascular
monitoring data from 36 successive,
artificially ventilated GBS patients
with that from 11 artificially ventilated control patients with myasthenia. Tolerance limits for daily means,
extremes, and variations in heart rate
(HR) and blood pressure (BP) were
estimated from the most abnormal
subgroups of the treatment days of
our control patients. These exceeded
previously suggested arbitrary cutoff
values for dysautonomia. The range in
systolic BP was increased in 27 GBS

Introduction
Dysautonomia is an important cause of death in severe
Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS) [15, 25, 28]. The risk of
dysautonomia is high in patients with tetraplegia, respiratory failure, or bulbar involvement [19]. Frequent manifestations are sinus tachycardia, hypertension, and labile
blood pressure (BP). One-fifth of GBS patients [25] have
a heart rate above 100 bpm. This rate rises to 75% in patients with respiratory failure [12]. Hypertension developed in 13.5% of the control patients in the American
plasma exchange trial [11]. BP increased by more than 25

the value suggested in previous work.

above 165 mm Hg also had persistent
tachycardia (mean HR > 125 bpm),
or were treated with β-blockers. This
pattern of sympathetic hyperactivity

in 6 patients. Hypotension (minimal

asystole on his first day on the ICU,
all episodes of bradycardia were preceded by increased daily systolic BP
variation (> 85 mm Hg), which thus
proved to be a sensitive and prognostically valuable indicator of dysautonomia in GBS.

The vagal nerve mediates reflex bradycardia. Fixed
HR, the hallmark of vagal failure, should therefore reduce

the risk of cardiac arrest, at least until vagal function recovers. As this event is hard to predict, GBS patients who
have had dysautonomia should be considered at risk until
all cardiovascular reflexes recover. This requires that
dysautonomia be detected reliably, either by autonomic
nervous system testing such as analysis of HR variability
[25], eyeball pressure [6, 8], carotid pressure [17], atropine administration [22], modified Valsalva manoeuver
[14], and tilt-table tests [4, 22–24], or by attention to abnormal cardiovascular monitoring parameters. Several tests
cannot be used during artificial ventilation. Artificial ventilation may superficially resemble the deep breathing test,
but it cancels respiratory drive, which is an important
source of HR variation during voluntary breathing [18].
Artificial ventilation also interferes with the Valsalva maneuver. Other tests must be adapted because of medication and lack of a relaxed atmosphere in the ICU. Most
tests can be used only discontinuously and may therefore
miss short episodes of dysautonomia. Observation gaps
do not arise if dysautonomia is monitored using cardiovascular data. High diurnal HR and BP variation have
been shown to identify patients at risk for arrhythmia in
GBS [25]. Unfortunately, the “normal” limits of HR and
BP variation in ICU patients are unknown. Various arbitrarily defined limits have been used. For instance, BP differences of more than 40 mm Hg within a single day defined “labile BP” in the French plasmapheresis trial [9]
and in the study by Winer and Hughes [25], while the
limit for “BP fluctuations” was “more than 50 mm Hg
from mean baseline” in another study [26]. We calculated
“normal” limits for various HR and systolic BP monitoring parameters from artificially ventilated patients with
myasthenia, whose ICU experience is comparable to that
of GBS patients. These were used to study prevalence and
pattern [15] of dysautonomia in 36 successive artificially
ventilated GBS patients.

Patients and methods
Between 1985 and 1994, 37 GBS patients were artificially ventilated on our neurological intensive care unit (Table 3; for diagnostic criteria see [3]). The file of one patient who died 2 days after
transfer to the normal ward was unobtainable. During the same period 14 patients with myasthenia were ventilated because of myasthenic crises. Three patients with preexisting atrial fibrillation
were excluded. The study was approved by the local ethics committee. We had data on 11 patients from 125 treatment days with
myasthenia and from 955 treatment days of 36 patients with GBS.
Treatment was categorized as follows:
– Principal mode of ventilation (Bennet MA1 until 1987; Bennet
7200 afterwards, Bennet Respiration Products, Santa Monica,
Calif. USA); controlled mandatory ventilation (CMV), synchronized intermittent mandatory ventilation or assisted ventilation
(SIMV), or continuous positive airways pressure ventilation
pressure support (CPAP)
– Sedation and analgesia: continuous fentanyl/midazolam infusion, or intermittent pyritramide, pethidine, or diazepam
– Neuroleptics: 75–150 mg melperone per day intravenously or
orally

– Catecholamines: continuous intravenous, single doses of etilefrine or theodrenaline, or intravenous dopamine hydrochloride
(2 µg/kg per minute)
– β-Blockers: metoprolol or propranolol intravenously or orally
– Other antihypertensives: continuous intravenous or nifedipine,
or intermittent nifedipine or clonidine
– pyridostigmine
– diuretics
Patients with myasthenia and GBS differed neither by age nor by
daily maximal body temperature (Mann-Whitney test; P > 0.05).
CMV was used more frequently in GBS (27% versus 5% of the
days). Patients on CMV received continuous intravenous analgesia
and sedation on 71% of the days. Sedation was used more frequently during SIMV in GBS (10.8 versus 1.5% of days). Neuroleptics were given on three-fourths of days in myasthenia and
GBS. GBS patients were treated more frequently with continuous
intravenous norepinephrine (11.8% versus 6.4% of days), β-blockers (21% versus 4% of days), and other antihypertensive medicaments (25.1% versus 0.8% of days). Diuretics were given more
frequently in myasthenia (40% versus 27% of days).
BP was measured intra-arterially or oscillometrically (Hellige
Servomed; Freiburg, Germany) at least twice hourly. From notes
taken at least every 2 h we calculated daily mean, standard deviation, maximum, and minimum of HR and systolic BP. These daily
HR and BP parameters were compared between GBS and myasthenia by Mann-Whitney tests (significance level: P < 0.05). The
effect of ventilation, sedation and analgesia, neuroleptics, catecholamines, β-blockers, diuretics, other antihypertensives, pyridostigmine, and fever above 38 °C on the various daily HR and BP parameters was assessed separately in GBS and myasthenia patients
(Mann-Whitney tests; significance level: P < 0.05). If there was a
significant effect in the control group, we calculated separate limits of normal (Table 1) so that they contained 95% of the control
values with an error probability of 5%. The strictest tolerance limits were used as cutoff values for dysautonomia. Increased daily
mean systolic BP defined persistent hypertension, decreased minimal BP defined hypotension, excessive daily BP variation defined
volatile BP, increased daily mean HR defined persistent tachycardia, and decreased minimal HR defined bradycardia. Prevalence of
these cardiovascular events was compared between GBS and
myasthenia with χ2 tests (significance level: P < 0.05; Table 2).
Patient files were examined for descriptions of cardiovascular
events and emergency medications.

Outcome
There was no death during intensive care. Patient 6 stayed
in a locked-in syndrome for months during rehabilitation
until his death. Patient 24 died 4 months after ICU care;
she had multiple intercurrent diseases. Patient 30 unexpectedly died 4 days after transfer to a normal ward. One
patient, whose files were unobtainable, died during the
second night on a normal ward.

Discussion
Our data confirm that volatile BP is common among artificially ventilated GBS patients and heralds dangerous
bradycardia [25]. Previously proposed quantitative criteria [9, 25], however, were not strict enough for ICU pa-

tients. They were positive in all but one of our control patients. Despite being more than twice as high as proposed,
a limit of 85 mm Hg for daily BP range still provided the
most sensitive indicator of dysautonomia. Volatile BP was
present in two-thirds of the GBS patients. Increased daily
BP standard deviation, previously as used an alternative
to daily BP range [18], was less sensitive and more difficult to calculate. Volatile BP is not only a sensitive indicator of dysautonomia but also a risk factor for cardiac arrest [25]. Patients who had volatile BP on the ICU should
remain under prolonged cardiovascular monitoring to prevent death early after transfer to normal wards, which
happened to two of our patients during the study period.
Daily pulse changes by more than 30 bpm predicted
significant arrhythmia independently of labile BP in a previous study [25]. Again, this limit was unspecific in our
ICU patients. They required a tolerance limit as high as 75
bpm, which was only rarely exceeded in GBS and therefore not useful for detection of dysautonomia. In contrast
to previous studies [12, 21], persistent hypertension was
more frequent than tachycardia in our patients. Even without the four patients who never had increased mean systolic BP but received maximal antihypertensive therapy,
its prevalence was second only to that of volatile BP. Hypertension obviously was hard to treat: maximal therapy
failed to lower mean daily mean systolic BP below 165
mm Hg on three-fourths of the treatment days. This therapy resistance may explain why we observed persistent
hypertension more frequently than persistent tachycardia,
which others [21] found to be the most frequent manifestation of GBS dysautonomia. Previous studies used a cutoff value of 100 bpm, probably for maximal HR [12, 25].
In our ICU patients this limit was not specific even for
mean HR. A 25% higher limit was exceeded by 25% of
our patients. A second one-fourth of our patients were under β-blockade and had mean HR values below this limit.
We observed fewer instances of supraventricular and
ventricular tachyarrhythmias than reported in a previous
study conducted by specifically trained nurses and physicians [10]. Some events may have been misinterpreted as
artifacts or left undocumented. As it is harder to miss
bradycardias during cardiovascular monitoring, the rate of
bradycardia compared favorably with that in the specific
prospective study [10]. It remains possible that we missed
some cardiovascular events because of the retrospective
design of our study. The retrospective design less affected
our monitoring data because their documentation followed common practice of ICU chart keeping and remained unchanged during the study period. They were
valid enough to detect the expected effects of fever, neuroleptics, diuretics, artificial ventilation, and analgesia
and sedation.
We were not fully successful in matching our ICU controls. Although artificial ventilation causes similar stress
and cardiovascular impact to similar weak and conscious
patients in GBS and myasthenia, the two groups were

1021

treated differently in respect to analgesia and sedation.
Benzodiazepines and opiates were used only exceptionally in myasthenia because they may accentuate myasthenic
weakness. CMV also was applied only rarely in myasthenia. Uncertainty about the effect of CMV and continuous
analgesia and sedation affected the final results only marginally, as only three patients were under their influence
on all days with increased BP variation.
It is questionable whether a prospective design would
have made therapy more consistent without exclusion of
an undue number of treatment days. It is impossible or unethical to withhold sedation and analgesia during intervening bronchopneumonia, pain, or untolerable psychological discomfort. There are reasons to leave hypertension and tachycardia untreated; they are only transient, and
β-blockade and antihypertensive treatment may aggravate
incipient bradycardia or hypotension. Older patients with
coronary heart disease, however, must be treated. Our limits would have narrowed if we had excluded all days with
confounding therapeutic interventions, although at the cost
of general applicability. The strict limits had the advantage that they distinguished well between patients with
and without dysautonomia. All patients would have qualified for dysautonomia by previously used limits. Our rate
of 75% agrees with clinical experience. Meaningful patterns of autonomic change [15] also emerged only by use
of the more strict criteria. HR and BP abnormalities indi-

cating hyperactivity of the autonomic nervous system prevailed, which is remarkable for a disease that otherwise
causes loss of function. Tachycardia was due to sympathetic hyperactivity rather than to parasympathetic hypoactivity in the patients who also had raised BP (nos.
1–13 and 28–30). Vagal failure obviously was not the
cause of tachycardia in the patients whose vagal function
became evident through reflex bradycardias (nos. 1, 2, 7,
8). Most bradycardias were probably caused by parasympathetic hyperactivity, which may result from afferent
baroreceptor reflex failure. This causes labile BP [20] and
releases the sympathetic efference [7, 20]. The ensuing
catecholamine excess sensitizes left ventricular stretch receptors [1, 2, 13] and other nociceptors which cause reflex
bradycardia if vagal function is intact. This would explain
the frequent association of bradycardia and labile BP.
Sympathetic hypoactivity may have been the cause of the
bradycardias that were associated with persistently low
BP (nos. 16, 17). This classification remains speculative
without comprehensive autonomic nervous function tests.
Our results help to target them on patients with “real”
dysautonomia. These patients are at risk of fatal bradycardia, especially if tests for exaggerated reflex bradycardia
such as the eyeball pressure test [8] are positive. Wide BP
swings and episodes of bradycardia should prompt extended cardiovascular monitoring.
