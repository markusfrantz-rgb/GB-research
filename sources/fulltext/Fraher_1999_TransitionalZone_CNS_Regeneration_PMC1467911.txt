The transitional zone and CNS regeneration

Author: JP Fraher
Journal: J Anat. 1999 Feb;194(Pt 2):161-182
DOI: 10.1046/j.1469-7580.1999.19420161.x
PMID: 10337950 | PMCID: PMC1467911

OVERVIEW

Comprehensive review of the CNS-PNS transitional zone (TZ) structure, development,
and its role as a barrier to CNS regeneration. The TZ is the site where central
myelin (oligodendrocytes) transitions to peripheral myelin (Schwann cells) at
cranial nerve and spinal nerve root entry/exit zones.

STRUCTURE OF THE CNS-PNS TRANSITIONAL ZONE

The transitional zone comprises the rootlet segment containing both central and
peripheral nervous tissue. Key structural features:

- Clear interface: The 2 tissues are separated by a very irregular but clearly
  defined interface, consisting of the surface of the astrocytic tissue
- Axon-only penetration: The interface is penetrated only by axons
- Glial composition: Oligodendrocytes produce myelin centrally; Schwann cells
  produce myelin peripherally
- Composite nodes: Transitional nodes have features that are a composite of
  central and peripheral type nodes of Ranvier

ANATOMY OF THE TRANSITION

Central side:
- Myelin formed by oligodendrocytes
- Supporting tissue is astrocytic
- Axons embedded in CNS-type neuropil

Peripheral side:
- Myelin formed by Schwann cells
- Each Schwann cell ensheathed in endoneurium
- Connective tissue framework present

Interface:
- Dome-shaped, directed toward the periphery
- Formed by astrocytic foot processes (glia limitans)
- Located more peripherally in sensory nerves than motor nerves
- Extent varies: from <1 mm to several mm depending on nerve and individual

TRANSITIONAL NODES OF RANVIER

At the exact junction point, there exist "transitional nodes" where one side of
the node is formed by oligodendrocyte myelin and the other by Schwann cell myelin.
These are structurally unique composite structures.

DEVELOPMENTAL FORMATION

The interface develops postnatally through active glial processes: It is formed
by growth of processes into the axon bundle from glial cell bodies around its
perimeter. These form a barrier across the bundle which fully segregates
prospectively myelinated axons.

PATHOLOGICAL VULNERABILITY

The TZ is clinically significant because:

1. Neurovascular compression syndromes: Vascular compression at the TZ can cause
   trigeminal neuralgia, hemifacial spasm, glossopharyngeal neuralgia

2. Demyelination vulnerability: The transition between two myelin types creates
   a structurally vulnerable zone

3. Barrier to regeneration: Following CNS injury, the TZ forms a gliotic barrier
   that prevents axonal regeneration from PNS into CNS

Following axon injury:
- The mature CNS component undergoes gliosis
- The gliotic TZ tissue expands distally along the root
- Regenerating axons cease growth on reaching the gliotic tissue
- This is a major barrier to CNS regeneration

RELEVANCE TO GBS AND INFLAMMATORY DEMYELINATION

The TZ represents a structurally unique and potentially vulnerable zone where:
1. Inflammatory processes in the PNS (GBS) can extend toward CNS territory
2. The lack of blood-nerve barrier protection in subarachnoid nerve segments
   allows immune cells access to both PNS and CNS myelin at this junction
3. Complement and antibody-mediated attack at the TZ could affect both
   oligodendrocyte and Schwann cell myelin

EXPERIMENTAL APPROACHES

1. Olfactory ensheathing cells (OECs): facilitate axon regeneration into cord
2. Molecular factors: tropic/trophic factors to alter TZ environment
3. Ventral motoneurons: may undergo some unaided CNS regeneration

The dorsal root TZ serves as an excellent experimental model because the tissue
is anisometric - individual parameters such as axon numbers, axon size and glial
ensheathment can be readily measured.
