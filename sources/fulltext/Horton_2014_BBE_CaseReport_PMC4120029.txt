Bickerstaff's encephalitis

Authors: Emma Horton, Sanjay Krishnamoorthy, Lucy Reynolds
Journal: BMJ Case Reports
Year: 2014
PMID: 25080547
PMCID: PMC4120029
DOI: 10.1136/bcr-2014-205336

DIAGNOSTIC CRITERIA FOR BBE (Odaka et al.)

Clinical criteria:
- Ophthalmoplegia and ataxia with disturbed consciousness and/or pyramidal signs
- Distinguishes BBE from MFS (ophthalmoplegia, ataxia, areflexia) and GBS (acute symmetrical limb weakness, areflexia)

CASE PRESENTATION

36-year-old male:
- Bilateral ophthalmoplegia with limitation of eye movements
- Ataxia of upper and lower limbs
- Impaired consciousness (GCS oscillating 13-15/15)
- Pseudobulbar affect (emotional lability)
- Slurred speech
- Upgoing plantar reflexes (Babinski positive)
- Fever 38C
- Preceding diarrheal illness (Campylobacter jejuni)

MRI FINDINGS

No abnormalities on T2 and FLAIR imaging in brain or upper spinal cord.
(Note: MRI is normal in ~70% of BBE cases)

LABORATORY FINDINGS

- LP: Raised RBC (4000/mm3), normal protein, WCC, glucose, lactate
- Negative viral PCR panel
- Negative antiganglioside antibodies (anti-GQ1b)
- Positive Campylobacter IgG serology with equivocal IgA

TREATMENT

IVIG plus plasmapheresis - complete recovery within 3 weeks with no neurological sequelae.

KEY TEACHING POINTS

1. BBE is a clinical diagnosis - negative anti-GQ1b and normal MRI do not exclude it
2. Approximately 30% have abnormal brain MRI
3. 66% have positive anti-GQ1b antibodies
4. BBE, MFS, and GBS form a continuous spectrum
5. Treatment: IVIG and/or plasmapheresis
