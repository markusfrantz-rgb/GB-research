Unresolved issues with efgartigimod in CIDP, and broader concerns for future trial design
The U.S. Food and Drug Administration ’s (FDA) approval of efgar -
tigimod, an FcRn antagonist, for the treatment of chronic inflammatory 
demyelinating polyneuropathy (CIDP) was based on the positive results 
of the ADHERE trial, which demonstrated that efgartigimod signifi -
cantly outperformed placebo in a double-blind withdrawal study [1]. 
However, a recent case series by Levine and Muley reports a concerning 
adverse outcome not highlighted in the trial [2]. This new finding un-
derscores the need for heightened safety vigilance and raises important 
questions about both the trial design and the broader implications for 
clinical research in CIDP.
In the case series, four out of nine patients, all treated post-approval, 
experienced severe disease worsening after transitioning from intrave -
nous immunoglobulin (IVIg) to efgartigimod. INCAT scores increased by 
2 to 5 points within two weeks of initiating treatment. Two patients 
required hospitalization, and none had returned to baseline despite 
receiving rescue therapy at the time of reporting. The final dose of IVIg 
occurred 12 to 42 days before the initiation of efgartigimod therapy.
The timeline aligns with a high dropout rate among patients who 
stopped immunoglobulin therapy in the ADHERE trial. The protocol 
required a run-in phase, during which patients stopped prior treatment 
and were monitored for disease worsening over 12 weeks. Over 90 % of 
cases worsened, allowing progression to stage A, where subjects started 
open-label efgartigimod. Only patients who improved after four doses 
advanced to a randomized double-blind withdrawal (stage B), where 
they continued efgartigimod or switched to placebo.
By the end of stage A, 53 of 165 subjects who had been on immu -
noglobulin therapy (32 %) dropped out, some within weeks of starting 
treatment. In contrast, patients on prior corticosteroids or no prior 
therapy had lower dropout rates (13 % and 9 %, respectively).
It is plausible that some ADHERE participants experienced severe 
deterioration, similar to patients in the Levine and Muley case series, but 
the trial data does not provide clarity. It remains difficult to determine 
whether these 4 patients represent an isolated event or indicate broader 
underreporting. Investigators did not design the ADHERE to capture 
outcomes for patients after they dropped out, leaving unanswered 
questions about responses to rescue interventions, the severity of 
worsening compared to typical IVIg relapses, or the occurrence of 
serious outcomes requiring hospitalization. While the adverse event 
results note that 17 patients (5 % of the total study) experienced “CIDP 
worsening ” during stage A, we do not know what prior treatments these 
patients were taking. If all 17 had been on immunoglobulin, it would 
suggest approximately 10 % of the subgroup worsened (17/165). 
However, this calculation does not fully explain the high dropout rate.
The ADHERE report also lists reasons for patient withdrawals but 
groups them into broad categories such as “participant withdrawal, ” “insufficient efficacy, ” and “adverse events, ” limiting interpretability. 
This lack of detail creates gaps in understanding the risk that newly 
treated patients might face. While the report acknowledges the high 
dropout rate among immunoglobulin-treated patients and the need to 
find the “optimal approach ” to transition therapy, the authors downplay 
concerns, adding that dropouts might have responded differently if they 
had completed their dosing schedules.
CIDP criteria have come full circle
Since the Ad Hoc AAN subcommittee first published CIDP research 
criteria in 1991, guidelines have evolved significantly [3]. Stated pur-
poses of their work were to “engender ” discussion about CIDP, to 
recognize the complexity of the condition, and to prioritize specificity 
over sensitivity in the context of clinical research. They defined disease 
attributes that were not meant to encompass all cases. However, these 
criteria eventually proved too restrictive for clinical practice, leading to 
the EFNS/PNS guidelines in 2005, which aimed to improve sensitivity 
and to make it easier for practitioners to treat patients [4]. Ironically, the 
ADHERE trial may have overlooked these foundational concepts by 
using, as the title implies, “management guidelines ” for research 
participant selection, potentially leading to over-diagnosis and affecting 
the study ’s rigor.
Estimates of misdiagnosis rates for CIDP in the community are 
problematic, ranging from 50 % [5] to near 70 % [6] depending on 
definitions. To reduce misdiagnosis, ADHERE researchers relied on a 
committee of independent expert adjudicators and used the updated 
2010 EFNS/PNS guidelines as study criteria [7]. Two adjudicators 
reviewed each case, and inclusion required two ‘yes’ votes. If adjudi -
cators disagreed, a committee chairperson acted as the tiebreaker.
Poor concordance raises questions about diagnosis
The novel study design employed by the ADHERE investigators 
addressed limitations in past CIDP trials, which lacked enrichment 
strategies and adjudication procedures. Some trials faltered due to a 
combination of patients without active disease and the confounding 
effects of prior treatments [8]. Selecting the appropriately diagnosed 
patients is critical to focus on desired effects and minimize “noisy ” 
outcomes from misdiagnosis or disease remission.
Two early abstracts, after screening 100 and then 200 cases, reported 
a discordance rate of about 50 % between the first two adjudicators [9]. 
This high discordance rate is a red flag, suggesting potential random 
guessing or significant differences in how adjudicators interpreted 
ambiguous cases. Statistically, the inter-rater reliability for the case

The adjudication challenges go beyond the decision to use guidelines 
meant for clinical practice. ADHERE tasked adjudicators with evaluating 
documentation from multiple countries, some based on clinic notes not 
created directly for trial enrollment. Like judges reviewing circum -
stantial evidence, adjudicators had to interpret case material, including 
some borderline findings—such as two isolated conduction blocks with 
no other abnormalities—that might meet EFNS/PNS guidelines but not 
the 1991 research criteria. In such an instance, it is hard to know how 
any given adjudicator would decide a confusing case. Research on 
decision-making suggests evaluators bring personal biases to similar 
processes, influenced by factors such as strictness with rules, responses 
to grammatical context, or even mood at the time of review [10]. Given 
the ambiguity in both the guidelines and the data, it is unsurprising that 
agreement among adjudicators was somewhat random. Notably, two 
adjudicators could enroll a subject simply by guessing the same way on a 
hard case.
Concern over placebo responses and incentives to respond to 
treatment
In a systematic review of earlier CIDP trials, Lewis and colleagues 
concluded that “placebo is an important variable in the design of CIDP 
trials” [11]. However, the paper never explicitly stated that misdiag -
nosis is a major contributor to placebo responses. ADHERE, as a with-
drawal study, was prone to such effects. Beyond adjudication, other 
observations raise concern about potential misdiagnosis. For example, a 
high percentage of patients in the placebo group in stage B never 
relapsed, despite rapid relapses during the lead-in phase. While the 
ADHERE report mentions potential carryover effects of efgartigimod, 
similar phenomena are known in other CIDP trials [8,11]. Additionally, 
subjects who had previously received immunoglobulin responded weeks 
faster to efgartigimod in stage A than those on prednisone or no prior 
therapy, suggesting that learned expectations, rather than immune ef-
fects alone, may have influenced the observed responses. Prior immu -
noglobulin patients who relapsed in stage B also did so more rapidly 
than those in other cohorts.
In clinical practice, patients misdiagnosed with CIDP often report 
positive responses to treatments [5]. CIDP specialists sometimes 
encounter misdiagnosed patients who have remained on treatment for 
years. These individuals, essentially proven placebo responders, pose 
unique challenges for clinical trialists, especially when incentivized to 
show positive treatment responses.
Using open-label extensions (OLE) in clinical trials can attract par-
ticipants who hope to transition to a new treatment but may also create 
incentives to respond to therapy. For example, it is hard to simply accept 
that 90 % of ADHERE patients relapsed during the run-in phase, 
compared to just 40 % in other trials when prior treatments were dis-
continued [8]. This is too large a difference to ignore a potentiral effect 
of incentives, in addition to blinding and patient selection. In addition, 
the OLE design may have inflated the observed response rate, as mis-
diagnosed participants also had to show clinical improvement during 
stage A to gain entry to the OLE. This potentially introduced a well- 
known response bias [12]. The open-label design of stage A, without a 
placebo control, removed a key safeguard against placebo responses. 
Combined with the potentially liberal criteria used in the adjudication 
process, some findings in the trial likely reflect placebo responses rather 
than efgartigimod’s efficacy in CIDP.
Furthermore, by the end of stage A, the design could have inadver -
tently enriched stage B with misdiagnosed patients motivated to show 
improvement. This contrasts with correctly diagnosed patients, who 
may have experienced worsening or severe relapses and dropped out. The outcome measures used in ADHERE—such as I-RODS, INCAT, and 
grip strength—are subjective and prone to participant effort, making 
them insufficient to address this potential confounding factor.
It follows that throughout stage B, the risk of unblinding required an 
additional guardrail, especially considering misdiagnosed subjects 
prone to placebo responses. In a typical head-to-head treatment trial, 
blinding helps mitigate this issue, but in ADHERE, one arm transitioned 
directly from active treatment to placebo, while the other did not. If 
participants sensed a change from the known therapy to a placebo, their 
expectations could influence their responses—particularly with a drug 
that causes injection site irritation. Any sense of unblinding could skew 
relapse rates, with the placebo group experiencing higher rates and the 
treatment group lower rates. The ADHERE report does not address 
unblinding, nor does it mention whether they queried participants about 
their assumptions regarding treatment assignment.
Final thoughts and the opportunity in the existing data
The report by Levine and Muley highlights critical safety concerns 
about transitioning patients from immunoglobulin therapy to efgarti -
gimod, potentially shedding light on the dropout patterns in the 
ADHERE trial. We cannot conclude efgartigimod is ineffective or 
dangerous for some patients, but these insights call for broader discus -
sion on study designs and require a reevaluation of the trial’s findings.
First, an intensive and transparent phase 4 safety monitoring pro-
gram is crucial, with systematic reporting of severe relapses. If the 
incidence of deterioration proves high, a warning should be issued to 
inform patients and clinicians of the risks. Reassessing the outcomes of 
patients who dropped out of the ADHERE trial and required rescue 
therapy could also provide valuable clarity. Second, while the reported 
trial results seem encouraging, further data and detailed case reporting 
are still needed to confirm long-term safety and efficacy, particularly for 
newly diagnosed or untreated patients, where documenting improve -
ment is more straightforward than for patents transitioning treatments 
who remain stable. Third, a comprehensive sensitivity analysis of the 
ADHERE data is necessary to explore critical likelihoods that the study 
did not fully account for, such as misdiagnosis rates, placebo effects, 
response biases in open-label stages, unaccounted dropouts, and 
unblinding risks. This analysis could help exclude a gross overestimation 
of the drug’s safety and efficacy. Finally, the variability in adjudicated 
diagnoses offers an opportunity to refine CIDP diagnostic criteria and 
address methodological flaws that have led to noisy outcomes in several 
CIDP trials. A post-hoc analysis examining recognizable subsets—like 
dropouts needing rescue therapy, placebo non-relapsers, or newly 
diagnosed responders—who had vastly different outcomes despite 
following the same protocol, is needed to improve diagnostic precision 
and strengthen confidence in efgartigimod’s role in CIDP. Addressing 
these issues will only ensure that both patients and clinicians benefit 
from a clearer, more accurate understanding of promising therapies.
