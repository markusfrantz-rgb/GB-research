TITLE: Effectiveness of Radiotherapy in Preventing Heterotopic Ossification: A Meta-analysis of Randomized Controlled Trials Beyond Total Hip Arthroplasty
ABSTRACT: Heterotopic ossification (HO) frequently occurs after procedures such as hip replacements, acetabular fractures, and elbow fractures. While radiotherapy (RT) has been a longstanding preventive measure against HO, its efficacy remains controversial, with some studies questioning its effectiveness and noting potential side effects. Previous research has predominantly focused on total hip arthroplasty (THA), leading to a gap in comprehensive evaluation of RT’s efficacy and safety across other conditions. This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted strictly adhering to PRISMA guidelines, utilizing the PICO framework to critically assess randomized clinical trials. A thorough literature search was executed across PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science). Methodological integrity was ensured through structured data extraction and quality assessment using validated criteria. The protocol was prospectively registered with INPLASY, ensuring full methodological transparency. A total of seven studies with 933 patients were included in the analysis, comprising 594 patients receiving RT and 339 patients in the control condition. Subgroup analyses included 784 patients undergoing THA and 149 patients receiving other surgical interventions. Overall findings supported RT’s efficacy in significantly reducing severe HO formation. Notably, subgroup analysis of patients who underwent non-THA procedures indicated no statistically meaningful reduction in the incidence of mild-to-moderate HO (Brooker I–II) after RT treatment compared to controls. Conversely, an increased risk of nonunion was specifically noted among patients receiving elbow RT. Our meta-analysis strongly supports the preventive effect of RT on HO, especially for high-risk patients and in cases that other treatments, like NSAIDs, are contraindicated or avoided. However, the risk of nonunion in elbow treatments warrants caution. Further prospective randomized controlled trials, particularly focusing on non-hip joints, are necessary to validate these findings.

FULLTEXT:

## Introduction
Heterotopic ossification (HO) involves the formation of mature bone tissue in non-bone tissues, commonly occurring after trauma around the hip joint and during joint replacement surgeries [1–3]. HO can lead to pain, restricted movement, and diminished quality of life. One study reported an incidence exceeding 40% following hip replacement surgeries [4]. Without preventive measures, the incidence of HO following acetabular fracture surgeries was 34.9% [5]. HO is also a prevalent factor impairing joint function recovery in patients with elbow and spinal cord injuries, with a reported incidence of 28.7% after surgical repair of various elbow fractures [6, 7]. Radiotherapy (RT) and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) are primary clinical approaches to prevent HO [2]. RT has been validated by numerous studies demonstrating its effectiveness in reducing HO incidence after total hip arthroplasty (THA) [8–10]. Kushima et al. indicated that RT lowers the expression of proinflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α, thereby mitigating the local inflammatory response and inhibiting bone formation [11]. Cantrell et al. performed a systematic review and confirmed the significant role of RT in preventing HO post-acetabular fracture surgeries [2]. A retrospective study involving 287 patients indicated a lower incidence of HO in those treated with prophylactic RT for acetabular fractures [12]. Another retrospective study with 229 patients attested to the safety and efficacy of RT in preventing HO at the elbow [13]. However, when applied to injuries at other sites, the results have been inconsistent. Hamid et al. observed that in elbow fractures, RT did not decrease HO occurrence but increased nonunion rates [14]. Honore et al. reported that local RT failed to prevent recurrence of HO in patients with spinal cord and traumatic brain injuries [6]. Similarly, a recent systematic review revealed no significant preventive advantage of RT compared with non-RT controls [15]. However, the latter review's control group combined individuals receiving NSAIDs with untreated subjects, potentially confounding its results. While the majority of meta-analyses have predominantly examined HO after THA, we suggest that evaluating RT's prophylactic effect solely based on surgical procedures or operative sites has inherent limitations, since other anatomical regions experience distinct trauma characteristics and possess varying degrees of soft tissue protection. Therefore, significant controversy persists regarding the necessity and efficacy of RT as prophylaxis against HO, especially outside the hip region. The present systematic review and meta-analysis aim to comprehensively examine the clinical effectiveness and adverse outcomes associated with prophylactic RT by analyzing published prospective randomized controlled trials (RCTs), including recent data from studies addressing acetabular fracture management. To avoid drug-related confounding, our control group exclusively involved subjects who received no preventive pharmacologic treatments for HO.

## Materials and Methods

## Data Sources and Search Strategy
This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with PRISMA guidelines [10] and the PICO framework for evaluating randomized controlled trials (RCTs). Our comprehensive search strategy integrated controlled vocabulary (MeSH terms including “Radiotherapy,” “Ossification, Heterotopic,” and “Randomized Controlled Trials as Topic”) with free-text keywords. Searches were executed across four major biomedical databases: PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science. This was done through iterative query optimization assisted by a medical librarian. The initial searches were conducted on January 24, 2025, with an updated search on March 20, 2025, which revealed that the “Article in Press” of “Boissonneault 2024” had changed and was officially published on February 1, 2025, with no new RCTs meeting the inclusion criteria. Our search strategy covered each database’s full temporal range from inception to the date of execution, imposing no restrictions on language or publication status. Below, we detail the optimized PubMed search methodology using field-specific syntax: ((“Radiotherapy”[Mesh] OR (Radiotherapies) OR (Radiation Therapy) OR (Radiation Therapies) OR (Therapies, Radiation) OR (Therapy, Radiation) OR (Radiation Treatment) OR (Radiation Treatments) OR (Treatment, Radiation) OR (Radiotherapy, Targeted) OR (Radiotherapies, Targeted) OR (Targeted Radiotherapies) OR (Targeted Radiation Therapy) OR (Radiation Therapies, Targeted) OR (Targeted Radiation Therapies) OR (Therapies, Targeted Radiation) OR (Therapy, Targeted Radiation) OR (Radiation Therapy, Targeted) OR (Targeted Radiotherapy)) AND (“Ossification, Heterotopic”[Mesh] OR (Ossification, Ectopic) OR (Ossification, Pathologic) OR (Ossification, Pathological) OR (Pathological Ossification) OR (Pathologic Ossification) OR (Heterotopic Ossification) OR (Ectopic Ossification))) AND (Randomized Controlled Trials as Topic or Clinical Trials, Randomized or Trials, Randomized Clinical or Controlled Clinical Trials, Randomized or Randomized Controlled Trial or Clinical Trial, Randomized or Trial, Randomized Clinical or Controlled Clinical Trial, Randomized or RCT).
This systematic identification process retrieved 217 initial records, with 88 duplicates removed through automated and manual verification. Following rigorous title/abstract screening of 129 unique publications, 63 were excluded based on predefined eligibility criteria. A subsequent full-text evaluation of 66 potentially relevant articles resulted in the exclusion of one unavailable publication, ultimately yielding seven randomized controlled trials that met our inclusion criteria. These studies specifically analyzed the incidence of HO in indole therapy recipients compared to controls. The complete selection methodology is detailed in the PRISMA 2020 flowchart (Fig. 1).Fig. 1PRISMA flow diagram of screening process.

This study utilized a dual-independent review process to minimize assessment bias, with two principal investigators (LW and KL) conducting parallel literature screening and quality evaluation. Cases of discordance were adjudicated by a third party (GC) to achieve consensus. The investigation applied the PICO framework as follows: population (P): trauma or surgery patients requiring prophylaxis against HO; Intervention (I): prophylactic RT; Comparator (C): non-radiated controls undergoing standard rehabilitation; outcomes (O): primary outcome: radiologically confirmed HO incidence (Brooker Grade); and secondary outcome: adverse reactions.

## Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria
Inclusion criteria encompassed trauma or surgery patients requiring HO prophylaxis, those undergoing radiotherapeutic intervention or standard care, and human clinical trials published in peer-reviewed randomized controlled trials up to January 2025, with mandatory 12-week radiographic surveillance. Exclusion criteria consisted of studies including patients taking NSAIDs, retrospective studies, case series, abstracts from conferences, and secondary reviews.

## Quality Assessment and Data Extraction
Two investigators (LW and CC) assessed methodological quality using Cochrane’s standardized risk of bias criteria. For unresolved discrepancies, an independent arbiter (GC) adjudicated final determinations (Fig. 2a, b). The dual-independent data extraction process involved cross-validation and captured details such as authorship, publication chronology, cohort parameters (sample size, mean age, gender distribution), attrition rates, clinical profiles (diagnosis/surgical interventions), therapeutic protocols (dosage/duration), and outcome metrics (incidence of ectopic ossification, treatment-related complications). ## Data Analysis
The seven included studies underwent quantitative synthesis via RevMan 5.4 (Version 5.4.1, Cochrane Collaboration), with results visualized through forest plot diagrams. Continuous outcomes were expressed as weighted mean differences (WMD) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), whereas dichotomous outcomes were reported as risk ratios (RR) with corresponding 95% CIs. Inter-study heterogeneity was quantified using Cochran’s Q statistic (χ2 test) supplemented by I2 index. For analyses demonstrating low heterogeneity (I2 < 50%), a Mantel–Haenszel fixed-effect model was implemented; conversely, when substantial heterogeneity was present (I2 ≥ 50%), a DerSimonian-Laird random-effects model was employed to account for between-study variance. The statistical significance threshold was set at P = 0.05 for all inferential tests.

## Results
The Brooker grading system [16] was applied for categorization of HO severity, comprising five distinct grades: grade 0 indicates no observable ossification; grade I describes isolated small bone fragments within soft tissues around the hip joint; grade II features bone spurs on the pelvis or proximal femur, with a gap of ≥ 1 cm between ossifications; grade III involves ossifications separated by less than 1 cm, potentially impairing joint mobility; and grade IV denotes complete ankylosis of the hip joint. Initially designed for post-arthroplasty HO assessment, this grading system is now widely utilized for various anatomical regions and served as the basis for collating data in the current analysis. Among the seven selected studies, four were multi-arm trials. Overall, 933 participants were evaluated, with 594 undergoing prophylactic RT, while 339 control subjects received neither RT nor pharmaceutical prophylaxis. ## Heterotopic Ossification (HO)
The total incidence of HO was 17.3% in RT group and 61.7% in control group. Seven studies (Four were multi-arm studies [17, 18, 20, 21]) reported HO outcomes for both the RT and control groups, and meta-analysis revealed significant heterogeneity (I2 = 86%, P < 0.00001), thus a random-effects model was applied. Meta-analysis indicated that the incidence of HO was significantly lower in the RT group [MD = 0.31 95% CI (0.18, 0.56), P < 0.0001] (Fig. 3). Due to significant heterogeneity, subgroup analyses for THA and non-THA groups were conducted, supporting the benefits of RT. Despite significant heterogeneity (I2 = 85%, P < 0.0001) in the THA group (Fig. 4), sensitivity analysis (one-by-one exclusion method) identified Ince 2007 as the main source of heterogeneity. ## Adverse Reactions
Adverse reactions were evaluated in five studies, involving 26 patients in the RT cohort and 16 in controls. Meta-analysis of these events exhibited minimal heterogeneity (I2 = 0%, P = 0.77), thus justifying a fixed-effects approach. The analysis yielded a marginal, nonsignificant trend toward increased adverse reactions following RT [MD = 1.66, 95% CI (0.98, 2.80), P = 0.06] (Fig. 11). Notably, the RT group included nine cases of bone nonunion in the Hamid study, compared to only one in the control group. No RT-related complications such as dermatitis or myelosuppression were observed, and no tumors induced by RT were reported.Fig. 11Forest plot for meta-analysis of adverse reactions

## Discussion
The incidence of HO after hip replacement has been reported to be as high as 90% [23]. RT is widely used to prevent HO in clinical practice, especially post-hip replacement, with most studies affirming its efficacy and safety [8, 9, 24]. While some research indicates that delayed RT can effectively control the progression of HO after its definite onset, dissenting views exist [25]. An animal model study suggested that RT does not consistently prevent [26] HO. Similarly, a recent systematic review on patients undergoing hip surgery demonstrated that RT clinically reduced the incidence of HO, but did not statistically outperform the control group [15]. Interestingly, the findings of our study differ from these reports. Our meta-analysis strongly supports the use of RT for preventing HO, not only post-THA but also in cases of acetabular and elbow fractures. There were clinical and statistical differences between the two groups, both in the overall studies and in subgroup analyses. To verify the robustness of our meta-analysis, we conducted further subgroup analyses for THA and non-THA groups and performed sensitivity analysis using the one-out exclusion method. Although Ince 2007 [20] was identified as a main source of heterogeneity, including or excluding it yielded stable results; thus, it was retained in the analysis. Discrepancies observed in existing literature may stem from prior control groups incorporating NSAIDs for HO prophylaxis, whereas the control groups in this meta-analysis explicitly excluded drug interventions, thereby providing a clearer assessment of RT’s isolated effect. Consequently, our study strongly supports RT’s efficacy in preventing both mild-to-moderate (Brooker I–II) and severe (Brooker III–IV) HO, especially post-THA.
Moreover, current debates persist concerning optimal timing and dosage of RT. Several investigations have documented successful outcomes with postoperative fractionated RT regimens [8, 27]. Seegenschmiedt et al., for instance, compared two RT regimens (5 × 2 Gy vs. 10 × 2 Gy), noting no significant differences in HO incidence [28]. Kolbl and colleagues assessed single doses of 5 Gy and 7 Gy postoperatively, determining that 7 Gy was more effective in preventing HO [29]. Hedley et al. monitored 16 patients over 11.8 months, all of whom received a single 600 cGy dose of RT within three days after THA; all were categorized as Brooker 0–I, with none developing severe HO [30]. Lo et al. administered a single 700-cGy dose post-surgery and observed Brooker grade II HO in only one of 24 patients [31]. In a similar study, a single 700 centigray dose achieved a clinical 100% and radiographic 94% success rate in preventing HO in high-risk patients after THA [32]. Seegenschmiedt et al. compared preoperative and postoperative RT for preventing HO of the hip and found equivalent efficacy between the two, noting that preoperative management simplifies patient logistics and reduces complications associated with postoperative transport [33]. Transporting patients for postoperative RT may increase distress, leading to some patients in our study being reassigned to the control group due to non-cooperation [19]. Heyd et al. [34] and Hanna et al. [35] found that preoperative RT also yields good outcomes and suggested it as an alternative to postoperative treatment. A study involving 462 hips demonstrated that preoperative RT administered the night before surgery was an effective treatment modality [36]. Gregoritch et al. recommend RT within four hours before surgery to achieve satisfactory results [37]. Consistent with numerous studies, Pakos et al., who followed up 97 patients for up to ten years, observed no tumor development in the RT group, with prosthesis loosening comparable to that in the control indomethacin group [38]. In our included study, Ince et al. followed patients for five years and reported no significant implant loosening. Although no severe complications were reported, Kouloulias et al. highlighted the need to limit the RT dose, finding that doses above 30 Gy were associated with later-stage hip dysfunction [39]. After a meta-analysis revealed no difference in total HO incidence between high- and low-dose groups, further analysis suggested that a moderate biologically effective RT dose (20 Gy ≤ biologically effective dose ≤ 24 Gy) is optimal for HO prevention, with fractionated RT proving more effective than a single dose [24]. In the studies we included, the lowest effective dose reported was 600 cGy of postoperative RT by Kienapfel et al., suggesting that low-dose RT may suffice for HO prevention after THA [21]. The maximum frequency and cumulative dose reported by Knelles et al. was 3 Gy per fraction, totaling four fractions, with no serious complications observed, supporting the safety and efficacy of low-dose RT [29].
Can RT also safely and effectively prevent HO in patients with acetabular fractures? Acetabular fractures often involve severe soft tissue injuries and are frequently associated with HO [40]. Surgical approaches, particularly the posterior acetabular or extended surgical approaches, often exhibit higher HO incidence [2]. For trauma patients, surgeries may be delayed due to the severe impact of the condition, significantly correlating later surgery with increased HO incidence [41]. Burd et al. demonstrated that both RT and indomethacin effectively reduced HO post-acetabular fracture surgery [42]. Mathew et al. conducted a systematic review of 5,890 patients and found that RT decreased the incidence of HO from 28.4 to 21.2% and severe HO from 13.9 to 5.7% [5]. The only included study on acetabular fractures showed that RT was effective in reducing the incidence of severe HO compared to control (1/40 vs 11/64) [19]. The elbow joint is another critical site for HO formation outside the hip, and trauma is a common cause of elbow HO [43]. RT, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and surgical resection are common treatments [44, 45]. Heyd et al. treated nine patients with severe elbow HO by surgical resection followed by local irradiation of 500 to 700 cGy, successfully preventing HO recurrence and significantly improving elbow function (Morrey score from 33.3 to 84.5) [46]. Strauss et al. reviewed postoperative radiographs of 44 patients treated with RT to prevent elbow HO and found no cases of severe HO, with 21 patients (48%) exhibiting mild HO [47].In contrast to THA patients, our meta-analysis found no significant difference in the incidence of Brooker I–II after RT in two non-THA studies. Fortunately, it is generally accepted that severe HO (Brooker III–IV) is required to cause clinical discomfort or dysfunction [48]. Our meta-analysis showed a favorable effect on Brooker III–IV in all subgroups. However, it is noteworthy that there was no significant difference in the incidence of HO, including severe HO, between the RT and control groups in “Hamid 2010 [14].” This is the only study conducted outside the hip joint, which may also caution us to carefully assess the use of RT in preventing HO in the elbow joint.
Regarding adverse effects, no significant RT-related dermatitis or neoplasms were reported. The meta-analysis showed a strong nonsignificant trend between the two groups, which deserves our attention. We further investigated this noteworthy result and found that the reports of Kienapfel et al. and Hamid et al. were responsible for this trend. Although Kienapfel et al. [21] primarily reported that prolonged wound secretion and dyspepsia were not statistically different between the RT and control groups, However, it is noteworthy that the prolonged (> 5 days) wound secretion they reported appeared to be higher in the RT group (6/49 vs 1/50). Simultaneously, in the study by Hamid et al. [14], the incidence of bone nonunion in the RT group was significantly higher than that in the control group (38% vs 4%). Additionally, the probability of bone nonunion in the RT group was much higher than the 3.7% (13/351) reported in the study by Linker et al. This seems at odds with other studies in which Stein et al. treated 11 patients with fractures /dislocations of the elbow with a single dose of 700 centigray radiation after surgery, all without fracture nonunion [49]. Similarly, Robinson et al. reported that 50 patients undergoing Elbow Surgery were treated with a single dose of postoperative radiation to prevent HO, with only two patients having nonunion [50]. Although the effect of RT on elbow fracture healing is still controversial, as the only randomized controlled study that met the inclusion criteria, Hamid et al.’s conclusion deserves our attention. We advocate for more RCTs to fully evaluate the efficacy of RT in preventing HO across various joints.
This study has several limitations. First, the literature included was relatively small, particularly for patients with non-THA, which may introduce potential bias into the meta-analysis, necessitating further RCTs. Second, we did not include data using other classifications in our analysis. The Brooker classification has good applicability in hip joints, but its application in other parts is still controversial. For example, in elbow joints, classifications reported by Hastings et al. [51] and Diane et al. [52] may be more applicable. Third, functional outcomes in the enrolled patients were not evaluated due to limited data availability. Fourth, due to the few reports of adverse effects and inconsistent standards, a more in-depth analysis could not be performed.

## Conclusion
Our meta-analysis strongly supports the preventive effect of RT on HO, especially for high-risk patients and in cases that other treatments, like NSAIDs, are contraindicated or avoided. The risk of bone nonunion should be considered when using RT to prevent HO in the elbow. More prospective RCTs, particularly those outside the hip joint, are needed to confirm our conclusions.
