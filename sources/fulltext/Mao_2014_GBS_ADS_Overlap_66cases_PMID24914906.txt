Background: Some patients with Guillain–Barre ´ syndrome (GBS) also have acquired demyelination of the
central nervous system (CNS) (i.e. acquired demyelinating syndrome, ADS). Often, the overlap of GBS andADS is overlooked. Therefore, we evaluated case reports of GBS/ADS overlap syndrome.Methods: We mainly performed website-based research based on articles in cases presented with GBS/
ADS overlap syndrome. A total of 66 cases were included. Clinical and prognosis data were analyzed.Results: A total of 85% of patients with simultaneous or consecutive occurrence of GBS and ADS were
identified within 4 weeks of the initial diagnosis. Transverse myelitis (TM) (32%) was the most common ADSfound in GBS/ADS. Patients with Miller Fisher syndrome (MFS)/ADS overlap syndrome had greater female
predominance, mean age, frequency of onset at the same time period, or within a short period, and
percentage of sole involvement of the subtentorial region. The outcome was favorable based on thefunctional status in 74% of patients. The sensory level (OR 50.182, 95% CI 50.055–0.598; P50.005)
was the best predictor of a poor outcome, while visual deficit (OR 54.667, 95% CI 51.187–18.352;
P50.027) predicted a favorable outcome.
Conclusion: The ADS in GBS are diverse, CNS demyelinating may occur at any time, but early in the GBS
course (and vice versa). MFS/ADS overlap syndromes is more common. The prognosis is generally good,but patients with sensory level deficit are likely to have a poor prognosis. The features of MFS/other CIS
may better reflect involvement of the brainstem in MFS itself, rather than ADS in autoimmune peripheral
neuropathies.
Keywords: Guillain–Barre ´ syndrome, Demyelinating diseases, Central nervous system, Overlap syndrome, Miller Fisher syndrome, Clinically isolated
syndromes, Transverse myelitis
Introduction
Guillain–Barre ´ syndrome (GBS) and acquired demy-
elination of the central nervous system (CNS) (i.e.
acquired demyelinating syndrome, ADS) are classi-
cally viewed as distinct autoimmune diseases. Patientspresenting with clinical, biochemical, and serologicalresults of both diseases, either simultaneously orconsecutively, have been repeatedly described andrecognized in case reports.
1–3Overlap syndromes are
deﬁned as the association of two conditions in a singlepatient. However, the clinical spectrum of GBS/ADSoverlap syndrome remains poorly established and isoften overlooked.
4Nonetheless, GBS/ADS overlap
syndrome encompasses a wide spectrum of manifesta-
tions of both the CNS and peripheral nervous system
(PNS). As the clinical spectrum of GBS/ADS overlapsyndrome is heterogeneous, a tentative subdivision indistinct patient subgroups may predict a therapeutic
outcome.
3Therefore, we performed a website search-
based study to evaluate the incidence, clinical course,and outcomes of GBS/ADS overlap syndrome.

Clinical characteristics
Demographic and clinical characteristics are pre-
sented in Table 1. Sixty-six (33 women and 33 men)
patients were analyzed in this study. The mean age
was 30.99 years old (range from 2 to 77 years old).
We found that the occurrence of overlap syndrome
after infection or vaccinations accounted for 47 cases
(70%). In 57 cases (85%), GBS (including both non-
MFS and MFS) and ADS occurred simultaneously
or consecutively within 4 weeks after onset. In the
rest nine cases (15%), the time interval between the
occurrence of GBS and ADS was beyond 4 weeks
after onset. Non-MFS/ADS overlap syndrome was
identiﬁed in 55 patients (83%), while MFS/ADS
overlap syndrome was identiﬁed in only 11 patients
(17%). The most common subtypes of ADS identiﬁed
were TM (32%), other forms of CIS (24%), and ON
(20%). Twenty-nine patients (43%) initially manifested
GBS/ADS overlap syndrome ‘at the same time’
(deﬁned as within a very short period with no more
than 1 day of the onset of GBS or ADS event),
followed by 21 patients with GBS (31%) manifesta-
tions preceding ADS symptoms, whereas 17 patients
(25%) presented with ADS symptoms preceding GBS.

six subtypes of ADS (and vice versa). Miller Fisher

non-MFS groups with ADS (solid shapes).
Table 2 summarizes the clinical patterns among

nerve palsy was observed; however, on average, it
represented 55%; while extrapyramidal syndrome
only presented in patients with GBS/MS but not in
the other subtypes of the overlap syndrome.

Prognosis analysis
Immunotherapies were prescribed in 85% of patientswith ADS or GBS presentation, mostly prednisone
and intravenous immunoglobulin. The outcome of
GBS/ADS overlap syndrome was good in 48 of 65
patients (74%, Table 1) during follow-up (median
10.7 months) using the Hughes scale. Only four
patients died (one with GBS/ON overlap syndrome,
two with GBS/MS, and one with GBS/ADEM) in the
deteriorating stage. Of seven patients who did not
receive immunotherapy, six recovered well and onlyone with GBS/MS overlap syndrome had a poor
outcome. No signiﬁcant factors from the clinical,
biochemical, electrophysiological, and neuroimaging
results were identiﬁed (data not shown). Only the
sensory level (OR 50.182, 95% CI 50.055–0.598;

Since the course between groups of MFS/ADS

However, patients with MFS/ADS overlap syndrome

ment. Predictor (sensory level) of poor outcome can
be identiﬁed after a careful analysis. Our results

First, it is worthwhile to note that we found several
demographic features of GBS/ADS overlap syn-
drome such as a younger age at onset and a balanced

typical global ADS studies; whereas these features

majority of typical GBS studies, it is believed that the
incidence increases linearly with age and that men are
approximately 1.5 times more likely to be affected
than women.59,62The predominance of cases found at
younger ages in our study could be attributed to
higher exposures to triggering factors, as an infection
or potentially after vaccination.
Second, our data showed that current classiﬁcation
of the six ADS subgroups share many clinicalfeatures, such as age of onset, duration of disease,
cerebral spinal ﬂuid protein, and cell counts, suggest-
ing that these presentations may be a part of the
spectrum or range of disease diversity. Additionally,
based on the results presented in Table 3, it seemsthat distinguishing these groups from each other at
the ﬁrst presentation is impossible. Thus, our results
suggest that current biological tests fail to identify a
distinct subgroup of patients with overlap syndrome.
Third, TM/GBS overlap syndrome was the most
common subgroup of GBS/ADS overlap syndrome in
our study, in contrast to the very uncommonfrequency of TM reported in 842 Western Australian

57In addition,

reported to be uncommon in other studies.7,60The
higher frequency of TM found in the present study
may simply reﬂect two factors: (1) there could be adiscrepancy in deﬁning TM; and (2) it is likely that the
overlap between GBS and TM may share more
common etiologies and genetic backgrounds. Takentogether with our observations, the latter factor is
more likely to explain the higher frequency of GBS/TM; however, further investigations are necessary.
We found that the onset of GBS/ADS overlap
syndrome occurred at the same time or that one
disease (GBS or ADS) secondary to the other disease
(ADS or GBS) occurred in the acute phase
(,4 weeks). By deﬁnition, all patients with GBS
had their nadir (maximal weakness) within 4 weeks,which was the reason that they were initially
diagnosed with GBS. We further explored the timing
of onset between GBS and ADS (Figure 1). The
onset of this concurrent event mostly occurred at the
very beginning of the course ( ,10 days in a total of
32 cases). Based on these ﬁndings, this study
supported that GBS and ADS may form a contin-uous spectrum with variable PNS and CNS involve-
ment rather than co-incidental occurrence.
The most frequent diseases in the ADS group, ON
and NMO, caused a visual deﬁcit. It is known that
patients with GBS rarely present with a visual deﬁcit;
therefore, the clinical presentation of a visual deﬁcit
becomes a very speciﬁc sign/symptom in the diagnosisof GBS/ADS overlap syndrome among the ﬁve most
common clinical presentations (e.g. paresis, sensory
deﬁcit, cranial nerve palsies, urinary disturbance, and
visual deﬁcit). During the early stage of the overlap
syndrome, differentiating concurrent GBS and ADS
from GBS alone can be very difﬁcult. Based on our
ﬁndings, one patient was diagnosed with GBS butwith a later onset of a visual deﬁcit. It is reasonable to
suspect that this patient might have had GBS/ADS
overlap syndrome. Additionally, clinical and other
characteristics were not signiﬁcantly different among
patients except for sensory level (76%) in GBS/TMoverlap syndrome.

Conclusions
The ADS in GBS are diverse, CNS demyelinatingmay occur at any time, but early in the GBS course(and vice versa). Miller Fisher syndrome/ADS over-lap syndromes is more common. The prognosis isgenerally good, but patients with sensory level deﬁcitare likely to have a poor prognosis. The other CIS inMFS may be one of the nosologic position, ratherthan ‘ADS in autoimmune peripheral neuropathies.’We emphasize both the clinical and prognosticﬁndings of GBS/ADS overlap syndrome, which willhelp us to further understand the nosologicalrelationships and to improve the outcome of theoverlap syndrome.
