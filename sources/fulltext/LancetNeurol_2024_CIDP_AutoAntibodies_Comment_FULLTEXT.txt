Targeting auto-antibodies for CIDP: hope and questions
Despite the availability of evidence-based treat -
ments for chronic inflammatory demyelinating 
polyradiculoneuropathy (CIDP), their use frequently results in incomplete recovery.
1 Intravenous immuno -
globulins, plasma exchange, and corticosteroids might improve function in some, but not all, people with CIDP. No trials involving other agents have, to date, shown a benefit.
2 Side-effect profiles, tolerability, logistics 
for intravenous infusions, and difficulties of long-term venous access are additional barriers to effective 
treatment for patients with CIDP. 
In The Lancet Neurology, Jeffrey Allen and colleagues
3 
report findings from ADHERE, an international, double-blind, placebo-controlled, phase 2 trial of efgartigimod—a human IgG1 antibody Fc fragment that reduces circulating concentrations of pathogenic IgG autoantibodies—co-formulated with recombinant human hyaluronidase PH20. Efgartigimod has previously shown effectiveness in patients with myasthenia gravis.
4 The ADHERE trial was done in two 
main stages, after a run-in period of no longer than 12 weeks, during which participants who had been receiving immunoglobulins or corticosteroids before the trial had these treatments withdrawn. Participants not on immunoglobulins or corticosteroids during  
the previous 6 months were classified as off-treatment. During the run-in period, participants who showed evidence of clinical deterioration (defined as an increase by 1 point or more on the adjusted Inflammatory Neuropathy Cause and Treatment [aINCAT] score, deterioration by 4 points or more on the centile Inflammatory Rasch-built Overall Disability Scale [I-RODS], or decline by 8 kPa or more in grip strength) entered stage A. Stage A was a treatment period of up to 12 weeks, during which all participants received once-weekly subcutaneous efgartigimod PH20 until evidence of clinical improvement or until the end of the 12-week treatment period; the primary endpoint in stage A was the proportion of responders, defined as participants who had improvement of aINCAT, I-RODS, or grip strength, whichever had deteriorated during run-in. Participants with confirmed evidence of clinical improvement then advanced to stage B and were randomly assigned (1:1) to once-weekly subcutaneous efgartigimod PH20 (n=111) or placebo (n=110) for up to 48 weeks, with stage B planned to end once a total of 88 relapses had occurred. The primary outcome in stage B was a deterioration of 1 point or more on the aINCAT scale. The main results were convincingly in favour of subcutaneous efgartigimod PH20, with evidence of clinical improvement in 66% (95% CI 61 ·0–71· 6) 
of participants in stage A, and subcutaneous efgartigimod PH20 reducing the risk of relapse by 61% versus placebo (hazard ratio 0 ·39 [95% CI 0· 25–0· 61]; 
p<0· 0001) in stage B. 
Prespecified subgroup analyses showed a more 
nuanced picture. In stage A, participants treated with immunoglobulins within 6 months before study had a lower responder rate (97 [59%] of 165) than those previously off-treatment (68 [72%] of 94) and those treated with corticosteroids in the previous 6 months (49 [78%] of 63). Furthermore, treatment discontinuation for any reason other than trial

patients previously treated with immunoglobulins 
(53 [32%] of 162) than in those previously off-treatment (eight [9%] of 94) or those previously treated with corticosteroids (eight [13%] of 63). However, in stage B, relapse rates were lower with subcutaneous efgartigimod PH20 than with placebo in patients pretreated with immunoglobulins (14 [29%] of 48 vs 28 [58%] of 48) and patients treated with 
corticosteroids (five [21%] of 24 vs 15 [65%] 23), but 
not in previously off-treatment participants (12 [31%] of 39 vs 16 [41%] of 39). Relapse rates in the placebo 
group of the three subgroups were similar. 
Previous CIDP trials have included participants who 
were concurrently receiving, or who had recently discontinued, immunoglobulins
5 and corticosteroids,6–8 
and their results might have been  affected by 
previous treatments, owing to persistent treatment effects, heterogeneous underlying pathophysiological 
mechanisms, or both. Interpretation of the detailed results of ADHERE is therefore not straightforward. 
Immunoglobulin cessation shortly before entry into stage A might explain the poorer response in participants pretreated with immunoglobulins, which raises questions regarding the ideal timing of a switch from immunoglobulins to FcRn blocker therapy such as 
subcutaneous efgartigimod PH20. The use of multiple primary outcomes in stage A but of only the aINCAT in stage B might partly explain the absence of a reduction in relapse rates with subcutaneous efgartigimod PH20 in the previously off-treatment participants. A post-hoc analysis of a previous trial found that early improvement of I-RODS or grip strength were poor predictors of subsequent aINCAT improvement,
9 
suggesting inconsistent use of outcomes within a trial is probably unadvisable. The appropriateness of a minimum clinically important difference for the centile I-RODS of only 4 points, as used in ADHERE, is also debatable, as a cutoff of 8 points has been derived previously.
10 The relatively high placebo 
response in previously off-treatment participants in stage B might be another explanation for the absence of effect of subcutaneous efgartigimod PH20 in this subgroup, which itself might suggest a greater carry-over effect from stage A in this subgroup, than in the immunoglobulin and corticosteroid pre-treated subgroups. Whether and how the subgroup analyses of the ADHERE trial should affect consideration of efgartigimod as a first-line treatment for CIDP is unknown. 
The ADHERE trial offers the prospect of a new, 
rapidly effective, well tolerated, and easy to administer treatment for CIDP. Uncertainties remain regarding which patients would be likely to benefit the most in light of their previous treatment status. However, after decades with little therapeutic progress, this study offers reasons for overall optimism and enthusiasm.
