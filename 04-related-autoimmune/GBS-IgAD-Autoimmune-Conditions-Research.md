## 1. Autoimmune Conditions Associated with Guillain-Barre Syndrome

### 1.1 Overview

Guillain-Barre syndrome (GBS) is an acute immune-mediated polyradiculoneuropathy characterized by rapidly progressive, symmetric limb weakness with hyporeflexia or areflexia. Subtypes include acute inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy (AIDP), acute motor axonal neuropathy (AMAN), acute motor and sensory axonal neuropathy (AMSAN), and Miller Fisher syndrome (MFS). GBS is classically triggered by preceding infections (Campylobacter jejuni, CMV, EBV, Zika virus) rather than by chronic autoimmune disease, and it follows a monophasic, self-limited course -- features that distinguish it from typical chronic autoimmune disorders.

**Critical nuance:** A 2024 PMC review (Papadopoulou et al.) directly addressed whether GBS is related to SLE or other autoimmune diseases. The authors concluded that GBS is "not typically associated with any autoimmune disease" and distinguished between "SLE-related GBS" (polyneuropathy attributable to SLE) and "pure GBS" (GBS attributable to conventional triggers such as infections). Nevertheless, GBS has been reported in association with numerous autoimmune conditions, and the clinical overlap is well-documented in case reports and series.

### 1.2 Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE)

Polyneuropathy occurs in 1.5-36% of SLE patients, more frequently in those with CNS involvement and high disease activity. GBS as a presenting manifestation of SLE is rare but well-documented, particularly in pediatric populations. The coexistence has been explained by shared autoimmune mechanisms, including anti-ganglioside antibody cross-reactivity and immune complex deposition.

- Papadopoulou M, et al. "Is Guillain-Barre syndrome related to systemic lupus erythematosus or other autoimmune diseases?" *Clin Rheumatol.* 2024. PMC10806246.
- Viegas Barroso SN, et al. "Guillain-Barre syndrome as the initial presentation of systemic lupus erythematosus: Case report with a systematic and literature review." *Autoimmun Rev.* 2023.
- Ince OB, et al. "Rheumatic Diseases Presenting with Guillain-Barre Syndrome: Sjogren's Syndrome and Systemic Lupus Erythematosus." *Istanbul Med J.* 2020.

### 1.3 Sjogren's Syndrome

Sjogren's syndrome (SS) may coexist with GBS, and their chronic autoimmune backgrounds, multifactorial etiology, and multiple circulating autoantibodies create conditions for co-occurrence. The rheumatic diseases presenting with GBS have been described as extremely rare, but they may represent an underlying etiological cause in resistant and prolonged GBS cases.

- Ince OB, et al. "Rheumatic Diseases Presenting with Guillain-Barre Syndrome: Sjogren's Syndrome and Systemic Lupus Erythematosus." *Istanbul Med J.* 2020.

### 1.4 Sarcoidosis

Neurosarcoidosis can present as a GBS-like clinical phenotype, creating diagnostic challenges. Multiple case reports document Guillain-Barre-like syndrome secondary to sarcoidosis, with a literature review identifying at least 9 similar cases. Key distinguishing features include:
- CSF pleocytosis (atypical for GBS; suggests sarcoidosis)
- Increased lymphocyte count (inconsistent with GBS)
- Cranial neuropathies (the most common neurological manifestation of sarcoidosis)

Acute polyradiculopathy from sarcoidosis (true peripheral nervous system involvement) is extremely rare, whereas the typical sarcoid neuropathy is subacute or chronic sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy.

- Tana C, et al. "Neurosarcoidosis presenting as Guillain-Barre-like syndrome. A case report and review of the literature." *Medicine.* 2015.
- Stojkovic T, et al. "Sarcoidosis presenting with an acute Guillain-Barre syndrome." *J Neurol Neurosurg Psychiatry.* 1989;52(4):566.
- Roe J, et al. "A case of neurosarcoidosis that presented with symptoms of Guillain-Barre syndrome." *BMJ Case Rep.* 2011. PMC3079465.

### 1.5 Hashimoto's Thyroiditis and Autoimmune Thyroid Disease

The association between GBS and Hashimoto's thyroiditis is a rare clinical entity. However, a pathophysiological connection exists: plasma membranes of thyrocytes and neuronal cells are both rich in gangliosides, which may lead to cross-reactive autoantibody production generating the pathogenesis of both conditions. The calculated coincidental concurrence was extremely low (0.0004%), suggesting common pathogenic mediators rather than chance.

Cases of GBS with transition from Hashimoto's to Graves' disease have also been documented, along with concurrent immune thrombocytopenic purpura and GBS in patients with Hashimoto's thyroiditis.

- Mirza S, et al. "Guillain-Barre syndrome and Hashimoto's thyroiditis." *BMJ Case Rep.* 2016. PMC4986428.
- Pham HP, et al. "Guillain-Barre syndrome with transition from Hashimoto's to Graves' disease: a case report." *BMC Endocr Disord.* 2022. PMC9195218.
- Zimmerman J, et al. "Guillain-Barre syndrome associated with Hashimoto's thyroiditis." *J Neurol.* 1989;236(1):56-57.

### 1.6 Type 1 Diabetes

Type 1 diabetes (T1D) and GBS share autoimmune pathogenic mechanisms. While direct co-occurrence is relatively rare, population-based studies have identified shared genetic and immunological risk factors. Both conditions involve aberrant T-cell responses and autoantibody-mediated tissue damage. The autoimmune nature of T1D places patients within a broader immune dysregulation spectrum that may increase susceptibility to other autoimmune conditions.

### 1.7 Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD)

GBS accounts for the majority of reported demyelinating neuropathies associated with IBD. The association has been documented in both Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis:

- GBS is considered a potential extraintestinal manifestation of Crohn's disease
- Concomitant GBS during ulcerative colitis relapse is very rare but documented
- Chronic inflammation of intestinal mucosa allows presentation of microbial antigens normally hidden from the immune system, potentially inciting extraintestinal immune response via molecular mimicry
- Campylobacter jejuni infection is a common trigger for both GBS and IBD flares, providing a shared infectious trigger

Peripheral neuropathy is broadly recognized as related to IBD, with either demyelinating or axonal involvement documented.

- Elsehety A, Bertorini TE. "Neurologic and neuropsychiatric complications of Crohn's disease." *South Med J.* 1997.
- Gondim FAA, et al. "Guillain-Barre syndrome as an extraintestinal manifestation of Crohn's disease." *World J Gastroenterol.* 2005. PMC2397986.
- Jayasundara JMSW, et al. "Concomitant Guillain-Barre Syndrome in a young Sri Lankan male with severe ulcerative colitis." *BMC Gastroenterol.* 2022.
- Lossos A, et al. "Guillain-Barre syndrome, tuberculosis and inflammatory bowel disease: a multiple association." *Int Arch Med.* 2010.

### 1.8 Rheumatoid Arthritis

The co-existence of GBS and rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is uncommon. However, a significant epidemiological finding is that women with rheumatological disease had the highest incidence of GBS at 9.84 cases per 100,000 person-years -- approximately 7 times the general population risk.

- Naeem S, et al. "Co-occurrence of Guillain-Barre syndrome and rheumatoid arthritis in a young female: A case report from a low middle-income country." *Clin Case Rep.* 2024. PMC10849983.
- Kuitwaard K, et al. "Early predictors of Guillain-Barre syndrome in the life course of women." *Int J Epidemiol.* 2018;47(1):280-288.

### 1.9 Other Documented Associations

- **Celiac disease:** A 9-year-old girl with celiac disease presented with recurrent GBS (third episode). Molecular mimicry has been proposed as the mechanism linking these two disorders.
- **Myasthenia gravis:** Both GBS and MG are autoimmune diseases of the neuromuscular/peripheral nervous system. MG patients have an increased overall risk for complicating autoimmune diseases (13-22%).
- **Multiple sclerosis:** T1D, RA, GBS, and MG are found in MS patients and their relatives, suggesting one or more common etiologic mechanisms.

## 2. Autoimmune Conditions Associated with IgA Deficiency

### 2.1 Overview

Selective immunoglobulin A deficiency (SIgAD) is the most common primary immunodeficiency in Caucasians, with prevalence ranging from 1:155 (Spain) to 1:18,550 (Japan). Defined as serum IgA <0.07 g/L with normal IgG and IgM in individuals >4 years of age, most IgAD patients remain asymptomatic. However, autoimmune disease occurs in approximately 10-30% of affected individuals, with a 20-year longitudinal study demonstrating a 4-fold increase in autoimmune conditions (23% in SIgAD vs. 5% in controls).

### 2.2 Celiac Disease (Strong Association)

Celiac disease is one of the most strongly associated autoimmune conditions with IgAD:
- 3% of celiac disease patients have IgA deficiency
- 6.57% of IgA-deficient patients develop celiac disease
- CD patients with SIgAD are more likely to have a co-existing autoimmune disease (67% vs. 23.5%)
- CD patients with SIgAD more often lack classic GI symptoms, complicating diagnosis
- Both conditions share the HLA-DQ2 haplotype (HLA-DQB1*02)
- IgA deficiency can mask serological diagnosis of celiac disease (IgA-tTG testing yields false negatives)

- Chow MA, et al. "Immunoglobulin A deficiency in celiac disease in the United States." *J Clin Gastroenterol.* 2012. PMC4718864.
- Poddighe D, et al. "The Role of HLA in the Association between IgA Deficiency and Celiac Disease." *Dis Markers.* 2021. PMC8856801.

### 2.3 Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE)

IgAD prevalence in SLE is significantly elevated. Both IgAD and SLE share the ancestral 8.1 haplotype (HLA-A1, -B8, -DR3). SLE patients with concurrent IgAD may have altered disease expression and treatment considerations.

- Hammarstrom L, et al. "Selective IgA deficiency in autoimmune diseases." *Mol Med.* 2012. PMC3321806.

### 2.4 Rheumatoid Arthritis (RA)

Rheumatoid arthritis is among the most prevalent autoimmune conditions in IgA-deficient patients (3.80% prevalence). The shared genetic associations in the MHC region and non-MHC genes contribute to this comorbidity.

- Hammarstrom L, et al. "Selective IgA deficiency in autoimmune diseases." *Mol Med.* 2012. PMC3321806.

### 2.5 Type 1 Diabetes

T1D is strongly associated with IgA deficiency, sharing the HLA-DR3 and HLA-DQB1*02 associations. The ancestral 8.1 haplotype predisposes to both conditions. Non-MHC genes IFIH1 and CLEC16A are associated with both IgAD and T1D.

- Hammarstrom L, et al. "Selective IgA deficiency in autoimmune diseases." *Mol Med.* 2012. PMC3321806.

### 2.6 Autoimmune Thyroid Disease

Both Graves' disease and Hashimoto's thyroiditis are associated with IgA deficiency. The ancestral 8.1 haplotype is shared between IgAD and Graves' disease. Autoimmune thyroid disease is also listed among the endocrine manifestations of autoimmune polyendocrine syndrome type 2 (APS-2), which includes IgA deficiency as a non-endocrine component.

### 2.7 Inflammatory Bowel Disease

IgA deficiency is associated with significantly elevated rates of IBD:
- Overall IBD: 3.9% vs. 0.81% in controls
- Crohn's disease: 2.4% vs. 0.42% (prevalence ratio 5.7)
- Ulcerative colitis: 1.7% vs. 0.46% (prevalence ratio 3.9)
- 4.01% prevalence of IBD in IgA-deficient patients (second most common autoimmune comorbidity after celiac disease)

Mechanistically, IgA deficiency elicits expansion of some commensal bacterial strains and promotes inflammation in the small intestine. The small intestine is the largest reservoir of IgA-producing plasma cells, and absence of IgA disrupts mucosal homeostasis.

- Ludvigsson JF, et al. "Association between IgA deficiency and other autoimmune conditions: a population-based matched cohort study." *J Clin Immunol.* 2014.
- Cong Y, et al. "Immunoglobulin A-specific deficiency induces spontaneous inflammation specifically in the ileum." *J Exp Med.* 2022. PMC8809603.

### 2.8 Autoimmune Hepatitis

Weaker but documented associations exist between IgAD and autoimmune hepatitis.

### 2.9 Vitiligo

Vitiligo is associated with IgA deficiency, and both are included in the constellation of conditions associated with APS-2.

### 2.10 Other Documented Associations

- **Autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA):** Well-documented, often presenting alongside other autoimmune cytopenias
- **Immune thrombocytopenic purpura (ITP):** The most common autoimmune hematologic manifestation in SIgAD
- **Autoimmune neutropenia**
- **Juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA)**
- **Ankylosing spondylitis**
- **Myasthenia gravis (MG):** Data on prevalence in IgAD is limited
- **Pernicious anemia**
- **Sjogren's syndrome:** Documented in cases of IgAD progressing to CVID
- **Scleroderma:** Weaker association noted
- **Dermatomyositis/polymyositis**
- **Alopecia areata**
- **Dermatitis herpetiformis**

## 3. Overlapping Autoimmune Conditions Associated with BOTH GBS and IgA Deficiency

### 3.1 Conditions Appearing in Both Lists

| Condition | GBS Association | IgAD Association | Strength of Evidence |
|-----------|----------------|-------------------|---------------------|
| Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE) | Case reports/series; polyneuropathy in 1.5-36% of SLE | Strong; HLA-DR3/8.1 haplotype shared | Moderate for both |
| Type 1 Diabetes (T1D) | Shared autoimmune mechanisms | Strong; HLA-DR3/DQB1 shared, IFIH1/CLEC16A | Strong for IgAD, moderate for GBS |
| Autoimmune Thyroid Disease | Rare but documented; ganglioside cross-reactivity | Strong; ancestral 8.1 haplotype | Moderate for GBS, strong for IgAD |
| Inflammatory Bowel Disease | Extraintestinal manifestation; C. jejuni trigger | Prevalence ratio 3.9-5.7x above controls | Moderate for both |
| Celiac Disease | Recurrent GBS reported; molecular mimicry | 6.57% prevalence in IgAD | Case reports for GBS, strong for IgAD |
| Rheumatoid Arthritis | 7x increased GBS risk in RA patients | 3.80% prevalence in IgAD | Moderate for both |
| Myasthenia Gravis | Both affect neuromuscular/PNS | Listed in APS-2 with IgAD | Weak-moderate for both |
| Sjogren's Syndrome | Rare but documented co-occurrence | Documented in IgAD-to-CVID progression | Weak-moderate for both |

### 3.2 Shared Pathogenic Mechanisms

#### 3.2.1 Molecular Mimicry
Both GBS and IgAD-associated autoimmune conditions involve molecular mimicry -- the process by which microbial antigens structurally resemble host molecules, leading to cross-reactive immune responses. In GBS, Campylobacter jejuni lipooligosaccharides mimic peripheral nerve gangliosides. In IgAD, the absence of mucosal IgA allows increased translocation of microbial antigens, potentially amplifying molecular mimicry-based autoimmune responses.

#### 3.2.2 Mucosal Immune Barrier Breakdown
IgA is the primary immunoglobulin at mucosal surfaces. Its absence leads to:
- Increased translocation of specific bacterial taxa
- Elevated levels of systemic IgG targeting fecal microbiota
- Expansion of commensal bacterial strains that promote inflammation
- Enhanced follicular CD4 T helper cell frequency and activation
- Altered CD8 T cell activation state

This barrier breakdown could theoretically increase exposure to antigens that trigger GBS through molecular mimicry, particularly Campylobacter jejuni.

#### 3.2.3 T-cell Dysregulation
Both GBS and IgAD-associated conditions show T-cell regulatory dysfunction:
- GBS: Elevated Th1, Th17, and Th22 cells; elevated IL-17 and IL-22; CD4+ helper T cell dysregulation throughout disease course
- IgAD: Enhanced follicular CD4 T helper cell frequency and activation; associated with T1D and autoimmune thyroid disease which involve T-cell mediated destruction

### 3.3 HLA Associations

#### 3.3.1 IgA Deficiency HLA Profile
IgAD is strongly associated with the ancestral HLA-B8, DR3, DQ2 (8.1) haplotype:
- Up to 45% of IgAD patients carry at least one copy (vs. 16% in general population)
- Homozygosity for the 8.1 haplotype further increases risk
- Primary signal maps to HLA-DQB1*02 in the Class II region
- On HLA-DR1 and -DR7-positive haplotypes, IGAD1 maps to the class II region
- On HLA-DR3 haplotypes, susceptibility maps to the telomeric end of the class III region

**Key reference:** Mohammadi J, et al. "MHC susceptibility genes to IgA deficiency are located in different regions on different HLA haplotypes." *J Immunol.* 2002;169(5):2747-2753.

#### 3.3.2 GBS HLA Profile
HLA associations in GBS are less consistent:
- A systematic review and meta-analysis (Blum et al. 2015) examined HLA-DQB1 polymorphisms and GBS risk
- Some studies suggest associations with specific HLA alleles (HLA-DR/DQ polymorphisms in Tunisian patients)
- A genome-wide association study found no significant associations with individual SNPs or imputed HLA types
- The genetic basis of GBS susceptibility remains poorly characterized

**Key reference:** Blum S, et al. "Human Leukocyte Antigen DQB1 (HLA-DQB1) Polymorphisms and the Risk for Guillain-Barre Syndrome: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis." *PLoS One.* 2015;10(7):e0131374.

#### 3.3.3 Shared HLA Overlap
The ancestral 8.1 haplotype (HLA-A1, -B8, -DR3) is shared between IgAD and several autoimmune conditions that also appear in GBS association lists:
- SLE
- Type 1 diabetes
- Graves' disease
- Celiac disease (via HLA-DQ2)
- Myasthenia gravis

However, direct HLA overlap between GBS and IgAD specifically is not strongly established in current literature. The overlap occurs indirectly through shared associations with the same autoimmune comorbidities.

## 4. Immune Dysregulation Spectrum

### 4.1 IgA Deficiency and CVID: A Continuum

IgA deficiency and common variable immunodeficiency (CVID) exist on a continuum of primary antibody deficiency:

- **Shared genetic basis:** IgAD and CVID occur in members of the same family, with similar underlying B-cell defects
- **Progression:** Several documented cases of IgAD progressing to CVID, particularly in patients with autoimmune features
- **Shared susceptibility locus:** HLA-DR3, -B8, -A1 haplotype (ancestral 8.1 haplotype) contains susceptibility loci for both IgAD and CVID
- **B-cell defects:** Both involve defects in B-cell maturation and class-switch recombination

**Key reference:** Aghamohammadi A, et al. "Progression of selective IgA deficiency to common variable immunodeficiency." *Int Arch Allergy Immunol.* 2008;147(2):87-92.

### 4.2 CVID and Autoimmunity

CVID carries an even higher burden of autoimmunity than isolated IgAD:
- 14-54% of CVID patients have autoimmune disorders
- Autoimmunity can be the first clinical manifestation of CVID
- Most common: autoimmune cytopenias (ITP, AIHA)
- Also: autoimmune thyroiditis, T1D, IBD, rheumatological disorders

### 4.3 CVID Neurological Manifestations

Approximately 42% of CVID patients recorded at least one neurologic condition or symptom, demonstrating that the immune dysregulation extends to the nervous system. CVID-associated autoimmune and inflammatory responses can affect all organ systems including the nervous system, creating potential overlap with GBS-spectrum disorders.

**Key reference:** Barmettler S, et al. "Neurologic Manifestations of Common Variable Immunodeficiency." *Neurol Neuroimmunol Neuroinflamm.* 2023;10(2):e200088.

### 4.4 Autoimmune Polyendocrine Syndrome Type 2 (APS-2)

APS-2 (Schmidt syndrome) represents a clustering of autoimmune conditions that includes many of the same diseases associated with both GBS and IgAD:
- **Mandatory component:** Addison's disease
- **Endocrine components:** Graves' disease, Hashimoto's thyroiditis, T1D, primary hypogonadism
- **Non-endocrine components:** IgA deficiency, celiac disease, vitiligo, myasthenia gravis, pernicious anemia, alopecia

APS-2 is associated with HLA-B8 and DR3, the same haplotype strongly associated with IgAD. This suggests that patients with IgAD who also carry APS-2 susceptibility may be at increased risk for multiple autoimmune conditions, some of which are independently associated with GBS.

### 4.5 Monitoring Recommendations

All symptomatic IgAD patients, especially those with:
- Associated IgG subclass deficiency
- Autoimmune features
- Multiple autoimmune conditions

...should be monitored for evolution to CVID, as this progression can significantly alter the immune dysregulation profile and treatment considerations.

## 5. Shared Molecular Mechanisms

### 5.1 Complement System

#### In GBS:
The complement system is central to GBS pathophysiology:
- Anti-ganglioside antibodies (anti-GM1, anti-GQ1b) bind to peripheral nerve surfaces
- **Classical pathway activation:** C1q binds to antibodies on nerve surfaces, initiating the complement cascade
- This produces opsonins (C3b, C4b), anaphylatoxins (C3a, C5a), and the membrane attack complex (MAC; C5b-9)
- MAC directly lyses Schwann cell membranes (in AIDP) or axonal membranes at nodes of Ranvier (in AMAN)
- C3d and MAC deposition detectable on Schwann cell surfaces in AIDP patients
- Anti-myelin antibodies peak prior to activated complement levels, confirming antibody-mediated complement attack
- Blocking C1q prevents downstream tissue inflammation and damage

**Key reference:** Sawai S, et al. "Evolving understanding of Guillain-Barre syndrome pathophysiology and the central role of the classical complement pathway in axonal injury." *Front Neurol.* 2025. PMC12117664.

#### In IgA Deficiency:
IgA has unique complement interactions:
- IgA generally cannot fix complement via the classical pathway (unlike IgG and IgM)
- Secretory IgA clears antigens while provoking little inflammation
- IgA can activate complement via the **alternative and lectin pathways** (not classical)
- In IgA deficiency, the absence of this anti-inflammatory immunoglobulin may shift the immune response toward more complement-activating IgG responses
- Compensatory IgG production against mucosal antigens can activate the classical complement pathway, potentially contributing to systemic inflammation

### 5.2 T-Cell Regulation

#### In GBS:
- **Th1/Th17 axis:** Circulating Th1, Th17, and Th22 cells are significantly elevated
- **Cytokine profile:** Elevated plasma IL-17 and IL-22; elevated CSF IFN-gamma, IL-4, IL-17, IL-22
- **Correlation with severity:** CSF IL-17 and IL-22 levels correlate with GBS disability scores
- **CD4+ dysregulation:** Helper T cell dysregulation persists throughout the disease course
- **Th1 proinflammatory activity** upregulated at the initial phase
- **T-cell infiltration** of myelin sheath during acute phase
- **Treg insufficiency:** Mitigated Treg cells with augmented Th17 contribute to disease severity
- IVIG treatment downregulates Th17/Th22 cells and reduces IL-17/IL-22 levels

**Key references:**
- Li S, et al. "Circulating Th17, Th22, and Th1 Cells Are Elevated in the Guillain-Barre Syndrome and Downregulated by IVIg Treatments." *Mediators Inflamm.* 2014. PMC4036596.
- Nyati KK, et al. "Role of Cytokines and Toll-Like Receptors in the Immunopathogenesis of Guillain-Barre Syndrome." *Mediators Inflamm.* 2014. PMC4189947.
- Liang SL, et al. "Th1/Th2/Th17/Treg cytokines in Guillain-Barre syndrome and experimental autoimmune neuritis." *Cytokine Growth Factor Rev.* 2013;24(5):443-453.

#### In IgA Deficiency:
- Enhanced follicular CD4 T helper cell (Tfh) frequency and activation
- Altered CD8 T cell activation state
- Elevated levels of inflammatory cytokines
- Abnormally augmented calcium signaling in B cells from Peyer's patches
- These T-cell abnormalities parallel the Th17/Th1 skewing seen in GBS

### 5.3 B-Cell Maturation

#### In IgA Deficiency/CVID Spectrum:
Defective B-cell maturation is the central pathogenic mechanism:
- **TACI mutations:** Found in 20-30% of CVID patients; TACI (transmembrane activator calcium-modulator and cyclophilin ligand interactor) impacts B cell maturation
- **BAFF/APRIL signaling:** BAFF and APRIL binding to TACI, BAFF-R, and BCMA normally facilitates T cell-independent IgA class switching
- **NF-kappaB pathway:** BAFF signaling through TACI activates canonical NF-kappaB pathway, driving class switch recombination
- **Class-switch recombination defects:** Impaired in both IgAD and CVID
- Additional genetic defects: NFKB1, NFKB2, ICOS, CD19

#### In GBS:
- B cells produce autoantibodies that cross-react with axon gangliosides
- These autoantibodies perpetuate axonal degeneration by activating macrophages and the complement system
- Peripheral blood mononuclear cells responsive to GM1 and GM3 gangliosides

### 5.4 Cytokine Profiles

| Cytokine/Mediator | Role in GBS | Role in IgAD-Associated Autoimmunity |
|-------------------|-------------|--------------------------------------|
| IL-17 | Elevated in serum/CSF; correlates with severity | Elevated in autoimmune comorbidities (T1D, RA) |
| IL-22 | Elevated; correlates with disability | Involved in mucosal immunity |
| IFN-gamma | Elevated in CSF; Th1-driven | Elevated inflammatory cytokines in IgAD |
| TNF-alpha | Drives demyelination in AIDP | Elevated in CVID-associated autoimmunity |
| IL-6 | Elevated; drives inflammatory responses | Drives B-cell differentiation |
| IL-4 | Elevated in CSF | Involved in B-cell class switching |

### 5.5 Toll-Like Receptor (TLR) Involvement

TLRs play a role in both conditions:
- In GBS, TLRs contribute to the immunopathogenesis by recognizing pathogen-associated molecular patterns
- In IgAD, the absence of mucosal IgA leads to increased microbial translocation, increasing TLR stimulation
- This creates a pro-inflammatory feedback loop that may contribute to autoimmune disease development in both conditions

## 6. Clinical Implications

### 6.1 IVIG Treatment in IgA-Deficient GBS Patients

This is the most direct and clinically urgent intersection of GBS and IgA deficiency:

**Risk:** IVIG is contraindicated in patients with anti-IgA antibodies and selective IgA deficiency due to anaphylaxis risk:
- Anti-IgA antibodies found in 20-40% of IgA-deficient patients
- Anaphylaxis incidence: 0.23 per 10,000 IVIG administrations
- Most anaphylactic reactions occurred with products containing >50 micrograms/mL IgA

**Management strategies:**
1. **Pre-treatment screening:** Screen immunoglobulin levels and anti-IgA antibodies before IVIG administration
2. **Low-IgA IVIG products:** Most modern IVIG products have very low IgA levels; use products with <50 micrograms/mL IgA
3. **Premedication:** Antihistamine and hydrocortisone in patients with known IgG anti-IgA antibodies
4. **Alternative therapy:** Plasmapheresis (plasma exchange) as an equally efficacious alternative that avoids IgA exposure entirely
5. **Subcutaneous immunoglobulin (SCIG):** May be an option for immune support in IgAD patients

**Key references:**
- Brannagan TH. "Intravenous immunoglobulin in the Guillain-Barre syndrome. Products with low IgA content may be used in patients with total IgA deficiency." *BMJ.* 1998;316(7131):624. PMC2352877.
- Quinti I, et al. "IgA deficiency and the risk of transfusion of human blood products." *AAAAI Ask the Expert.* 2023.

### 6.2 Plasmapheresis as an Alternative

In IgA-deficient GBS patients, plasmapheresis may be the preferred first-line treatment:
- Removes pathogenic antibodies, cytokines, and chemokines without introducing IgA
- Equally efficacious as IVIG when started within 2 weeks of symptom onset
- Higher adverse event rate and post-treatment worsening than IVIG in general populations
- Higher patient discontinuation rate than IVIG

**Key reference:** Chevret S, et al. "Plasma exchange (PE) versus intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) for the treatment of Guillain-Barre syndrome (GBS) in patients with severe symptoms: A systematic review and meta-analysis." *J Clin Apher.* 2023. PMC10242495.

### 6.3 Treatment Considerations with Multiple Autoimmune Conditions

**Corticosteroids:** Do not hasten recovery from GBS or affect prognosis when administered alone. This is notable because corticosteroids are standard treatment for many autoimmune conditions (SLE, RA, IBD, autoimmune thyroid disease). The poor steroid response in GBS distinguishes it from typical chronic autoimmune diseases.

**Immunosuppressants:** No evidence supports efficacy of immunosuppressants/immunomodulators in GBS, unlike in many chronic autoimmune diseases. This creates treatment complexity in patients with GBS concurrent with conditions requiring immunosuppression.

**Multiple autoimmune syndrome considerations:**
- Patients with pre-existing autoimmune disease may have altered immune responses affecting GBS presentation and recovery
- Women with rheumatological disease have 7x the risk of developing GBS
- Pre-existing immunosuppressive therapy may modify GBS course
- Anti-TNF therapy for Crohn's disease has been implicated in some GBS cases (causation vs. coincidence debated)

### 6.4 Prognosis

GBS prognosis in the general population:
- Mortality: 3-7% overall; approximately 20% for ventilated patients
- Long-term disability common: weakness, neuropathic pain, fatigue, psychiatric symptoms
- Complications: cardiac arrhythmia, infections, blood clots, autonomic dysfunction

Factors that may complicate prognosis in patients with multiple autoimmune conditions:
- Pre-existing immune dysregulation may affect recovery kinetics
- Concurrent immunosuppressive therapy may modify immune reconstitution
- IgA deficiency limits treatment options (IVIG contraindication)
- CVID progression from IgAD adds further immune compromise

### 6.5 Screening Recommendations

For patients with known IgA deficiency presenting with acute weakness:
1. Consider GBS in differential diagnosis
2. Screen for anti-IgA antibodies before administering blood products or IVIG
3. Have plasmapheresis available as alternative to IVIG
4. Monitor for concurrent autoimmune conditions (thyroid function, celiac serology, glucose)
5. Monitor for CVID progression (repeat immunoglobulin levels)

For patients with GBS and known autoimmune conditions:
1. Screen immunoglobulin levels (including IgA) before IVIG
2. Evaluate for additional autoimmune comorbidities
3. Consider the autoimmune condition as a potential contributing factor in treatment-resistant cases
4. Distinguish between "SLE-related GBS" and "pure GBS" for prognostic purposes

## Summary of Key Findings

1. **GBS is typically not considered a comorbidity of chronic autoimmune disease**, but documented associations exist with SLE, Sjogren's, Hashimoto's, IBD, celiac disease, RA, and sarcoidosis. The distinction between "autoimmune disease-related GBS" and "pure GBS" is clinically important.

2. **IgA deficiency is strongly associated with multiple autoimmune conditions**, particularly celiac disease (6.57%), IBD (4.01%), RA (3.80%), T1D, SLE, autoimmune thyroid disease, and autoimmune cytopenias.

3. **Six conditions appear in both GBS and IgAD association lists:** SLE, T1D, autoimmune thyroid disease, IBD, celiac disease, and RA. These overlapping conditions share features of the ancestral HLA-B8/DR3/DQ2 (8.1) haplotype.

4. **IgA deficiency fits within the CVID spectrum** and can progress to CVID. The 42% rate of neurological conditions in CVID suggests a broader immune dysregulation that can encompass the nervous system. IgAD is also a component of APS-2.

5. **Shared molecular mechanisms** include complement activation (classical pathway in GBS; compensatory IgG-mediated complement activation in IgAD), T-cell dysregulation (Th17/Th1 elevation in both), and defective mucosal immunity potentially amplifying molecular mimicry triggers.

6. **The most critical clinical implication** is the contraindication of standard IVIG products in IgA-deficient GBS patients due to anaphylaxis risk. Plasmapheresis or low-IgA IVIG products must be used instead. Pre-treatment IgA screening should be standard practice.
