## 1. ADEM-GBS Overlap Syndrome: Definition and Pathogenesis

### 1.1 What Is ADEM-GBS Overlap?

Acute disseminated encephalomyelitis (ADEM) and Guillain-Barre syndrome (GBS) are commonly recognized as separate entities involving different parts of the nervous system — ADEM affects the central nervous system (CNS) while GBS affects the peripheral nervous system (PNS). However, they share fundamental features: *[Level 4 — case series, expert review]*

- **Autoimmune pathogenesis** with post-infectious immune-mediated demyelination
- **Myelin as the target** — both involve autoimmune attack on myelin
- **Common triggers** — preceding viral infections or vaccinations
- **Shared immunological markers** — significant increases in activated CD4+ and helper inducer cells have been observed in both GBS and ADEM

> **Citation:** Hamaguchi K. Guillain-Barre syndrome and acute disseminated encephalomyelitis (ADEM). *Rinsho Shinkeigaku*. 1996;36(12):1308-9. PMID: 9128388

Hamaguchi (1996) proposed categorizing GBS, Fisher syndrome, and ADEM together as "acute immunological nervous diseases" rather than treating them as entirely separate entities, based on finding anti-P2 protein antibodies and sensitized lymphocytes in both conditions.

### 1.2 Pathogenic Mechanism: Shared Epitope Hypothesis

The simultaneous occurrence of ADEM and GBS may be explained by **molecular mimicry targeting a shared epitope between peripheral and central nervous system myelin**. This means the same immune response triggered by an infection or vaccination can simultaneously attack both CNS and PNS myelin. *[Level 4 — pathogenesis hypothesis based on case series]*

> **Citation:** Bernard G, Riou E, Rosenblatt B, Dilenge ME, Poulin C. Simultaneous Guillain-Barre syndrome and acute disseminated encephalomyelitis in the pediatric population. *J Child Neurol*. 2008;23(7):752-757. PMID: 18354151

### 1.3 Combined Central and Peripheral Demyelination (CCPD)

CCPD is a broader term encompassing syndromes where both CNS and PNS demyelination occur simultaneously or sequentially. It includes:

- ADEM + GBS overlap
- MS + CIDP overlap
- MOGAD with peripheral nerve involvement
- Unclassified combined demyelination

In a landmark cohort study, Cortese et al. (2016) characterized 31 CCPD patients: *[Level 3 — retrospective cohort]*

- **65% presented post-infectiously** as myeloradiculoneuropathy, encephalopathy, cranial neuropathy, or pseudo-GBS
- **74%** demonstrated demyelinating peripheral neuropathy meeting EFNS/PNS criteria for CIDP
- **46%** showed brain MRI lesions meeting McDonald MS criteria
- **Two-thirds** had relapsing or progressive disease course
- **71%** were left significantly disabled

> **Citation:** Cortese A, Franciotta D, Alfonsi E, et al. Combined central and peripheral demyelination: Clinical features, diagnostic findings, and treatment. *J Neurol Sci*. 2016;363:182-187. PMID: 27000248

## 2. Epidemiology and Prevalence

### 2.1 How Common Is ADEM-GBS Overlap?

The overlap is **rare but well-documented**. Key prevalence data:

| Metric | Data | Source |
|--------|------|--------|
| **Largest literature review** | 66 cases of GBS + acquired demyelinating syndrome (ADS) | Mao & Hu 2014, PMID: 24914906 |
| **Temporal pattern** | 85% of overlap cases identified within 4 weeks of initial diagnosis | Mao & Hu 2014 |
| **Most common ADS in GBS** | Transverse myelitis (32%), followed by ADEM | Mao & Hu 2014 |
| **CNS lesions in pediatric GBS** | 3 of 9 brain MRIs showed demyelination | Hausler et al. 2013 |
| **CNS lesions in adult GBS** | 1 of 9 adult brain MRIs showed combined supra/infratentorial CNS demyelination | Hausler et al. 2013 |
| **ADEM incidence in adults** | ~0.4/100,000/year (much rarer than in children) | Li et al. 2022 |

> **Citation:** Mao Z, Hu X. Clinical characteristics and outcomes of patients with Guillain-Barre and acquired CNS demyelinating overlap syndrome: a cohort study based on a literature review. *Neurol Res*. 2014;36(12):1106-1113. PMID: 24914906

> **Citation:** Hausler M, Claeys K, Schoberer M, Busch V, Rostasy K, Nolte K. Central nervous system demyelination complicating Guillain-Barre syndrome. *Neuropediatrics*. 2013. DOI: 10.1055/s-0033-1337709

### 2.2 ADEM in Adults

A systematic review and meta-analysis of 437 adults across 12 studies found: *[Level 2 — systematic review]*

- Mean age 37.1 years; 41.7% male
- Preceding infection in 49.8% (upper respiratory tract infection: 25.7%)
- Delay of 12.5 days post-infection
- **Mortality rate: 7.8%** (95% CI: 3.3-13.5%)
- **Residual neurological deficits: 47.5%** (95% CI: 31.8-63.4%)
- Adults have worse outcomes than children (higher ICU rates, longer hospitalization, higher mortality)

> **Citation:** Li K, Li M, Wen L, et al. Clinical presentation and outcomes of acute disseminated encephalomyelitis in adults worldwide: systematic review and meta-analysis. *Front Immunol*. 2022;13:870867.

## 3. Diagnostic Criteria for ADEM-GBS Overlap

### 3.1 ADEM Diagnostic Criteria (International Pediatric MS Study Group, adapted for adults)

**There are no set diagnostic criteria for ADEM in adults.** Diagnosis is based on: *[Level 5 — consensus/expert opinion]*

**Required clinical features:**
- **Encephalopathy** (altered mental status, confusion, psychosis, somnolence) — this is *essential*
- **Polyfocal neurological deficits** (motor/sensory impairment, brainstem involvement, seizures)

**Required MRI features:**
- Multiple, widespread, bilateral (but asymmetric) white matter lesions
- Lesions are characteristically **ill-defined** (vs. MS: well-defined margins)
- Lesions appear **approximately the same age** (vs. MS: varying ages)
- **Thalamic involvement** more common in ADEM than MS
- **Periventricular white matter** may be involved (43.2% of adults) but less prominently than subcortical white matter (41.9%)

**ADEM MRI patterns (4 proposed patterns):**
1. Small lesions (<5 mm)
2. Large, confluent, or tumefactive lesions with perilesional edema
3. Symmetric deep gray matter involvement (thalamus, basal ganglia)
4. Acute hemorrhagic encephalomyelitis

### 3.2 GBS Diagnostic Criteria (Brighton Collaboration)

Standard GBS criteria (which Madeleine already meets):
- Progressive bilateral weakness of limbs
- Decreased/absent deep tendon reflexes
- Disease course <4 weeks
- CSF: albuminocytologic dissociation (elevated protein, normal cells)
- Electrodiagnostic evidence of neuropathy

### 3.3 Overlap Diagnosis

Diagnosis of ADEM-GBS overlap requires: *[Level 4 — case series-based consensus]*

1. **Meeting criteria for GBS** (clinical + electrodiagnostic + CSF)
2. **Plus** MRI evidence of CNS demyelination consistent with ADEM
3. **Plus** temporal association (both occurring within 4 weeks)
4. **Plus** exclusion of alternative etiologies (infection, MS, NMOSD, MOGAD, vasculitis)

> **Key point:** The diagnosis is based on temporal association and exclusion of alternative etiologies, with electro-diagnosis (EMG/NCV) and MRI supporting the diagnosis.

> **Citation:** Mohammed RR, Jan MM. Co-morbid Guillain-Barre syndrome and acute disseminated encephalomyelitis. *Neurosciences (Riyadh)*. 2013;18(2):166-8. PMID: 23545617

## 4. White Matter Lesions on Brain MRI in GBS Patients

### 4.1 Prevalence and Significance

White matter lesions on brain MRI are **atypical** in GBS. The characteristic MRI finding in GBS is enhancement of the cauda equina nerve roots, not brain lesions. When brain white matter lesions are found in a GBS patient, several possibilities must be considered: *[Level 3 — cohort data]*

| Possibility | Characteristics | Likelihood |
|-------------|----------------|------------|
| **Nonspecific/incidental** | Small, few, subcortical; common in adults >50 with comorbidities | Most common |
| **PRES** (posterior reversible encephalopathy) | Bilateral parieto-occipital; related to dysautonomia/hypertension | Uncommon but documented |
| **ADEM overlap** | Multiple, widespread, bilateral, ill-defined; with encephalopathy | Rare |
| **Hashimoto encephalopathy** | Periventricular/subcortical WM hyperintensities; steroid-responsive | Consider with Hashimoto's history |
| **MOGAD** | Large lesions, poorly marginated, cortical involvement | Rare; test MOG-IgG |
| **Bickerstaff brainstem encephalitis (BBE)** | Brainstem/thalamus/basal ganglia T2/FLAIR hyperintensity | 11-30% of BBE have MRI findings |
| **Small vessel disease/migraine** | Punctate, deep white matter, sparing periventricular zone | Common incidental finding |

### 4.2 PRES in GBS — An Important Differential

GBS can be considered an **independent risk factor for PRES** due to:

- Acute dysautonomia causing hypertensive crises
- Pain-related sympathetic activation with consecutive arterial hypertension
- Cytokine production changing capillary permeability

**PRES MRI pattern:** Bilateral symmetrical subcortical hyperintense foci on T2/FLAIR, predominantly parieto-occipital, with nodular contrast enhancement. *[Level 4 — case series]*

> **Citation:** Joshi S, Prentice D, van Heerden J, Chemmanam T. Guillain-Barre syndrome and posterior reversible leukoencephalopathy syndrome: a rare association. *BMJ Case Rep*. 2020. PMID: 32317366

**Relevance to Madeleine:** She has documented severe dysautonomia (blood pressure instability, noradrenaline requirement, anisokori). PRES should be specifically evaluated on the MRI review.

### 4.3 Incidental/Nonspecific White Matter Lesions

In a 52-year-old woman with multiple autoimmune conditions, **small nonspecific white matter lesions are common** and may reflect:

- Age-related small vessel disease (present in >50% of elderly individuals)
- Migraine-associated changes
- Autoimmune-related microvascular changes (especially with Hashimoto's)
- Chronic hypertension effects

**Key distinguishing features from ADEM:**
- ADEM: multiple, widespread, bilateral, ill-defined, with clinical encephalopathy
- Nonspecific: few, small (<5mm), well-circumscribed, without corresponding symptoms

## 5. Differential Diagnosis of Small White Matter Lesions in This Context

Given the complete clinical picture (severe GBS + IgA deficiency + Hashimoto's + ventilator-dependent + white matter lesions on MRI), the differential diagnosis for the MRI findings includes:

### 5.1 Diagnostic Decision Tree

```
Brain MRI shows small white matter lesions
│
├── Are lesions MULTIPLE, WIDESPREAD, BILATERAL, ILL-DEFINED?
│   ├── YES + Encephalopathy present → Suspect ADEM overlap
│   │   └── Test: MOG-IgG, anti-ganglioside panel, repeat MRI in 2-4 weeks
│   └── NO (few, small, well-circumscribed) → Likely nonspecific
│       └── Consider: age-related, autoimmune vasculopathy, Hashimoto's
│
├── Are lesions primarily PARIETO-OCCIPITAL, BILATERAL?
│   ├── YES + History of BP instability → Suspect PRES
│   │   └── PRES is REVERSIBLE with BP control
│   └── NO → Less likely PRES
│
├── Are lesions in BRAINSTEM, THALAMUS, BASAL GANGLIA?
│   ├── YES + Encephalopathy → Consider BBE or ADEM
│   └── NO → Less likely BBE
│
├── Are lesions PERIVENTRICULAR, SUBCORTICAL?
│   ├── YES + Hashimoto's → Consider Hashimoto encephalopathy (SREAT)
│   │   └── Check anti-TPO, anti-Tg titers
│   │   └── Hashimoto encephalopathy is STEROID-RESPONSIVE
│   └── YES without Hashimoto context → Consider MS (unlikely in this setting)
│
└── Are lesions NEW compared to prior MRI (2026-03-23)?
    ├── YES → Actively evolving process (ADEM, PRES, or Hashimoto encephalopathy)
    └── NO (were present but not reported) → Likely chronic/incidental
```

### 5.2 Critical Question: Were Lesions Present on Earlier MRI?

Madeleine had a **normal brain MRI on 2026-03-23** (day 2 of illness). The second MRI on 2026-03-28 (day 7) showed findings. This temporal evolution is important:

- **If lesions are truly new:** Suggests an active process developing during hospitalization (ADEM, PRES, or Hashimoto encephalopathy are all possible)
- **If lesions were subtle and missed on first scan:** Less concerning, suggests chronic changes
- **ADEM lesions can develop 5-14 days after symptom onset** — delayed MRI is a recognized phenomenon (Marin & Callen, 2013)

## 6. CSF Findings: ADEM vs GBS vs Overlap

### 6.1 Comparison Table

| Parameter | GBS (classic) | ADEM (classic) | ADEM-GBS overlap |
|-----------|--------------|----------------|------------------|
| **Protein** | Elevated (>0.45 g/L); present in 70-80% | Mildly elevated (<1.0 g/L); present in 50-80% | Elevated |
| **Cell count** | Normal (<5 cells/uL) in 83% | Mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (usually <100 cells) in ~80% | Variable; may show both patterns |
| **Classic pattern** | Albuminocytologic dissociation | Mild pleocytosis + mild protein elevation | ACD suggests GBS predominance |
| **Myelin basic protein** | Usually normal | May be elevated (indicates CNS demyelination) | If elevated → suggests CNS component |
| **Oligoclonal bands** | Usually absent | Present in ~10-29% | Variable |

### 6.2 Implications for Madeleine

Madeleine's LP (2026-03-30) showed:
- **Elevated albumin** (elevated protein)
- **Normal cell count** (no pleocytosis)
- **No signs of CNS infection or inflammation**

**Interpretation:** Classic albuminocytologic dissociation confirming GBS. The **absence of pleocytosis** argues against classic ADEM. However:

- ADEM CSF can be normal in ~20% of cases
- The ACD pattern does not exclude coexisting ADEM — it confirms GBS is present
- In overlap cases, the GBS pattern may dominate the CSF profile
- Myelin basic protein (MBP) in CSF, if tested, could provide additional information about CNS demyelination

## 7. Treatment Implications — Corticosteroids in ADEM-GBS Overlap

### 7.1 Corticosteroids in Pure GBS: NO Benefit

The Cochrane review (8 trials, 653 participants) is clear: *[Level 1 — Cochrane/RCT]*

- **Corticosteroids alone do NOT hasten GBS recovery** (moderate quality evidence)
- **Oral corticosteroids may actually delay recovery** (very low quality evidence)
- **IV methylprednisolone + IVIg** showed borderline benefit in one trial after statistical adjustment, but this was not confirmed

> **Citation:** Hughes RAC, Brassington R, Gunn AA, van Doorn PA. Corticosteroids for Guillain-Barre syndrome. *Cochrane Database Syst Rev*. 2016;(10):CD001446. PMID: 27775812

### 7.2 Corticosteroids in Pure ADEM: FIRST-LINE Treatment

ADEM is very steroid-responsive: *[Level 3 — case series, guidelines]*

- **First-line:** IV methylprednisolone 20-30 mg/kg/day (max 1000 mg/day) for 3-5 days
- Followed by oral prednisone taper over 4-6 weeks
- **Second-line:** IVIg (2 g/kg over 2-5 days) if steroids fail
- **Third-line:** Plasma exchange or cyclophosphamide for refractory cases
- Half of ADEM patients achieve complete remission with corticosteroids

### 7.3 Corticosteroids in ADEM-GBS Overlap: THE KEY QUESTION

**This is the critical clinical decision point.** The evidence, while limited to case reports and small series, suggests: *[Level 4 — case series]*

| Study | Treatment | Outcome |
|-------|-----------|---------|
| Amit et al. 1992 (PMID: 1394285) | Corticosteroids alone | "Prompt clinical response followed by complete recovery" |
| Bernard et al. 2008 (PMID: 18354151) | IVIg or corticosteroids | 3/4 recovered favorably; 1 developed CIDP |
| Pagano et al. 2011 (PMID: 21745776) | Corticosteroids + IVIg | Motor and sensory recovery, independent ambulation at 6 months |
| Mohammed & Jan 2013 (PMID: 23545617) | IVIg then IV methylprednisolone | "Remarkable improvement" after adding steroids |
| Hoshino et al. 2012 (PMID: 22728497) | IVIg or corticosteroids | Recovery (first case with anti-ganglioside antibodies) |
| Deshmukh et al. 2015 (PMID: 25878749) | IVIg (2g/kg) + IV methylprednisolone (30 mg/kg/d x3d) | Significant improvement; ambulating with assistance within weeks |

**Key insight from case reports:** In cases where initial GBS treatment (PE or IVIg) failed to produce improvement, the **addition of IV methylprednisolone** for the ADEM component produced remarkable improvement. This is consistent with ADEM being steroid-responsive even when GBS is not.

### 7.4 Proposed Treatment Rationale for Overlap

```
IF ADEM-GBS overlap is confirmed/suspected:
│
├── The GBS component has already been treated (5x PE)
│   └── PE treats GBS (removes antibodies) but does NOT treat ADEM
│
├── The ADEM component has NOT been treated
│   └── ADEM requires corticosteroids as first-line
│   └── Neither PE alone nor IVIg alone is first-line for ADEM
│
├── Corticosteroids can be added WITHOUT worsening GBS
│   └── Cochrane: steroids don't help pure GBS but also don't clearly harm
│   └── Exception: oral steroids may delay recovery (avoid oral route)
│   └── IV methylprednisolone is the safe route
│
└── THEREFORE: If MRI review supports ADEM or overlap:
    └── IV methylprednisolone 1000 mg/day x 3-5 days is rational
    └── This targets the untreated ADEM component
    └── This does not negate the PE already given for GBS
    └── Follow with oral prednisolone taper (4-6 weeks)
```

### 7.5 Important Caveat: Mechanism of Steroid Harm in GBS

One study proposed that corticosteroids may worsen GBS by:
- Aggravating demyelination via macrophage-mediated Schwann cell destruction
- This mechanism is specific to **PNS demyelination** (GBS) and does NOT apply to CNS demyelination (ADEM)

> **Citation:** Zhu J, et al. Action mechanism of corticosteroids to aggravate Guillain-Barre syndrome. *Sci Rep*. 2015;5:13931.

This theoretical concern about steroid harm applies to the **GBS component** but not the **ADEM component**, further supporting targeted steroid use if ADEM is confirmed.

## 8. Connection to IgA Deficiency and Autoimmune Clusters

### 8.1 IgA Deficiency and Autoimmune Demyelinating Disease

Selective IgA deficiency (SIgAD) is associated with: *[Level 3 — cohort/meta-analysis]*

- **Autoimmune diseases in 20-30% of SIgAD patients**
- The most common autoimmune manifestations: celiac disease (6.6%), IBD (4.0%), rheumatoid arthritis (3.8%)
- Shared genetic susceptibility via **HLA-B8/DR3/DQ2** haplotype (see [Autoimmune Cluster document](/doc/04-related-autoimmune/Hashimoto-IgAD-GBS-Autoimmune-Cluster.md))

### 8.2 IgA and CNS Demyelination — A Novel Link

A recent finding connects IgA to CNS demyelination directly: **MOG-specific IgA antibodies** have been identified in patients who are double-seronegative for AQP4-IgG/MOG-IgG, suggesting that MOG-IgA may be a novel diagnostic biomarker. *[Level 4 — exploratory study]*

> **Citation:** (MOG-IgA in CNS demyelination, Neurology, 2023. PMID: 37548987)

**Potential relevance:** A patient with selective IgA deficiency who has *absent* IgA antibodies would, by definition, not produce MOG-IgA. This could paradoxically be protective against one pathway of CNS demyelination, but may also confound diagnostic testing.

### 8.3 The Quadruple Autoimmune Cluster

If ADEM-GBS overlap is confirmed, Madeleine's autoimmune profile expands to:

1. **Recurrent GBS** (PNS autoimmune demyelination)
2. **ADEM** (CNS autoimmune demyelination) — *if confirmed*
3. **Selective IgA deficiency** (primary immunodeficiency)
4. **Hashimoto's thyroiditis** (organ-specific autoimmunity)

All four conditions share the **HLA-B8/DR3/DQ2 susceptibility haplotype**, consistent with Multiple Autoimmune Syndrome (MAS) type 3. The addition of CNS demyelination would strengthen the evidence for a broad immune dysregulation phenotype rather than isolated organ-specific autoimmunity.

## 9. Hashimoto's Encephalopathy as Differential Diagnosis

### 9.1 Why Consider Hashimoto's Encephalopathy (HE/SREAT)?

Hashimoto's encephalopathy (also known as Steroid-Responsive Encephalopathy Associated with Autoimmune Thyroiditis, SREAT) is relevant because: *[Level 4 — case series]*

- Madeleine has **known Hashimoto's thyroiditis**
- HE can cause **white matter lesions on brain MRI** (diffuse/focal subcortical and periventricular hyperintensities in 26-42% of cases)
- HE is a diagnosis of exclusion, treatable with steroids
- **Prevalence:** ~2.1/100,000; female:male ratio 5:1; peak onset age 45-55 (matches Madeleine's profile)
- **Key feature:** Dramatic response to corticosteroids — the condition is defined by steroid responsiveness

### 9.2 HE vs ADEM: Distinguishing Features

| Feature | HE (SREAT) | ADEM |
|---------|-----------|------|
| **Age** | 45-55 peak | Any age; uncommon in adults |
| **Thyroid antibodies** | High anti-TPO/anti-Tg (defining) | Not associated |
| **MRI** | Normal in ~50%; nonspecific WM changes | Multiple widespread bilateral lesions (defining) |
| **CSF** | Usually normal or mild protein elevation; may have elevated anti-TPO | Pleocytosis + protein elevation |
| **Clinical** | Cognitive decline, seizures, psychosis | Polyfocal deficits + encephalopathy |
| **Treatment** | Steroids (defining response) | Steroids (first-line) |
| **Reversibility** | Lesions often reverse with steroids | Lesions may resolve or persist |

### 9.3 Relevance

If the white matter lesions are small and nonspecific (not meeting ADEM MRI criteria), Hashimoto's encephalopathy should be considered. Both conditions are **treated with corticosteroids**, so the therapeutic approach converges regardless.

> **Citation:** Ferracci F, Carnevale A. The neurological disorder associated with thyroid autoimmunity. *J Neurol*. 2006;253:1585-1589. PMID: 17063319

## 10. MOGAD (MOG Antibody-Associated Disease) as Differential Diagnosis

### 10.1 When to Test for MOG-IgG

All patients with CNS demyelinating events (optic neuritis, transverse myelitis, or ADEM) without typical MS brain lesions should be tested for MOG-IgG and AQP4-IgG. *[Level 3 — diagnostic criteria/consensus]*

**2023 MOGAD Diagnostic Criteria require:**
1. A core clinical demyelinating event (including ADEM)
2. Positive serum MOG-IgG (cell-based assay, titer >=1:100)
3. Exclusion of better diagnoses (including MS)

**MOGAD and CCPD:** Several cases of combined central and peripheral demyelination (CCPD) have been reported in MOGAD, making MOG-IgG testing relevant when both CNS and PNS are affected.

### 10.2 Timing Caveat

MOG antibodies may **decrease over time** and may not be detectable early in the disease or during remission. In adult ADEM, antibody detection may be fleeting. Testing should ideally be performed during or shortly after the acute phase.

### 10.3 Relevance to Madeleine

If brain MRI review suggests ADEM-type lesions, MOG-IgG testing is indicated. However:
- MOGAD is relatively rare in adults
- The clinical picture is dominated by GBS (PNS), not CNS
- IgA deficiency does not directly predict MOGAD

## 11. Prognosis: ADEM-GBS Overlap vs Pure GBS

### 11.1 Prognostic Data

| Outcome | Pure GBS | ADEM-GBS Overlap | CCPD (all types) |
|---------|----------|-----------------|------------------|
| **Favorable outcome** | 60-80% good recovery | 74% favorable (Mao & Hu 2014) | 29% (Cortese 2016) |
| **Mortality** | 3-7% | 7.8% for adult ADEM (Li 2022) | Not well established |
| **Residual deficits** | 20% significant disability | 47.5% in adult ADEM | 71% significant disability |
| **Ventilator dependence** | 20-30%; most recover | Limited data | Worse if combined |
| **Recovery time** | Weeks to months | Weeks (ADEM component often faster) | Prolonged |

### 11.2 Prognostic Factors in Overlap

From Mao & Hu (2014): *[Level 3]*

- **Poor prognosis predictor:** Sensory level deficit (OR = 0.182)
- **Favorable prognosis predictor:** Visual deficit (OR = 4.667)
- **Miller Fisher/ADS overlap** showed greater female predominance, higher mean age, and more frequent simultaneous onset

### 11.3 Key Prognostic Insight

**The ADEM component generally has a better prognosis than the GBS component**, especially when treated with corticosteroids. In cases where GBS is refractory (as in Madeleine's case), identifying and treating an ADEM component could provide an additional therapeutic avenue and improve overall outcomes.

> **Citation:** Amit R, Glick B, Itzchak Y, Dgani Y, Meyeir S. Acute severe combined demyelination. *Childs Nerv Syst*. 1992;8:354-356. PMID: 1394285 — Reported "prompt clinical response followed by complete recovery" with corticosteroids in combined ADEM-GBS.

### 12.2 The Steroid Decision Framework

```
Neurologist/Radiologist MRI Review (2026-03-31):
│
├── Findings consistent with ADEM (multiple, widespread, ill-defined lesions):
│   └── START IV methylprednisolone 1000 mg/day x 3-5 days
│   └── Follow with oral prednisolone taper (4-6 weeks)
│   └── This addresses the UNTREATED ADEM component
│   └── Monitor for response (motor improvement, MRI follow-up at 2-4 weeks)
│
├── Findings consistent with PRES (bilateral parieto-occipital):
│   └── Optimize blood pressure control
│   └── PRES is REVERSIBLE — lesions resolve with BP management
│   └── Steroids NOT specifically indicated for PRES
│   └── Follow-up MRI to confirm resolution
│
├── Findings consistent with Hashimoto encephalopathy:
│   └── Check anti-TPO/anti-Tg titers if not recent
│   └── START IV methylprednisolone (same protocol as ADEM)
│   └── HE is defined by steroid responsiveness
│
├── Findings nonspecific (few small lesions, no pattern):
│   └── Likely incidental/age-related/autoimmune microvasculopathy
│   └── Steroids NOT indicated for nonspecific lesions
│   └── Focus on GBS management (next-line therapy per existing protocol)
│
└── If uncertain:
    └── Consider diagnostic trial of IV methylprednisolone (3-5 days)
    └── Low risk: steroids don't clearly harm GBS (Cochrane)
    └── Potential benefit if any steroid-responsive component exists
    └── Monitor response carefully
```

### 12.3 Key Arguments FOR a Steroid Trial

1. **PE has failed** — additional pathology may explain non-response
2. **New MRI lesions** suggest an active process not addressed by PE
3. **Steroids treat ADEM, HE, and some autoimmune encephalitides** — all possible in this patient
4. **Steroids do NOT clearly harm GBS** (Cochrane: no benefit for GBS, but no clear harm from IV route)
5. **Steroid-responsive conditions** (ADEM, HE) are potentially reversible if treated early
6. **Autoimmune cluster** suggests broad immune dysregulation that may respond to immunosuppression
7. **Risk-benefit ratio favors trial** — patient is already ventilator-dependent and PE-refractory

### 12.4 Key Arguments AGAINST Steroids

1. **Lesions may be nonspecific** — steroids are ineffective for incidental findings
2. **Infection risk** — immunosuppression in ICU setting (though recent infection has cleared)
3. **Theoretical concern about steroid aggravation of GBS** (Zhu et al. 2015) — though this applies more to the PNS component
4. **Critical illness myopathy risk** — steroids combined with prolonged ICU stay can cause myopathy that mimics GBS weakness
5. **Masking other diagnoses** — steroid response could obscure the true pathology
