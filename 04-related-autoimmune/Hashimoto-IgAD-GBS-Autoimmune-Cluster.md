## 1. Hashimoto's Thyroiditis and IgA Deficiency

### 1.1 How Common Is This Combination?

Selective IgA deficiency (SIgAD) is the most common primary immunodeficiency in Caucasians, with a prevalence of approximately 1:600 in Northern European populations. Autoimmune thyroid diseases — including Hashimoto's thyroiditis (HT) and Graves' disease (GD) — are among the most common autoimmune conditions in the general population, with HT affecting 1-2% of the population.

**Key prevalence data:**

- **IgAD prevalence in Graves' disease:** In a landmark Swedish study *[Level 3 — kohortstudie, n=841]* screening 841 TRAb-positive Graves' patients, 14 had IgA deficiency (1:60) — a markedly elevated prevalence compared to 1:600 in the general population (P = 0.006). Across five studies totaling 1,045 GD patients, 17 IgAD cases were identified (1:61). (Jorgensen et al., *Mol Med*, 2011)
- **IgAD prevalence in autoimmune thyroid disease generally:** IgAD shows a markedly increased prevalence in Graves' disease, SLE, type 1 diabetes, and celiac disease. Thyroiditis (both Hashimoto's and Graves') is listed among the autoimmune diseases more common in SIgAD patients. (Jorgensen et al., 2011; Yazdani et al., *Scand J Immunol*, 2017)
- **Autoimmune disease prevalence in IgAD:** The overall prevalence of autoimmune diseases in SIgAD patients ranges from 5% to 30%, with the higher end (20-30%) in adult populations. (Yazdani et al., 2017)
- **Isolated case reports:** Hashimoto's thyroiditis with selective IgA deficiency has been reported as a defined clinical entity, though the precise nature of the association remained unclear — simple coincidence could not be excluded. (Hadithi et al., *Acta Chir Scand*, 1996; Takcuchi et al., 1992)
- **Animal model:** Selective IgA deficiency occurs naturally in the Obese Strain (OS) chicken, a model for spontaneous autoimmune thyroiditis, suggesting a fundamental biological link. (Grumet et al., *Nature*, 1975)

**Bottom line:** The co-occurrence of IgAD and autoimmune thyroid disease is significantly more common than chance alone would predict. The prevalence of IgAD in Graves' disease is approximately 10-fold higher than in the general population. While specific Hashimoto's-IgAD prevalence data are less robust, the shared genetic and immunological basis strongly supports a non-random association.

### 1.2 Shared Genetic Susceptibility (HLA Haplotypes)

The HLA-B8/DR3 (8.1 ancestral haplotype) is the critical genetic link:

- **The 8.1 ancestral haplotype (AH8.1):** HLA-A1, C7, B8, C4AQ0, C4B1, DR3, DQ2 — the most common Caucasian extended haplotype — is associated with multiple immunopathological diseases, including IgA deficiency, type 1 diabetes, SLE, dermatitis herpetiformis, CVID, myasthenia gravis, and autoimmune thyroid disease. (Price et al., *Immunol Rev*, 1999)
- **IgA deficiency:** Up to 45% of IgAD patients carry the HLA-B8, DR3, DQ2 haplotype versus ~16% in the general population. The primary association maps to HLA-DQB1*02 in the HLA Class II region. (Ferreira et al., *PLoS Genet*, 2012)
- **Hashimoto's thyroiditis:** HLA-DR3 is significantly more frequent in HT patients than in controls (P < 0.01). HLA-DR3 is associated with the atrophic form of thyroiditis. HLA-DR5 is more frequent in goitrous thyroiditis. (Farid et al., 1992; Weetman & McGregor, 1994)
- **Of 5 Graves' patients with IgAD where DNA was available, 4 carried HLA-DR3, DQ2** — a remarkable enrichment. (Jorgensen et al., 2011)
- **Non-MHC shared genes:** IFIH1 (interferon-induced helicase 1) and CLEC16A are associated with both IgAD and autoimmune thyroid disease. (Jorgensen et al., 2011)

### 1.3 Autoimmune Polyendocrine Syndrome Type 2 (APS-2) — Does This Triad Fit?

APS-2 is classically defined as autoimmune Addison's disease combined with either autoimmune thyroid disease or type 1 diabetes (or both). The most frequent combination is Addison's disease + Hashimoto's thyroiditis (Schmidt syndrome).

**For Madeleine's case:**
- APS-2 **requires** adrenal insufficiency (Addison's disease) — this must be excluded.
- Without Addison's disease, the combination is better classified as:
  - **APS-3:** Autoimmune thyroid disease + another autoimmune illness, excluding Addison's disease
    - **APS-3C:** Autoimmune thyroiditis + other organ-specific autoimmune disease (which could include neuropathy)
  - **Multiple Autoimmune Syndrome (MAS) Type 3:** Autoimmune thyroid disease + immune-mediated neuropathy + immunodeficiency
  - **Polyautoimmunity:** Two or more autoimmune diseases in one patient; three or more = MAS

**Screening for APS-2 components should include:**
- Morning cortisol and ACTH (to exclude Addison's)
- Fasting glucose and HbA1c (type 1 diabetes risk)
- Anti-21-hydroxylase antibodies (adrenal autoimmunity)
- Anti-GAD and anti-IA2 antibodies (pancreatic autoimmunity)
- Anti-parietal cell antibodies, vitamin B12 (pernicious anemia)

## 2. Hashimoto's Thyroiditis and GBS

### 2.1 Association: Evidence

The association between Hashimoto's thyroiditis and GBS is classified as a **rare but documented clinical entity**.

**Case reports and series:**
- A 44-year-old woman with no prior medical history developed flaccid quadriplegia with absent reflexes. CSF showed albuminocytologic dissociation (protein 128 mg/L), EMG confirmed AIDP. Anti-TPO was elevated at 229.76 IU/mL (normal <5.6). All infectious triggers were negative. She responded to IVIg. (Chtaou et al., *Pan Afr Med J*, 2015)
- A 73-year-old patient with subclinical Hashimoto's died from GBS complications (tetraplegia, cranial neuropathies, autonomic instability). (Pollak et al., *J Neurol*, 1986)
- A case of GBS with transition from Hashimoto's to Graves' disease — the first such reported case. (Li et al., *BMC Endocr Disord*, 2022)
- Cases of AIDP masked by autoimmune thyroiditis. (JES, 2021)

**Epidemiological data:**
- Papadopoulou et al. (2024) concluded that GBS is "not typically associated with any autoimmune disease" but noted the overlap is well-documented in case reports.
- The concurrence of autoimmune thyroiditis with acquired demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy (GBS or CIDP) is "less frequently reported" than expected given the high prevalence of thyroid autoimmunity. (Bussa et al., *Clin Chem Lab Med*, 2001)

### 2.2 Shared Autoimmune Mechanisms

The proposed pathophysiological link between Hashimoto's and GBS centers on **ganglioside molecular mimicry:**

- Both thyrocyte and neuronal cell plasma membranes are rich in gangliosides.
- Autoantibodies against thyroid peroxidase (anti-TPO) may cross-react with neuronal gangliosides due to shared glycolipid epitopes.
- This provides a biologically plausible mechanism for autoantibodies targeting thyrocytes to also attack peripheral nerve myelin or axonal membranes.

### 2.3 Thyroid Function and GBS Severity

**Critical findings:**

1. **Thyroid autoantibody positivity in GBS is remarkably high:**
   - 70.1% of GBS patients (68/97) were thyroid autoantibody positive, compared to 14.1% in CIDP patients (P < 0.01).
   - TT4, TPO-Ab, and TG-Ab levels in GBS were all higher than in CIDP and controls (P < 0.01).
   (Wang et al., *Front Neurol*, 2020)

2. **TSH is an independent risk factor for recurrent GBS:** *[Level 3 — retrospektiv analys]*
   - TSH > 3.87 uIU/mL predicted GBS recurrence with **88.9% sensitivity and 90.4% specificity**.
   - Multivariate regression: antecedent illness, AIDP subtype, and high TSH were independently associated with RGBS.
   (Wang et al., *Muscle Nerve*, 2022; PMID: 35194803)

3. **Abnormal thyroid function predicts worse GBS outcomes:**
   - GBS patients with abnormal thyroid function had: longer hospitalization, higher frequency of cranial nerve damage, higher incidence of weakened tendon reflexes.
   - MRC scores (muscle strength) were lower at admission, nadir, and discharge.
   - Hughes disability scores were higher at admission and discharge.
   (The prediction effects of thyroid function in the severity of GBS, *Neurol Sci*, 2022)

4. **Thyroid hormone levels and GBS prognosis (2025):**
   - A retrospective study of 182 GBS patients (2019-2024) found that thyroid hormone levels correlated with inflammatory markers and functional outcomes measured by Hughes scale at 3 months post-discharge.
   (Huang et al., *Health Sci Rep*, 2025)

5. **Earlier findings:**
   - GBS patients had lower TSH and higher FT4 and FT4/FT3 ratios compared to healthy controls (within normal range). TSH levels correlated with disease severity on the Hughes Functional Grading Scale.
   (Huang et al., *Int J Neurosci*, 2017; n=90 GBS, 104 controls)

### 2.4 Clinical Significance for Madeleine

The combination of Hashimoto's thyroiditis with recurrent GBS is especially concerning because:
- TSH > 3.87 is an independent risk factor for GBS recurrence (high sensitivity/specificity)
- Hypothyroidism (undertreated Hashimoto's) worsens GBS severity markers
- 70% of GBS patients have positive thyroid autoantibodies — this is not coincidental
- **Maintaining optimal thyroid function may be a modifiable factor in reducing recurrence risk and improving outcomes**

## 3. Levothyroxine Management During Critical Illness / ICU

### 3.1 Non-Thyroidal Illness Syndrome (NTIS) / Sick Euthyroid Syndrome

**Prevalence and pattern:**
- NTIS affects approximately 75% of hospitalized patients in ICU settings.
- Typical hormone pattern: low T3, low or normal T4, elevated reverse T3 (rT3), normal or slightly decreased TSH.
- These changes begin very rapidly — serum T3 decreases within 2 hours of acute stress onset.
- NTIS severity correlates with illness severity and mortality risk.

**Interpretation:**
- A normal TSH in a critically ill patient excludes primary hypothyroidism.
- TSH > 20 uIU/mL or undetectable TSH makes NTIS less likely — consider true thyroid dysfunction.
- Elevated TSH with low T4 in the ICU favors primary hypothyroidism requiring treatment.

**NTIS in GBS specifically:**
GBS patients who require ICU care (ventilator-dependent, autonomic instability) will develop NTIS as part of the critical illness response. In a patient with pre-existing Hashimoto's on levothyroxine, distinguishing NTIS from undertreated hypothyroidism becomes crucial.

### 3.2 Should Levothyroxine Be Continued During ICU Stay?

**Unequivocal answer: YES.**

> "Levothyroxine treatment should be continued during a patient's stay in the ICU in those who are known to have hypothyroidism."
> — Van den Berghe, *Thyroid*, 2014

- There is no evidence-based consensus supporting thyroid hormone treatment of NTIS in previously euthyroid patients.
- However, **for patients with pre-existing hypothyroidism, levothyroxine must not be interrupted.**
- Failure to reinstate thyroid replacement therapy during hospitalization is a recognized risk factor for myxedema coma. (StatPearls, Myxedema Coma, 2024)

### 3.3 IV Levothyroxine When Enteral Route Not Available

For an intubated/ventilated GBS patient who cannot take oral medications:

**Option 1: Enteral via nasogastric/orogastric tube**
- Preferred if a feeding tube is in place.
- **Liquid formulation is superior to crushed tablets:** no difference in TSH/FT4/FT3 before and after treatment with liquid levothyroxine; it can be administered directly through the tube without empty stomach requirement. (Ribas et al., *J Endocrinol Invest*, 2014)
- **Crushed tablets** may work but with some drug loss during crushing and transfer.
- **Critical interaction:** More than half of patients receiving concurrent levothyroxine + continuous enteral nutrition developed subclinical or overt hypothyroidism. In one study, 6/13 patients developed overt hypothyroidism within 2-3 weeks. (Dickerson et al., *Pharmacotherapy*, 2010)
- **Protocol:** Hold tube feeding for 1 hour before and 1 hour after levothyroxine administration.
- **Monitor:** Weekly TSH/FT4 when using concurrent enteral nutrition.

**Option 2: Intravenous levothyroxine**
- IV dose = 50-75% of oral dose (AACE/ATA guideline).
- Levothyroxine has a half-life of up to 7 days; therefore, immediate IV transition may not be clinically necessary in the acute NPO setting of a few days.
- **For prolonged inability to use enteral route:** IV levothyroxine 50-75% of usual oral dose once daily.
- **Cardiac monitoring required:** IV levothyroxine is associated with cardiac toxicity (arrhythmias, tachycardia, myocardial ischemia) particularly in the elderly and those with cardiovascular disease.
- Example: If oral dose is 100 mcg, IV dose would be 50-75 mcg/day.

### 3.4 Dose Adjustments During Critical Illness

- Critically ill hypothyroid patients may require **increased** levothyroxine doses — average increase of 54.4% was needed in one study to maintain normal TSH. (Increased levothyroxine requirements in critically ill patients, *Thyroid*, 2010)
- Factors increasing requirements: impaired GI absorption, protein binding changes, increased metabolism from fever/inflammation, drug interactions.

### 3.5 Monitoring TSH/T4/T3 in ICU

**Practical guidelines:**

| Test | When to Order | How to Interpret |
|------|--------------|-----------------|
| TSH | Admission, then weekly if known hypothyroid | Normal = no primary thyroid issue; >20 = likely true hypothyroidism; <0.1 = possible hyperthyroid or sick euthyroid |
| Free T4 | With TSH when abnormal | Low + high TSH = true hypothyroidism; Low + normal/low TSH = likely NTIS |
| Free T3 | Not routinely recommended | Low in virtually all ICU patients (NTIS); poor diagnostic value |
| Reverse T3 | Can help differentiate | Elevated in NTIS; normal/low in true hypothyroidism |

**Pitfalls:**
- TSH may paradoxically decrease during acute illness even in hypothyroid patients receiving dopamine or high-dose glucocorticoids.
- Heparin increases lipoprotein lipase activity, producing a 5-fold increase in FT4 levels — measure FT4 at least 1 hour after IV heparin or 10 hours after LMWH. (How medications affect thyroid function, *CMAJ*, 2005)
- Repeated testing in NTIS may be misleading; normalizing serum concentrations does not necessarily restore tissue thyroid hormone levels.

### 3.6 Risk of Myxedema Coma If Levothyroxine Is Interrupted

Myxedema coma is a life-threatening decompensation of hypothyroidism with mortality rates of 25-60%.

**Risk in ICU setting:**
- Failure to reinstate thyroid replacement therapy during hospitalization is a direct risk factor.
- Precipitating factors common in GBS ICU patients: infection, sedatives, hypothermia, hypoventilation.
- Signs may be masked by GBS symptoms: weakness, hypoventilation, hypothermia, altered consciousness.

**If myxedema coma develops:**
- IV T4 loading dose: 200-400 mcg, then 50-100 mcg/day IV.
- Consider adding IV T3: 5-20 mcg loading, then 2.5-10 mcg every 8 hours.
- **Administer IV hydrocortisone 100 mg before thyroid hormone** to prevent adrenal crisis (especially important given the polyautoimmune context — must exclude adrenal insufficiency).

### 3.7 Drug Interactions in ICU

| ICU Medication | Effect on Levothyroxine/Thyroid | Clinical Action |
|----------------|-------------------------------|-----------------|
| **Heparin** | Falsely elevates FT4 (displacement by free fatty acids) | Time FT4 measurement: >1h after IV heparin, >10h after LMWH |
| **Dopamine** | Suppresses TSH; may cause iatrogenic central hypothyroidism | Do not rely on TSH alone; monitor FT4 |
| **Glucocorticoids** (high dose) | Suppress TSH; inhibit T4-to-T3 conversion | TSH may be misleadingly low; monitor FT4 |
| **Phenytoin** | Reduces total and free T4 by 20-40% via hepatic enzyme induction | May need to increase levothyroxine dose; monitor TSH |
| **Carbamazepine** | Increases hepatic T4/T3 metabolism via CYP450 induction | Levothyroxine dose increase may be needed |
| **Furosemide** (high dose) | Displaces T4 from binding proteins | Transient FT4 increase |
| **Aluminum/magnesium antacids, sucralfate** | Impair levothyroxine absorption | Separate administration by 4 hours |
| **Proton pump inhibitors** | Reduce gastric acid, impair levothyroxine absorption | Monitor TSH; may need dose increase |

## 4. Thyroid Function and Neuromuscular Recovery

### 4.1 Thyroid Hormones Are Essential for Peripheral Nerve Regeneration

This is among the most directly relevant findings for Madeleine's case.

**Preclinical evidence (strong):**

1. **Schwann cell proliferation:** T3 increases Schwann cell proliferation and triggers phenotypic switching essential for regeneration. Schwann cells re-express thyroid hormone receptors (TRbeta) following nerve injury — this is a transient, regeneration-specific phenomenon. (Barakat-Walter, *Neural Regen Res*, 2018)

2. **Axonal regeneration:** T3 administration results in "more complete nerve regeneration, demonstrated by a significant increase in the number and diameter of axons." This represents a real improvement, not merely acceleration. (Barakat-Walter, 2018)

3. **Myelination:** T3 upregulates myelin-associated genes: myelin basic protein (MBP), protein zero (P0), and peripheral myelin protein 2 (PMP-2). Local T3 administration demonstrated "acceleration and improvement of the myelination of regenerating axons and subsequent reinnervating of denervated muscles." (Barakat-Walter, 2018)

4. **Neurotrophic factors:** Both BDNF (brain-derived neurotrophic factor) and NT3 (neurotrophin-3) are thyroid hormone-dependent and reduced in hypothyroid conditions. T3 upregulates BDNF, NT3, and NGF expression. (Barakat-Walter, 2018)

5. **Neuromuscular junction recovery:** T3 enhanced reinnervation of denervated end-plates, with higher recovery of end-plate size and complexity, and increased acetylcholine receptor density on postsynaptic membranes. (Barakat-Walter, 2018)

6. **Hypothyroid animals:** In hypothyroid neonatal rats, there was a reduction in the number of myelinated fibers and a delay in axon diameter increase in sciatic nerves. Historical studies (1910-1932) showed that "regeneration following peripheral nerve injury was retarded in thyroidectomized dogs, cats, rabbits and rats." (Barakat-Walter, 2018; Papakostas et al., *J Thyroid Res*, 2013)

**Clinical evidence:**
- A patient with subclinical hypothyroidism experienced painful neuropathy. After thyroid function normalization with levothyroxine, neuropathic pain progressively decreased, and skin biopsy at 6 and 12 months showed complete reinnervation of the epidermis. (Magri et al., *J Neurol Neurosurg Psychiatry*, 2009)
- Accelerated outgrowth of new axons and recovery of sensory conduction was documented in one patient with a transected ulnar nerve receiving T3. (Clinical case, cited in Barakat-Walter, 2018)

### 4.2 Clinical Implications for GBS Recovery

**The evidence strongly suggests that optimal thyroid status is a prerequisite for peripheral nerve recovery after GBS:**

- Hypothyroidism impairs Schwann cell function, reduces neurotrophic factor expression, slows myelination, and delays axonal regeneration.
- In a GBS patient with Hashimoto's thyroiditis, **suboptimal thyroid replacement could directly impair neurological recovery.**
- Target: maintain **euthyroid state** with TSH in the lower half of the normal range (0.5-2.5 mIU/L) to optimize nerve regeneration conditions.
- Avoid both hypothyroidism (impairs regeneration) and overreplacement/thyrotoxicosis (may increase metabolic demand without proportional benefit).

## 5. Triple Autoimmune Cluster — Treatment Implications

### 5.1 Polyautoimmunity and Multiple Autoimmune Syndrome (MAS)

**Classification of Madeleine's condition:**
- Three autoimmune diseases: Hashimoto's thyroiditis + selective IgA deficiency + Guillain-Barre syndrome
- This constitutes **Multiple Autoimmune Syndrome (MAS)**, defined as three or more autoimmune diseases in one patient
- Best classified as **MAS Type 3** (autoimmune thyroid disease + immune-mediated neuropathy + immunodeficiency)
- Also fits **APS-3C** (autoimmune thyroiditis + organ-specific autoimmune disease)

**Key statistic:** Approximately 25% of patients with one autoimmune disease will develop additional autoimmune diseases over time. The coexistence of autoimmune diseases contributes to a more severe disease course. Polyautoimmunity was observed in 34.4% of patients in one large cohort (373/1,083). (Rojas-Villarraga et al., 2012; Cojocaru et al., 2010)

### 5.2 Impact on Immunotherapy Decisions

**IVIg considerations:**
- IVIg is the standard first-line treatment for GBS.
- In IgA-deficient patients, IVIg carries an elevated risk of anaphylaxis due to anti-IgA antibodies. IgA-depleted IVIg products should be used (as documented in the existing knowledge base).
- IVIg is generally beneficial for autoimmune thyroid disease and does not trigger thyroid flares.

**Plasma exchange (PE) considerations:**
- PE is equally effective to IVIg for GBS.
- PE removes circulating immunoglobulins, autoantibodies, and complement — potentially beneficial for all three conditions simultaneously.
- PE may also remove thyroid hormones and levothyroxine from circulation — **monitor thyroid function after PE and potentially increase levothyroxine dose.**
- PE avoids the IgA-related anaphylaxis risk of IVIg.

**Rituximab considerations (if used for refractory GBS):**
- Rituximab-induced B cell depletion can worsen hypogammaglobulinemia.
- **Critical risk in IgA-deficient patient:** 19.3% of patients with normal IgG pre-rituximab develop hypogammaglobulinemia; IgA deficiency was observed in 33.5% of rituximab-treated patients.
- Pre-existing IgA deficiency is a risk factor for further immunoglobulin decline.
- **Pre-treatment baseline:** Check IgG, IgA, IgM, and B cell count before rituximab.
- **Monitoring:** Serial immunoglobulin levels every 3 months; consider immunoglobulin replacement if IgG drops below 4 g/L with recurrent infections.
(Barmettler et al., *JAMA Netw Open*, 2018; Kado et al., *Ann Allergy Asthma Immunol*, 2023)

**Corticosteroid considerations:**
- Not typically used for GBS (no proven benefit for standard GBS).
- If used for other autoimmune comorbidities, may suppress TSH — complicating thyroid monitoring.
- High-dose corticosteroids inhibit T4-to-T3 conversion.
- Consider adrenal insufficiency screening before initiating, given the polyautoimmune context.

### 5.3 Risk of Triggering Other Autoimmune Flares

- Immunosuppressive treatment for one condition may theoretically protect against flares of other autoimmune conditions by broadly suppressing immune activity.
- However, immune reconstitution after B cell-depleting therapy (rituximab) can trigger autoimmune flares as the immune system "reboots."
- **Thyroid monitoring is essential** during and after any immunosuppressive treatment.
- IVIg can modulate immune function broadly and may have beneficial effects on concurrent autoimmune conditions.

### 5.4 Screening for Additional Autoimmune Conditions

Given MAS/polyautoimmunity, comprehensive screening is recommended:

| Condition to Exclude | Test | Rationale |
|---------------------|------|-----------|
| Celiac disease | IgG-tTG, IgG-DGP (not IgA-based tests — she is IgA deficient!) | IgA-deficient patients are 10-20x more likely to develop celiac disease |
| Addison's disease | Morning cortisol, ACTH, anti-21-hydroxylase Ab | Exclude APS-2; risk of adrenal crisis |
| Type 1 diabetes | Fasting glucose, HbA1c, anti-GAD, anti-IA2 | Associated with HLA-DR3/DQ2 |
| Pernicious anemia | Anti-parietal cell Ab, vitamin B12, methylmalonic acid | Common in polyautoimmune thyroid disease; B12 deficiency causes neuropathy |
| SLE | ANA, anti-dsDNA, complement C3/C4 | Associated with IgAD and thyroid disease |
| Sjogren's syndrome | Anti-SSA/Ro, anti-SSB/La | Causes peripheral neuropathy |
| Vitiligo | Clinical examination | Common in autoimmune thyroid disease |
| Myasthenia gravis | Anti-AChR antibodies | Associated with HLA-B8/DR3; neuromuscular overlap |

**Important note on celiac testing:** Standard celiac screening uses IgA-based serological tests (IgA-tTG, IgA-EMA). These tests will be **falsely negative in IgA-deficient patients**. IgG-based tests (IgG-tTG, IgG-DGP) must be used instead. (Mayo Clinic Labs, 2021; Celiac Disease Foundation)

### 5.5 Monitoring Recommendations for Multi-Autoimmune Patients

- **Vigilance for new autoimmune diseases:** Lifelong surveillance, as autoimmune diseases tend to cluster over time.
- **Annual autoantibody screening:** Thyroid antibodies, anti-GAD, anti-parietal cell, ANA.
- **Regular organ function:** TSH/FT4 every 3-6 months; fasting glucose annually; B12 annually; morning cortisol if symptomatic.
- **Immunoglobulin monitoring:** Quantitative IgG, IgA, IgM at least annually, more frequently if on immunosuppressive therapy.
- **Multidisciplinary approach:** Coordinated care between neurology, endocrinology, immunology, and gastroenterology.

## 6. HLA and Genetic Overlap

### 6.1 The 8.1 Ancestral Haplotype (AH8.1)

The extended MHC haplotype HLA-A1, C7, B8, C4AQ0, C4B1, DR3, DQ2 — known as the 8.1 ancestral haplotype — is the single most important genetic link connecting IgA deficiency and Hashimoto's thyroiditis.

**Association strength by condition:**

| Condition | HLA Association | Strength |
|-----------|----------------|----------|
| **Selective IgA deficiency** | HLA-B8, DR3, DQ2 (AH8.1) — up to 45% of patients | Very strong |
| **Hashimoto's thyroiditis** | HLA-DR3 (atrophic form), DR5 (goitrous form) | Moderate-strong |
| **Graves' disease** | HLA-DR3, DQ2 | Strong |
| **Guillain-Barre syndrome** | No consistent HLA association | Weak/absent |
| **Type 1 diabetes** | HLA-DR3/DQ2, DR4/DQ8 | Very strong |
| **Celiac disease** | HLA-DQ2 (95%), DQ8 | Very strong |
| **Myasthenia gravis** | HLA-B8, DR3 (early onset) | Strong |
| **SLE** | HLA-DR3 | Strong |

### 6.2 GWAS Data on GBS

**Important finding:** Unlike IgA deficiency and Hashimoto's, GBS does **not** show consistent HLA associations.

- A genome-wide association study found "no significant associations of individual SNPs or imputed HLA types" in GBS. (Grompas et al., *J Neuroimmunol*, 2018)
- A meta-analysis of 9 case-control studies (780 GBS cases, 1,353 controls) found no significant HLA-DQB1 associations in Asian or Caucasian populations. (Blum et al., 2015)
- The genetic basis of GBS susceptibility may lie outside the HLA region, potentially involving genes related to molecular mimicry response (anti-ganglioside antibody generation) rather than classical autoimmune HLA associations.

### 6.3 Clinical Significance

The genetic architecture of this triple cluster suggests two distinct pathogenic streams converging in one patient:

1. **HLA-B8/DR3/DQ2-driven autoimmunity:** This haplotype drives IgA deficiency and Hashimoto's thyroiditis (and potentially increases risk for celiac disease, T1DM, and other AH8.1-associated conditions).

2. **Non-HLA immune dysregulation:** GBS susceptibility is primarily driven by molecular mimicry after infections, not classical HLA-mediated autoimmunity. However, the broadly dysregulated immune system created by HLA-B8/DR3 and IgA deficiency may create a permissive environment where GBS triggers are more likely to produce severe, recurrent disease.

**This means:** The patient's IgA deficiency and Hashimoto's likely share a common genetic root (AH8.1), while GBS is mechanistically distinct but may be more severe and recurrent due to the underlying immune dysregulation caused by the first two conditions.

## 7. Levothyroxine Dose Adjustment as a Potential GBS Trigger

### 7.1 Biological Plausibility

Thyroid hormones are direct immunomodulators. T3 and T4 regulate immune cells via thyroid hormone receptors on neutrophils, NK cells, macrophages, dendritic cells, T cells, and B cells (Montesinos & Pellizas, *Front Endocrinol* 2019; Mooij et al., *Eur J Endocrinol* 2022).

**Mechanisms linking thyroid hormone fluctuation to autoimmune activation:**
- T3 promotes dendritic cell maturation via TRβ1-dependent Akt/NF-κB signaling, upregulating MHC-II and costimulatory molecules while **downregulating PD-L1 and Treg populations**
- T3-activated dendritic cells increase IL-12, IL-6, IL-23, IL-1β, and TGFβ1 — driving Th1/Th17 differentiation (the exact effector lineages implicated in GBS)
- T4 enhances NK cell lytic activity and IFN-γ production
- GBS patients have lower TSH and higher FT4 than controls; FT4 levels correlate with severity (Li et al., *Int J Neurosci* 2017, 90 GBS vs 104 controls)

### 7.2 Over-Replacement vs Under-Replacement

| Scenario | Immune Effect | GBS Risk |
|----------|--------------|----------|
| **Over-replacement** (transient thyrotoxicosis) | Th1/Th17 shift, Treg suppression, DC activation | **Higher** — pro-inflammatory state favoring autoimmune attack |
| **Under-replacement** (persistent hypothyroidism) | Impaired Treg function, reduced immune surveillance | **Indirect** — immune dysregulation, less direct GBS trigger |

A dose increase creating even subclinical over-replacement could amplify the T3-driven Th1/Th17 cascade and suppress immune tolerance.

### 7.3 Timeline Consistency

The typical GBS trigger latency is **1–4 weeks** after an antecedent event (~70% of GBS cases have identifiable triggers in this window). A levothyroxine dose adjustment **2–3 weeks before onset** falls squarely within this expected range.

**Molecular mimicry pathway:** Thyrocyte and neuronal cell membranes are both rich in gangliosides. Autoantibodies against thyrocytes can cross-react with neuronal gangliosides (Diagne et al., *Pan Afr Med J* 2016; Asano & Kenzaka, *BMC Endocr Disord* 2022).

### 7.4 PE Removes Levothyroxine

Levothyroxine is ~90% protein-bound with a low volume of distribution — highly susceptible to removal by plasmapheresis. Each PE session removes approximately 50% of circulating free thyroid hormones (Gauna et al., *J Pers Med* 2023).

**In a Hashimoto's patient (minimal endogenous production): there is no endogenous rebound after PE-induced depletion.**

**Protocol:**
- Administer levothyroxine **AFTER** each PE session (not before, to avoid immediate removal)
- Monitor TSH, fT3, fT4 after each PE cycle
- Consider supplemental dosing during PE series (Mahmoud et al., *Neurocrit Care* 2020)
- Hypothyroidism during PE may impair GBS recovery (T3 required for nerve remyelination)

### 7.5 Clinical Relevance for This Patient

The patient's levothyroxine dose was adjusted 2–3 weeks before GBS onset. **No follow-up thyroid function tests were performed.** Combined with:
- TSH >3.87 as independent risk factor for GBS recurrence (Wang 2022)
- 70.1% thyroid autoantibody positivity in GBS (Zhang 2020)
- Ganglioside cross-reactivity in thyroid autoimmunity

This represents a **biologically plausible contributing trigger** that warrants:
1. Urgent TSH/fT4/fT3 measurement
2. Thyroid function monitoring during and after PE
3. Optimization of levothyroxine dosing for neurological recovery

## 8. Clinical Recommendations Summary

### 7.1 Immediate ICU Management (During Acute GBS)

1. **Continue levothyroxine** — never interrupt.
2. **Route:** Enteral via NG tube (liquid formulation preferred) with 1-hour feeding hold before and after. If enteral impossible: IV levothyroxine at 50-75% of oral dose.
3. **Thyroid monitoring:** TSH + FT4 on admission, then weekly. Use reverse T3 if unclear whether NTIS or true hypothyroidism.
4. **Drug interactions:** Account for heparin (false FT4 elevation), dopamine (TSH suppression), and any anticonvulsants.
5. **PE note:** If plasma exchange is used, monitor thyroid function after each cycle — PE removes levothyroxine and thyroid hormones from circulation.
6. **Myxedema prevention:** If levothyroxine has been missed for >3-5 days, consider IV loading dose after excluding adrenal insufficiency.

### 7.2 Optimizing Recovery

1. **Target euthyroid state** with TSH 0.5-2.5 mIU/L for optimal peripheral nerve regeneration.
2. **Monitor thyroid function more frequently** during recovery (monthly initially, then every 3 months).
3. **Ensure adequate T3 availability:** If FT3 is persistently low after acute illness resolution despite normal TSH, discuss T3 supplementation with endocrinology (emerging evidence for nerve repair benefits).

### 7.3 Long-Term Autoimmune Surveillance

1. **Screen for celiac disease** with IgG-based tests (critical given IgA deficiency).
2. **Exclude Addison's disease** (morning cortisol, anti-21-hydroxylase).
3. **Screen for pernicious anemia** (anti-parietal cell antibodies, B12, methylmalonic acid).
4. **Annual autoimmune panel:** TSH, FT4, fasting glucose, B12, immunoglobulin levels, ANA.
5. **TSH monitoring as GBS recurrence marker:** TSH > 3.87 uIU/mL is an independent risk factor for RGBS.

### 7.4 Treatment Decisions

1. **IVIg:** Use IgA-depleted products only; monitor for anaphylaxis.
2. **Plasma exchange:** Good alternative avoiding IgA risk; monitor thyroid function after each session.
3. **If rituximab considered:** Check baseline immunoglobulins; monitor for further immunoglobulin decline; anticipate higher hypogammaglobulinemia risk.
4. **Corticosteroids:** Not indicated for GBS itself; if used for other reasons, monitor thyroid function (TSH suppression, T4-to-T3 conversion inhibition).
