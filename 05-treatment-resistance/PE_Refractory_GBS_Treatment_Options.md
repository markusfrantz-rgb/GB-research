# Plasmapheresis-Refractory GBS: Comprehensive Treatment Options

## Clinical Context

## 1. When Plasmapheresis Fails in GBS -- Next Steps

### 1.1 Can IVIg Be Given AFTER Plasmapheresis?

**Short answer: Yes, but the evidence is complex.**

The 2023 EAN/PNS guideline recommends **against** combining PE followed immediately by IVIg as a planned sequential strategy, based on one Class I trial (the Dutch GBS trial) that found PE followed by IVIg showed no significant benefit over PE alone (Doorn et al., 2023). However, this recommendation pertains to planned sequential therapy, not to switching modalities after treatment failure.

**Critical distinction:** There is no specific guideline recommendation prohibiting IVIg when PE has clearly failed. The recommendation against sequential therapy was based on data from patients who received both as a combined strategy, not from cases of PE non-response followed by IVIg rescue.

**Timing considerations:**
- IVIg is most effective when started within 2 weeks of symptom onset (Strong recommendation, EAN/PNS 2023)
- The guideline suggests (Good Practice Point) IVIg may also be effective 2-4 weeks from onset of weakness
- If PE has been completed and IVIg is given, one should wait at least 48 hours after the last PE session to avoid PE washing out the administered IVIg

**Clinical reality:** In practice, when one modality fails, clinicians do switch to the other despite limited formal evidence. This is a pragmatic decision driven by the severity of the clinical situation.

### 1.2 Evidence for Switching from PE to IVIg

- There is **no RCT** that specifically studies switching from PE to IVIg after PE failure
- The 2025 systematic review by Roe et al. in *BMJ Neurology Open* found "insufficient evidence" regarding TPE after IVIg, and the reverse scenario (IVIg after PE failure) is similarly under-studied (Roe et al., 2025)
- **Case-level evidence** exists for both directions of switching, but no systematic data supports one approach over another
- **Important caution from the literature:** When PE is tried after IVIg, there may be a risk of rapid deterioration due to removal of IVIg by PE in partial responders. The reverse (IVIg after PE) does not carry this specific risk

### 1.3 2023 EAN/PNS Guideline Key Recommendations

From the European Academy of Neurology/Peripheral Nerve Society Guideline on diagnosis and treatment of Guillain-Barre syndrome (Doorn et al., Eur J Neurol 2023; J Peripher Nerv Syst 2023):

1. **IVIg 0.4 g/kg/day for 5 days** recommended for patients within 2 weeks of symptom onset (Strong recommendation) *[Level 1]*
2. **PE is effective** when started within 2-4 weeks of onset in severely affected patients
3. **Recommends against** a second IVIg course in GBS patients with poor prognosis -- based on the landmark SID-GBS trial (Walgaard et al., Lancet Neurol 2021; PMID: 33743237) *[Level 1 — RCT]*, which found no benefit and increased risk of adverse events from a second IVIg dose
4. **Recommends against** PE followed immediately by IVIg as combined sequential therapy
5. **Treatment-related fluctuation (TRF):** A repeated course of IVIg or PE can be considered in case of TRF (observational data only)
6. **No specific recommendation** exists for what to do when either first-line treatment fails completely -- this is the critical gap in current guidelines

### 1.4 The SID-GBS Trial -- Landmark Evidence

Walgaard C, Jacobs BC, et al. "Second intravenous immunoglobulin dose in patients with Guillain-Barre syndrome with poor prognosis (SID-GBS): a double-blind, randomised, placebo-controlled trial." *Lancet Neurology* 2021;20(4):275-283.

- **Design:** Double-blind, placebo-controlled RCT, n=93 (49 SID, 44 placebo), 59 hospitals in the Netherlands. Inclusion: mEGOS >=6 (poor prognosis) *[Level 1]*
- **Primary endpoint:** Adjusted common OR 1.4 (95% CI 0.6-3.3, p=0.45) -- NOT significant
- **SAE:** 35% in second-dose group vs 16% in placebo group in first 30 days, predominantly thromboembolic events
- **Deaths:** 4 deaths in the SID group (13-24 weeks post-randomization)
- This trial definitively ended the widespread practice of giving repeat IVIg to non-responders
- **However:** This trial studied repeat IVIg after initial IVIg failure, not IVIg after PE failure -- a different clinical scenario

### 1.5 Case Reports of PE-Refractory GBS Responding to Other Therapies

- Fulminant GBS refractory to IVIg successfully treated with TPE has been reported (MedCrave, Hepatology and Transplantation International Journal)
- A refractory GBS patient with multiple myeloma who failed PE, IVIg, AND anti-myeloma therapy was successfully treated with low-dose rituximab (PMC10969645)
- A GBS patient unresponsive to standard therapies responded to alemtuzumab (anti-CD52) in a single case report (2014)

**Key point:** Gamanorm contains a MODERATE amount of IgA. It is NOT an IgA-depleted product. The fact that the patient has tolerated it subcutaneously is clinically significant.

### 2.2 Does SCIG Tolerance Predict IVIg Safety?

**The evidence suggests SCIG tolerance provides PARTIAL but NOT complete reassurance:**

1. **Subcutaneous route is inherently safer for IgA-deficient patients.** A retrospective study found that approximately 25% of patients who had adverse reactions to IVIg had anti-IgA antibodies; when switched to SCIG, "they experienced no adverse effects" (NCBI Bookshelf NBK564340)

2. **Some patients who tolerated SCIG subsequently tolerated IVIg.** The proposed mechanism is formation of antibody-IgA complexes that are "quickly removed from the circulation" with the slower subcutaneous absorption rate

3. **However:** The subcutaneous route delivers immunoglobulin gradually into the lymphatic system, avoiding the rapid peak serum IgA levels that occur with intravenous bolus infusion. This means SC tolerance does NOT guarantee IV tolerance

4. **Anti-IgA antibody levels may decrease over time** during SCIG therapy, potentially making subsequent IVIg safer -- but this needs to be verified for this specific patient

5. **76.3% of IgA-deficient individuals with a history of anaphylactic transfusion reactions** tested positive for anti-IgA antibodies, compared to only 21.7% of asymptomatic IgA-deficient individuals (NCBI Bookshelf NBK564340)

### 2.3 Anti-IgA Antibody Testing

**Should be ordered NOW:**
- Anti-IgA antibodies of both IgG class AND IgE class should be tested
- IgE-class anti-IgA antibodies are most strongly associated with anaphylaxis risk
- IgG-class anti-IgA antibodies are more common but less predictive of severe reactions

**Turnaround time:**
- ARUP Laboratories (reference lab): **5-11 days** after specimen receipt -- this is the standard turnaround
- The ARUP anti-IgA antibody test (CPT 83520) has been **on delay since 03/18/2025** due to reagent/testing issues
- Newer surface plasmon resonance sensor technology can enable point-of-care detection within approximately 1 hour, but availability is limited
- **In a clinical emergency, empiric decision-making may be necessary before results return**

**Clinical pragmatism for this patient:**
- The patient has tolerated Gamanorm (SCIG containing IgA <= 82.5 mcg/mL) without reaction
- This makes high-titer anti-IgA antibodies LESS LIKELY (but does not exclude them)
- If IVIg is urgently needed, using an IgA-depleted product under close monitoring (with epinephrine and resuscitation equipment at bedside) is a reasonable approach while awaiting antibody testing

## 4. Efgartigimod (Vyvgart) -- Availability for GBS

### 4.1 European Approval Status

- **Approved in EU (August 2022)** for generalized myasthenia gravis (gMG) in AChR-antibody positive adults
- **Approved in EU (June 2025)** for chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy (CIDP) -- subcutaneous formulation
- **NOT approved for GBS** in any jurisdiction
- Available in Sweden through standard procurement for approved indications

### 4.2 Off-Label Use for GBS in Sweden

Off-label use is legally possible in Sweden when:
- No approved treatment alternative exists
- Clinical justification is documented
- The patient (or surrogate) provides informed consent
- The treating physician assumes clinical responsibility

Given that this patient has exhausted standard GBS treatments (PE without response, IVIg contraindicated/complicated by IgA deficiency), off-label efgartigimod use has a defensible clinical rationale.

### 4.3 Mechanism in GBS

Efgartigimod is a human IgG1 Fc fragment that antagonizes the neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn). FcRn normally recycles IgG, prolonging its half-life. By blocking FcRn, efgartigimod accelerates IgG catabolism, reducing total IgG levels (including pathogenic autoantibodies) by approximately 50-80% within the first treatment cycle. This provides a mechanistic rationale in antibody-mediated GBS.

### 4.4 Dosing Protocol for GBS (from case reports)

| Case Report | Dosing | Duration | Route |
|-------------|--------|----------|-------|
| Ripellino et al., 2025 (Eur J Neurol) | 10 mg/kg weekly | 4 doses | IV |
| Deng et al., 2025 (Front Immunol) | 10 mg/kg weekly | 2-5 doses | IV |
| BMC Neurology 2025 | 800 mg (~16 mg/kg) | 4 doses | IV |
| Chinese case (Medicine, 2024) | 800 mg | variable | IV |

**Recommended protocol based on available evidence:** *[Level 4 — baserat på fallrapporter]*
- **10 mg/kg IV weekly x 4 infusions** (consistent with MG dosing and most GBS case reports)
- Infusion over 1 hour
- Can be started immediately (no loading dose needed)
- IgG levels should be monitored weekly

### 4.5 How Quickly Does It Work?

From the Ripellino et al. case (European Journal of Neurology 2025):
- 60-year-old woman, fulminant AIDP, ventilator-dependent
- Failed 5 PE sessions AND full IVIg course
- Efgartigimod started Day 30 post-admission at 10 mg/kg IV weekly
- **Improvement in upper limb strength within HOURS of first dose**
- After 2nd and 3rd infusions: upper limbs reached MRC grade 4
- Tracheostomy removed at 2 months
- Walking with assistance at 2.5 months
- Full limb strength recovery at 4 months

IgG level kinetics:
- Admission: 10.7 g/L
- Post-PE: 4.6 g/L
- Post-IVIg: 22.3 g/L
- Progressive reduction with efgartigimod: 13.5 -> 7.2 -> 6.3 g/L
- Week 10: 12.2 g/L (normalized)

1. **Ripellino et al. (2025)** *[Level 4]* -- 60-year-old woman, fulminant AIDP, ventilator-dependent within 12 hours. Failed PE + IVIg. Efgartigimod 10 mg/kg x4 doses starting Day 30. Upper limb improvement within hours. Walked at 2.5 months. Full recovery at 4 months. (Eur J Neurol 2025;32(8):e70308; PMID: 40799081)

2. **Deng et al. (2025)** -- Four GBS cases (ages 30-51) including MFS-GBS overlap and AMSAN. Case 3: AMSAN, ventilator-dependent, MRC 3, received efgartigimod monotherapy (3 weekly infusions). Favorable outcome. (Front Immunol 2025;16:1586663)

4. **Watanabe et al. (2025)** -- 84-year-old male, anti-GQ1b antibody syndrome overlapping MG. Ventilator-dependent after IVIg failure. Respiratory function improved within 7 days of efgartigimod. Ventilator weaned. Second cycle restored speech and mobility. (Front Immunol 2025;16:1605985)

Imlifidase (IdeS, marketed as Idefirix for kidney transplant) is a unique IgG-cleaving enzyme from Streptococcus pyogenes that:
- Specifically and rapidly cleaves ALL IgG antibodies (including pathogenic anti-ganglioside/anti-nerve antibodies)
- Acts within **hours** of a single IV dose
- Eliminates IgG-mediated immune response before IVIg can be given 48 hours later
- Does NOT affect IgA, IgM, IgE, or complement directly

**Results:**

- Median 6 days to improve >= 1 GBS Disability Scale grade
- Median 16 days to independent walking (GBS DS <= 2)
- Week 8: 67% walking independently; 40.7% able to run

- Improved >= 1 GBS DS step **3 weeks sooner** (p=0.002)

## 6. Rituximab in Acute GBS

### 6.1 Evidence Summary

The evidence for rituximab in acute GBS is **very limited** -- consisting only of case reports and small series:

1. **Post-transplant GBS:** 58-year-old male with AMAN subtype post-HSCT, failed IVIg. Rituximab 375 mg/m2 weekly x4 weeks led to clinical improvement (Bulsara et al., Bone Marrow Transplant 2008; PMC4445357)

2. **GBS with multiple myeloma:** Patient refractory to PE, IVIg, AND anti-myeloma therapy responded to low-dose rituximab (PMC10969645, 2024)

3. **GBS with dual primary tumors:** Resolution of GBS after rituximab treatment in a cancer patient (Front Neurol 2024;15:1348304)

### 6.2 When to Consider Rituximab

Rituximab has a stronger rationale in specific settings:
- **Anti-neurofascin-155 (NF155) IgG4 antibody-positive neuropathy** -- these patients are characteristically IVIg-refractory and respond to rituximab (this overlaps with CIDP more than acute GBS)
- **Anti-contactin-1 IgG4 antibody-positive neuropathy** -- similar to NF155+
- **Post-transplant GBS** (evidence from case reports)
- **When nodal/paranodal IgG4 antibodies are identified** -- rituximab is the treatment of choice

**Important:** Test for anti-NF155/NF186 and anti-contactin antibodies in this patient (see Section 8). If positive (especially IgG4 class), rituximab becomes a higher-priority option.

### 6.3 Dosing for GBS

- **Standard protocol:** 375 mg/m2 IV weekly x 4 doses (same as lymphoma dosing)
- **Low-dose alternative:** Some case reports suggest efficacy at lower doses
- Premedication: acetaminophen, diphenhydramine, methylprednisolone
- B cell depletion occurs within days; maximal effect at 2-4 weeks
- Monitor CD19/CD20 B cell counts

### 6.4 Availability in Sweden

- **Rituximab is readily available in Sweden** -- approved and widely used for multiple indications (lymphoma, rheumatoid arthritis, ANCA vasculitis, etc.)
- Biosimilar rituximab products (Truxima, Rixathon, Ruxience) are available and cost-effective
- Off-label use for GBS is at physician discretion

## 7. Immunoadsorption as Alternative to PE

### 7.1 Evidence in GBS

From Boedecker et al. (J Clin Apheresis 2022) and the MDPI review (J Clin Med 2020;9(7):2025):

- A retrospective analysis of 20 GBS cases treated with IA or PE showed:
  - 40% partial improvement
  - 25% large improvement
  - 20% no improvement
  - 15% equivocal
- **Seronegative GBS patients responded BETTER to apheresis** than those with detectable nodal/paranodal antibodies
- A Japanese retrospective study found that **patients who received IA within 6 days of onset had more rapid improvement** compared to later IA

### 7.2 Advantages over PE (Especially Regarding Coagulopathy)

**This is highly relevant for the current patient with PE-induced coagulopathy:**

| Feature | Plasma Exchange (PE) | Immunoadsorption (IA) |
|---------|---------------------|----------------------|
| Replacement fluid needed | Yes (albumin or FFP) | **No** |
| Removes coagulation factors | **Yes -- significant depletion** | **No -- preserves fibrinogen and clotting factors** |
| Removes complement | Yes | Minimal |
| Removes albumin | Yes | No |
| Selectivity | Non-selective | **Selective for immunoglobulins** |
| Coagulopathy risk | **HIGH** | **LOW** |
| Infection risk from FFP | Yes | No |
| Allergic reaction risk | Yes (from replacement products) | Lower |

**Key finding:** IA offers "advantages in reducing complications, such as those due to the unwanted removal of coagulation factors" (Boedecker et al., 2022)

### 7.3 Column Types

- **Tryptophan-based columns** -- specifically noted as "more effective than phenylalanine for adsorbing anti-ganglioside antibodies" (MDPI 2020)
- **Protein A columns** -- standard immunoadsorption
- **GAM-peptide-ligand-based columns (Globaffin)** -- used in some centers

### 7.4 Availability

- Immunoadsorption is available at major university hospitals in Europe, particularly in Germany (where much of the evidence originates)
- In Sweden, IA is used primarily in nephrology (anti-GBM disease, transplant) but may be available at major centers
- Contact the nephrology/apheresis unit at the treating hospital
- **Given the coagulopathy, switching from PE to IA could be a practical solution to continue antibody removal without further depleting clotting factors**

## 9. Eculizumab -- Note on Complement Inhibitors

### 9.1 Why Not Eculizumab?

The Japanese Phase 3 trial (JET-GBS Phase 3, Kuwabara et al., J Peripher Nerv Syst 2024) was **negative:**
- At week 24, MORE placebo patients (65%) could run compared to eculizumab patients (54%)
- No significant effect on motor function recovery
- This is why tanruprubart (anti-C1q) is preferred over eculizumab (anti-C5) -- they target different points in the complement cascade

### 9.2 Key Difference: C1q vs C5 Inhibition

- **Eculizumab** blocks C5 -> prevents MAC formation but allows C3b opsonization and anaphylatoxin generation
- **Tanruprubart** blocks C1q -> prevents the ENTIRE classical complement cascade at its initiation point
- The C1q target may be more relevant in GBS because complement-mediated damage involves both MAC and opsonization

## 10. Integrated Treatment Algorithm for This Patient

### IMMEDIATE PRIORITIES (within 24-48 hours)

1. **STOP further PE sessions** -- not working and worsening coagulopathy
2. **Order all antibody tests** (Section 8) -- especially anti-NF155/NF186, anti-ganglioside panel, and anti-IgA antibodies
3. **Correct coagulopathy** -- FFP, cryoprecipitate, vitamin K as needed
4. **Contact Hansa Biopharma** (medinfo@hansabiopharma.com, +46 46 16 56 70) -- request compassionate use of imlifidase for GBS; reference Phase 2 data. They are a Swedish company in Lund -- fastest access pathway
5. **Contact Annexon Biosciences** (hakroon@annexonbio.com) -- inquire about compassionate use of tanruprubart

### SHORT-TERM OPTIONS (48 hours - 1 week)

**Option A: Efgartigimod (Vyvgart) -- MOST ACCESSIBLE**
- Already approved in Sweden (for MG/CIDP)
- Can be used off-label for GBS immediately
- 10 mg/kg IV weekly x 4 doses
- Improvement possible within hours to days (based on Ripellino case)
- Does NOT carry IgA-related anaphylaxis risk (it is an Fc fragment, not an immunoglobulin)
- **RECOMMENDED as first rescue therapy given immediate availability**

**Option B: IVIg with IgA-depleted product**
- Request Gammagard S/D (IgA <= 1 mcg/mL) or Privigen (IgA ~25 mcg/mL)
- 0.4 g/kg/day x 5 days
- Administer in ICU with epinephrine at bedside
- Slow initial infusion rate (0.5 mL/kg/hour, increase gradually)
- Monitor for anaphylaxis signs
- **Can be combined with efgartigimod** (efgartigimod will reduce total IgG including pathogenic antibodies; IVIg provides immunomodulation through different mechanism)

**Option C: Immunoadsorption** (if available)
- Alternative to PE that does NOT worsen coagulopathy
- Tryptophan columns preferred for anti-ganglioside antibodies
- Contact nephrology/apheresis unit

### MEDIUM-TERM OPTIONS (1-2 weeks)

**If anti-NF155/NF186 or anti-CNTN1 antibodies return POSITIVE:**
- **Rituximab 375 mg/m2 IV weekly x 4 doses** -- evidence supports efficacy in nodal/paranodal antibody-positive neuropathy

**If compassionate use of imlifidase is approved:**
- Single dose 0.25 mg/kg IV
- Follow with IgA-depleted IVIg 48 hours later
- Rapid IgG cleavage (hours), does not worsen coagulopathy

**If compassionate use of tanruprubart is approved:**
- Single 30 mg/kg IV infusion
- Blocks complement-mediated nerve damage
- Effect demonstrated within first week in clinical trials
