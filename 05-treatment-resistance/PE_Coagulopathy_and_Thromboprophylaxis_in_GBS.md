# Coagulopathy in GBS Treatment with Plasmapheresis: Comprehensive Evidence-Based Guide

## Clinical Context

**Scope:** This document addresses the coagulopathy induced by therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE/PE) in Guillain-Barré syndrome, DVT/PE prophylaxis in immobilized GBS patients, tracheostomy timing in coagulopathic patients, and the complex balancing of bleeding versus thrombotic risk. Intended for neurologists and ICU physicians managing severe GBS.

---

## 1. PE-Induced Coagulopathy in GBS

### 1.1 Mechanism: Which Clotting Factors Are Depleted?

Therapeutic plasma exchange removes all plasma constituents non-selectively, including coagulation factors, immunoglobulins, albumin, and complement. The degree of depletion depends on the replacement fluid used.

**Per-session depletion (1.0 plasma volume exchange with 5% albumin replacement):**
- Each 1.0 plasma volume exchange depletes coagulation factors by approximately **60-65%** (Schwartz et al., J Clin Apher 2016; AJKD Core Curriculum 2023)
- **Fibrinogen** is the most clinically significant factor depleted: levels decrease **54-80%** post-procedure with albumin replacement (Blasi et al., Vox Sanguinis 2016)
- Approximately **85% of circulating fibrinogen** is removed when albumin serves as the sole replacement solution (Medscape, Plasmapheresis Technique)
- **Factor XIII** is also significantly depleted alongside fibrinogen
- **Factors VIII, IX, and von Willebrand factor** recover rapidly (within 4 hours post-PE)
- **Other coagulation factors** (II, V, VII, X) generally recover to pre-PE levels within 24 hours

**The critical difference -- fibrinogen recovery kinetics:**

| Parameter | Recovery Time |
|-----------|--------------|
| Factors VIII, IX, vWF | ~4 hours |
| Most coagulation factors (II, V, VII, X) | ~24 hours |
| Fibrinogen | 48-72 hours (66% recovery at 72h) |
| Fibrinogen and Factor XIII (full recovery) | 96-120 hours |
| Complement (C3) | ~60% recovery at 72h |

*Source: ASH Education Program review (Schwartz 2012); Partial plasma exchange study (Orlin & Berkman, Blood 1980)*

**Why fibrinogen is the bottleneck:** Fibrinogen has a relatively long half-life (~4 days) but a slow synthetic rate. Unlike other factors that redistribute from extravascular compartments, fibrinogen's recovery depends primarily on hepatic synthesis. The 72-hour recovery of fibrinogen is only **66%** of pre-apheresis levels, compared to near-complete recovery for most other factors (Orlin & Berkman, Blood 1980).

### 1.2 How Many PE Sessions Before Coagulopathy Becomes Clinically Significant?

**Standard GBS PE protocol (ASFA guidelines):**
- **5 sessions**, 1.0-1.5 plasma volumes per session (typically 40-50 mL/kg per session)
- Performed **every other day** over 1-2 weeks
- Total exchange: 200-250 mL/kg (approximately 5 plasma volumes total)

**Cumulative depletion pattern:**
- **After 1 session:** Fibrinogen may drop to 40-50% of baseline. Usually still above the critical threshold of 1.0 g/L (100 mg/dL) if baseline was normal (2.0-4.0 g/L)
- **After 2-3 sessions:** Fibrinogen levels often fall below 1.5 g/L. If sessions are performed daily rather than every other day, levels may fall below 1.0 g/L
- **After 3-5 sessions (cumulative):** Clinically significant coagulopathy is common, particularly with every-other-day scheduling if recovery is incomplete between sessions. Fibrinogen may fall below **0.8-1.0 g/L** (80-100 mg/dL)

**Key insight:** The every-other-day schedule is specifically designed to allow redistribution and partial recovery of coagulation factors between sessions. Daily PE dramatically increases coagulopathy risk because fibrinogen requires 48-72 hours for meaningful recovery.

**ASFA position on monitoring:** ASFA and Choosing Wisely recommend **against** routine coagulation monitoring during a course of TPE unless the procedure is performed **daily**. For the standard every-other-day GBS protocol, routine monitoring is not mandated but is strongly recommended in clinical practice, particularly after session 3 (AJKD Core Curriculum 2023).

### 1.3 Laboratory Monitoring Protocol

**Recommended labs before each PE session (after session 2):**

| Test | Critical Threshold | Action Threshold | Target |
|------|-------------------|-----------------|--------|
| **Fibrinogen** | <0.8 g/L (80 mg/dL) | <1.0 g/L (100 mg/dL) | >1.5 g/L (150 mg/dL) |
| **Platelet count** | <50 x 10^9/L | <80 x 10^9/L | >100 x 10^9/L |
| **PT/INR** | INR >2.0 | INR >1.5 | INR <1.3 |
| **aPTT** | >50 seconds | >1.5x upper normal | <1.2x upper normal |
| **D-dimer** | Monitor trend | Rising trend may indicate DIC | Context-dependent |

**Monitoring frequency recommendations:**
- **Before sessions 1-2:** Baseline labs (fibrinogen, CBC, PT/INR, aPTT)
- **Before sessions 3-5:** Check fibrinogen before each session. This is when cumulative depletion becomes clinically relevant
- **Post-session (when concerning):** Check fibrinogen 1-2 hours post-PE if pre-PE level was near threshold
- **Between sessions:** If fibrinogen was <1.0 g/L post-PE, recheck at 24-48 hours to assess recovery trajectory

**D-dimer interpretation in PE-treated patients:**
D-dimer may be elevated after PE due to fibrinolytic activity and does not necessarily indicate DIC. However, a persistently rising D-dimer trend combined with falling fibrinogen, rising PT/INR, and thrombocytopenia should raise suspicion for consumptive coagulopathy. The ISTH DIC score (>5 indicates overt DIC) can be applied, using PT prolongation, platelet count, fibrinogen level, and D-dimer/FDP as inputs.

### 1.4 Management of PE-Induced Coagulopathy

#### 1.4.1 When to Give Fibrinogen Replacement

**Fibrinogen <1.0 g/L (100 mg/dL) with no active bleeding:**
- Consider adding **FFP as partial replacement fluid** for the next PE session (typically last 1-2 units as FFP instead of albumin)
- Alternatively, supplement with fibrinogen concentrate or cryoprecipitate between sessions

**Fibrinogen <0.8 g/L (80 mg/dL) OR any active bleeding:**
- **Cryoprecipitate:** 6-10 units (pooled). Each pool of ~10 units raises fibrinogen by approximately 50-70 mg/dL in a 70 kg adult. Target: fibrinogen >1.0 g/L
- **Fibrinogen concentrate (RiaSTAP/Haemocomplettan):** 30-60 mg/kg IV. More precise dosing, no thawing required, lower volume, viral-inactivated
- **FFP:** 10-15 mL/kg. Less effective for isolated hypofibrinogenemia because fibrinogen concentration in FFP is only ~2.5 g/L, but replaces all factors simultaneously

**Evidence-based perspective:** Wodajo et al. (Transfusion, 2024) conducted a prospective study of 275 patients receiving 1406 PE procedures and found that a pre-procedure fibrinogen threshold of **80-100 mg/dL without plasma supplementation** did not increase bleeding risk in patients **not on anticoagulation**. Among patients on anticoagulants, bleeding occurred in 3% (2/62 patients). This suggests that fibrinogen thresholds may be safely lowered to 80 mg/dL in selected patients.

**However:** For GBS patients who are simultaneously receiving LMWH for DVT prophylaxis, the more conservative threshold of **1.0 g/L (100 mg/dL)** should be maintained, as the Wodajo data showed increased bleeding in anticoagulated patients.

#### 1.4.2 Choice of Fibrinogen Replacement Product

| Product | Fibrinogen Content | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---------|-------------------|------------|---------------|
| **Cryoprecipitate** (10 units pooled) | ~250 mg/unit, ~2500 mg total | Also contains Factor VIII, XIII, vWF, fibronectin | Requires ABO matching, thawing time, pooled donor risk |
| **Fibrinogen concentrate** (RiaSTAP) | 900-1300 mg/vial | Precise dosing, rapid reconstitution, viral-inactivated | Cost, may not replace other depleted factors |
| **FFP** (1 unit ~250 mL) | ~500-600 mg/unit (~2.5 g/L) | Replaces ALL factors | Large volume, allergic reactions, TRALI risk, slow thawing |

*Source: StatPearls FFP 2024; ISBT Plasma Transfusion module; ASH fibrinogen replacement review (Levy & Goodnough, Blood 2015)*

**Cryoprecipitate is preferred** for isolated hypofibrinogenemia because it delivers concentrated fibrinogen in a small volume. Since fibrinogen is the primary depleted factor in PE (other factors recover within 24 hours), cryoprecipitate addresses the specific deficiency most efficiently.

#### 1.4.3 Replacement Fluid Strategy During PE

- **5% albumin** is the standard replacement fluid for GBS PE (used in the landmark trials)
- **Partial FFP replacement** (e.g., last 500-1000 mL as FFP) can preserve coagulation factor levels, particularly fibrinogen
- The decreases in fibrinogen and prothrombin levels are **greater** with albumin than FFP replacement (Cochrane review, Chevret et al. 2017)
- **Pilot study (J Clin Med, 2024):** Fibrinogen concentrate supplementation and partial FFP replacement can both maintain fibrinogen >1.0 g/L after TPE, but modest differences in ROTEM tests favor FFP replacement
- **Clinical outcome:** The French Cooperative Group trial found no significant difference in GBS outcomes between FFP and albumin replacement (77% vs 65% full recovery, not statistically significant)

### 1.5 Can PE Continue with Coagulopathy? When to Pause?

**Continue PE (with modifications) if:**
- Fibrinogen >0.8 g/L (80 mg/dL) and no active bleeding
- Switch to partial FFP replacement to maintain coagulation factors
- The clinical benefit of completing the PE course (5 sessions) outweighs the coagulopathy risk
- No planned invasive procedures in the next 24-48 hours

**Pause PE if:**
- Fibrinogen <0.8 g/L (80 mg/dL) despite supplementation
- Active clinically significant bleeding (not minor oozing from catheter site)
- Planned tracheostomy or other invasive procedure -- allow 48-72 hours for fibrinogen recovery
- Platelet count <50 x 10^9/L
- Clinical signs of DIC (rising D-dimer + falling fibrinogen + thrombocytopenia + microangiopathic changes)

**Practical approach to continuing PE despite coagulopathy:**
1. Switch replacement fluid to **partial or full FFP** for remaining sessions
2. Extend interval between sessions from every-other-day to **every third day** to allow more fibrinogen recovery
3. Supplement with cryoprecipitate or fibrinogen concentrate between sessions
4. Monitor fibrinogen before and after each session
5. Overall bleeding risk remains low: bleeding occurred in only **1.3-3.1%** of PE treatments across large series, even with documented coagulopathy (Krieger et al., 2015)

## 2. DVT/PE Prophylaxis in Immobilized GBS Patients

### 2.1 Incidence of DVT and Pulmonary Embolism in GBS

GBS patients are at **very high risk** for venous thromboembolism due to immobilization, acute illness, central venous catheters, and often ICU admission.

**Key epidemiological data:**

| Study | DVT Incidence | PE Incidence | Notes |
|-------|--------------|--------------|-------|
| Gaber et al. 2002, Clin Rehabil | 7% clinical DVT | 4% had PE (3/5 DVT patients) | 1 fatal PE despite enoxaparin |
| Bersano et al. 2016, Neurol Sci | 52% DVT (ultrasound-screened) | 15% PE | Despite LMWH prophylaxis |
| Without prophylaxis (historical) | Up to **34%** DVT | Not specified | Before routine prophylaxis era |
| Rehabilitation cohort (Yoo et al. 2003) | 11.1% DVT (4/36) | -- | During inpatient rehabilitation |

**Critical finding:** The Bersano et al. (2016) study is particularly alarming -- even with LMWH prophylaxis, approximately **half** of GBS patients developed VTE when systematically screened with ultrasound. This suggests that subclinical DVT is far more common than clinically detected DVT.

**Pulmonary embolism as cause of death:** PE is one of the primary causes of death in GBS, responsible for death in approximately **half** of fatal GBS cases (alongside respiratory failure and sepsis). Overall GBS mortality is 3-7%, but in ventilated patients it rises to ~20% (Bellanti & Rinaldi, Eur J Neurol 2024).

**Risk factors for VTE in GBS (Bersano et al. 2016):**
- Severe disease requiring mechanical ventilation
- Bed rest >3 days
- Infectious complications
- Central venous catheter
- Age >40 years

### 2.2 Pharmacological Prophylaxis

#### 2.2.1 LMWH (Standard of Care)

**Enoxaparin dosing:**
- **Standard dose:** 40 mg subcutaneously once daily
- **Low body weight (<50 kg):** 30 mg subcutaneously once daily
- **Renal impairment (CrCl <30 mL/min):** 30 mg subcutaneously once daily
- **Severe obesity (>120 kg):** Consider 40 mg SC twice daily or anti-Xa monitoring

**Alternative LMWH options:**
- **Dalteparin:** 5000 IU SC once daily
- **Tinzaparin:** 4500 IU SC once daily (or 75 IU/kg for high-risk)

**Unfractionated heparin (UFH):**
- 5000 IU SC every 8-12 hours
- Preferred when renal function is severely impaired (CrCl <20 mL/min) or when reversal may be needed (planned procedures)

**Fondaparinux:**
- 2.5 mg SC once daily
- Alternative when heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) is suspected

**Timing:**
- Initiate within 24 hours of ICU admission unless contraindicated
- Continue throughout immobilization period
- Duration: until full mobility is restored or hospital discharge (NICE guidelines suggest up to 42 days for lower limb immobilization)
- Consider extended prophylaxis during rehabilitation phase if mobility remains severely limited

#### 2.2.2 Guideline Recommendations

**EAN/PNS 2023 (Doorn et al.):** The guideline acknowledges DVT as a significant complication of severe GBS requiring prophylaxis but does not provide specific pharmacological protocols, deferring to general VTE prophylaxis guidelines.

**NICE VTE Prophylaxis Guideline (NG89, updated):** Recommends LMWH or fondaparinux for acutely ill medical patients with reduced mobility whose VTE risk outweighs bleeding risk. GBS patients fall squarely into the "high VTE risk" category.

**ACCP/CHEST 2012 (9th Edition, Kahn et al.):** Recommends LMWH, LDUH, or fondaparinux for acutely ill hospitalized medical patients at increased thrombotic risk. Grade 1B recommendation. Recommends **against** routine ultrasound screening for DVT in asymptomatic patients.

**ASH 2018:** LMWH preferred over UFH for VTE prophylaxis in critically ill patients (conditional recommendation).

**Italian Society for Haemostasis and Thrombosis (SISET) 2009:** Specifically addresses immobilized neurological patients, recommending LMWH for GBS patients who are bed-bound.

### 2.3 Mechanical Prophylaxis

#### 2.3.1 Intermittent Pneumatic Compression (IPC)

- **Should be used in combination** with pharmacological prophylaxis when possible
- **Sole prophylaxis** when pharmacological agents are contraindicated (e.g., active bleeding, severe coagulopathy)
- Meta-analysis evidence: IPC alone reduces DVT risk comparably to LMWH in critically ill patients, with **no additional bleeding risk** (PMC 2024 systematic review)
- IPC alone may be sufficient when pharmacological prophylaxis is temporarily held

#### 2.3.2 Graduated Compression Stockings (GCS)

- Less effective than IPC as sole mechanical prophylaxis
- May be used in addition to IPC and pharmacological prophylaxis
- **Caution in GBS:** Monitor for skin breakdown due to sensory deficits -- patients may not report discomfort
- Contraindicated in peripheral arterial disease

#### 2.3.3 Practical Recommendation for GBS

**Combine LMWH + IPC** as the standard approach for immobilized GBS patients:
- Start IPC devices immediately upon admission (both legs)
- Start LMWH within 24 hours unless contraindicated
- If LMWH must be held (coagulopathy, planned procedure), continue IPC alone until LMWH can be resumed
- Progressive mobilization and physiotherapy as tolerated

### 2.4 Special Consideration: PE-Induced Coagulopathy + DVT Risk

This is the central clinical dilemma: the GBS patient needs PE (which causes coagulopathy and bleeding risk) AND needs anticoagulation (because immobilization causes high thrombotic risk).

**Recommended approach:**

1. **On PE treatment days:**
   - Hold LMWH dose on the morning of PE (give after PE is completed, if fibrinogen permits)
   - Ensure IPC devices are in place during and after PE
   - Check fibrinogen post-PE

2. **Between PE sessions:**
   - Resume LMWH if fibrinogen >1.0 g/L (100 mg/dL) and platelet count >50 x 10^9/L
   - If fibrinogen 0.8-1.0 g/L: use IPC alone + reassess fibrinogen at 24 hours. Resume LMWH once fibrinogen >1.0 g/L
   - If fibrinogen <0.8 g/L: IPC only + consider fibrinogen replacement. Hold LMWH until fibrinogen recovers

3. **Monitoring:**
   - Check fibrinogen before each PE session AND before restarting LMWH
   - Monitor for clinical signs of DVT daily (asymmetric limb swelling -- though examination may be difficult in paralyzed limbs)
   - Low threshold for duplex ultrasound if any suspicion

4. **If DVT develops during a PE course with coagulopathy:**
   - This requires individualized multidisciplinary decision-making (neurology, hematology, ICU)
   - Therapeutic anticoagulation may need to be initiated despite coagulopathy
   - Consider IVC filter placement if both therapeutic anticoagulation and active bleeding/severe coagulopathy coexist
   - Continue PE for GBS with FFP as replacement fluid (provides both treatment and coagulation factor replacement)

## 3. Coagulopathy and Tracheostomy Timing

### 3.1 When Is Tracheostomy Needed in GBS?

- Approximately **30%** of hospitalized GBS patients require mechanical ventilation
- Of intubated GBS patients, approximately **50%** ultimately require tracheostomy (Pubmed: Ali et al. 1999)
- **Timing recommendation:** Clinical monitoring for approximately **2 weeks** after intubation before deciding on tracheostomy, to avoid unnecessary procedures in patients who may recover ventilatory function (Walgaard et al., Neurocrit Care 2017)
- **Predictors of prolonged ventilation** (favoring earlier tracheostomy): severe muscle weakness, axonal degeneration or inexcitable nerves on NCS, mEGRIS score (EAN/PNS 2023)
- Delaying tracheostomy **>14 days** after intubation is associated with higher rates of ventilator-associated pneumonia and longer ICU stay

### 3.2 Minimum Coagulation Parameters for Safe Tracheostomy

**Consensus thresholds from ICU literature (Pilarczyk et al. 2016; PMC Patient Selection PDT 2021):**

| Parameter | Minimum for Tracheostomy | Preferred Target | Notes |
|-----------|-------------------------|-----------------|-------|
| **Fibrinogen** | >1.5 g/L (150 mg/dL) | >2.0 g/L (200 mg/dL) | Fibrinogen <7.4 umol/L (~2.2 g/L) identified as independent risk factor for bleeding (Pilarczyk et al. 2016) |
| **Platelet count** | >50 x 10^9/L | >80 x 10^9/L | Some centers accept >50 with ultrasound guidance |
| **INR** | <1.5 | <1.3 | Higher-risk procedures: INR <1.5 (SIR guidelines) |
| **aPTT** | <50 seconds | <1.5x upper normal | Stop heparin infusion 4-6h before if applicable |

**Important nuance -- evidence supports more liberal thresholds:**
- Pilarczyk et al. (2016) analyzed 483 PDTs over 10 years: after multivariate analysis, **only low fibrinogen** retained significance as an independent predictor of bleeding
- Percutaneous tracheostomy was safely performed in 42 patients with **severe thrombocytopenia** (mean platelet count 26.4 x 10^9/L), with only 5% major bleeding (Kluge et al., Anesth Analg 2004)
- A prospective study found no significant difference in bleeding between coagulopathic and non-coagulopathic patients undergoing PDT (9.7% vs 7.3% moderate bleeding) (Indian J Crit Care Med 2020)
- The TOPIC trial (RCT) failed to show that FFP transfusion to correct INR (>1.5-3.0) prevented bleeding complications in patients undergoing PDT

**Clinical implication for GBS patients on PE:** Fibrinogen is the most important parameter to optimize before tracheostomy. Given that fibrinogen is the factor most depleted by PE and the slowest to recover, this creates a specific scheduling challenge.

### 3.3 How to Correct Coagulopathy Before Tracheostomy

**Step-by-step protocol:**

1. **Timing:** Schedule tracheostomy at least **48-72 hours** after the last PE session to allow maximal fibrinogen recovery
2. **Check labs** 24 hours before planned tracheostomy:
   - If fibrinogen <1.5 g/L: administer **cryoprecipitate** (10 units) or **fibrinogen concentrate** (30-60 mg/kg). Recheck in 4-6 hours
   - If platelets <50 x 10^9/L: administer platelet transfusion (1 pool = ~4-6 units). Target >80 x 10^9/L
   - If INR >1.5: administer **FFP** 10-15 mL/kg. However, note that the TOPIC trial suggests this may not reduce bleeding risk
3. **Hold LMWH** at least **12 hours** before tracheostomy (24 hours for therapeutic-dose enoxaparin)
4. **Hold UFH** at least **4-6 hours** before tracheostomy
5. **Recheck fibrinogen** on the morning of the procedure. Proceed if >1.5 g/L
6. **Resume LMWH** 6-12 hours post-tracheostomy if no bleeding

**If PE course is incomplete and tracheostomy is needed:**
- Complete the most urgent intervention first (usually tracheostomy if ventilation is the immediate concern)
- Schedule remaining PE sessions after tracheostomy, with 48-72 hour buffer
- Alternative: if patient has already received 3 PE sessions, the remaining clinical benefit of sessions 4-5 may be modest, and the tracheostomy should take priority

### 3.4 Percutaneous vs. Surgical Tracheostomy in Coagulopathic Patients

**Percutaneous dilatational tracheostomy (PDT):**
- Can be performed at the bedside in the ICU
- Multiple studies confirm it is **safe in coagulopathic patients** when performed by experienced operators with bronchoscopic or ultrasound guidance (Kluge 2004; Beiderlinden 2007; Indian J Crit Care Med 2020)
- Complication rates in coagulopathic patients: 3.3% (1.04% bleeding), comparable to non-coagulopathic controls
- **Ultrasound guidance** further reduces bleeding risk by identifying aberrant vessels

**Surgical (open) tracheostomy:**
- Provides direct visualization and hemostatic control
- Traditionally preferred in "high-risk" patients, but recent evidence shows no advantage over PDT in coagulopathy
- May be preferred if: anatomy is unfavorable (short neck, cervical spine issues), previous neck surgery, or PDT expertise is not available

**Recommendation for GBS patients with PE-induced coagulopathy:**
PDT with bronchoscopic and/or ultrasound guidance is acceptable if fibrinogen is >1.5 g/L and platelets >50 x 10^9/L. If coagulopathy is more severe, surgical tracheostomy offers better hemostatic control but evidence suggests PDT remains safe even with coagulopathy if performed by experienced operators.

## 4. Anticoagulation Decisions in GBS with Concurrent Coagulopathy

### 4.1 The Dual-Risk Scenario

The immobilized GBS patient undergoing PE faces simultaneous:
- **High thrombotic risk:** Immobilization, acute illness, central venous catheter, possible ventilation
- **High bleeding risk:** PE-induced coagulopathy, hypofibrinogenemia, potential invasive procedures

This dual-risk situation requires **individualized, dynamic risk assessment** rather than a static protocol.

### 4.2 Decision Framework

**Phase 1: During active PE course (sessions 1-5, typically days 1-10)**

| Clinical State | LMWH | IPC | Additional Measures |
|---------------|------|-----|-------------------|
| Fibrinogen >1.5 g/L, no bleeding | Full-dose LMWH (40 mg enoxaparin SC daily) | Yes, continuously | Standard monitoring |
| Fibrinogen 1.0-1.5 g/L, no bleeding | Full-dose LMWH (may hold on PE days) | Yes, continuously | Check fibrinogen before each LMWH dose |
| Fibrinogen 0.8-1.0 g/L, no bleeding | Hold LMWH | Yes, continuously | Supplement fibrinogen; reassess daily |
| Fibrinogen <0.8 g/L, no bleeding | Hold LMWH | Yes, continuously | Replace fibrinogen; consider holding next PE |
| Any fibrinogen + active bleeding | Hold LMWH | Yes, continuously | Correct coagulopathy; hold PE; hematology consult |

**Phase 2: Post-PE course (after completing all 5 sessions)**

- Resume full-dose LMWH as soon as fibrinogen >1.0 g/L (typically 48-72 hours after last PE)
- Continue IPC until patient is ambulatory
- Continue LMWH throughout hospital stay and rehabilitation until independently mobile

**Phase 3: Rehabilitation**

- Continue LMWH prophylaxis if patient remains non-ambulatory
- Consider extended prophylaxis up to **42 days** if severe immobility persists (NICE NG89)
- Transition to aspirin or discontinue when patient achieves independent ambulation

### 4.3 Monitoring Protocol

**Daily during PE course:**
- Clinical bleeding assessment (catheter sites, oral/nasal mucosa, urine, stool)
- Limb examination for DVT signs (difficult in paralyzed limbs -- have low threshold for duplex US)

**Before each PE session:**
- Fibrinogen (mandatory from session 3 onward)
- Platelet count
- PT/INR if previously abnormal

**Before each LMWH dose (if fibrinogen was recently <1.5 g/L):**
- Fibrinogen level
- Only administer LMWH if fibrinogen >1.0 g/L

**Weekly during immobilization:**
- Duplex ultrasound of lower extremities (consider routine screening given the 52% subclinical DVT rate reported by Bersano et al.)
- D-dimer trend (rising levels may prompt earlier ultrasound)

### 4.4 Special Scenario: Active DVT/PE Discovered During PE Course with Coagulopathy

This is the most challenging scenario. Management options:

1. **Therapeutic anticoagulation if fibrinogen permits:**
   - Switch to therapeutic-dose enoxaparin (1 mg/kg SC q12h) or IV UFH (target aPTT 60-80s)
   - Requires fibrinogen >1.0 g/L and platelets >50 x 10^9/L
   - Supplement fibrinogen as needed to maintain levels

2. **IVC filter placement** if:
   - Fibrinogen <0.8 g/L AND therapeutic anticoagulation is contraindicated
   - Active proximal DVT or PE with hemodynamic compromise
   - Retrievable IVC filter preferred; remove when anticoagulation can be safely initiated

3. **Switch PE replacement fluid to FFP:**
   - Provides dual benefit: treats GBS AND replenishes coagulation factors
   - Allows safer concurrent therapeutic anticoagulation
   - Higher risk of allergic reactions and TRALI compared to albumin

4. **Multidisciplinary consultation:**
   - Neurology, hematology, vascular surgery/interventional radiology, ICU
   - Document risk-benefit analysis in patient record

## 5. Summary: Practical Quick-Reference

### 5.1 Key Thresholds

| Parameter | Safe for LMWH Prophylaxis | Safe for Tracheostomy | Requires Replacement |
|-----------|--------------------------|----------------------|---------------------|
| Fibrinogen | >1.0 g/L | >1.5 g/L | <1.0 g/L (bleeding) or <0.8 g/L (any) |
| Platelets | >50 x 10^9/L | >50 x 10^9/L (prefer >80) | <50 x 10^9/L |
| INR | <2.0 | <1.5 | >1.5 with bleeding |

### 5.2 Fibrinogen Replacement Quick Guide

- **Cryoprecipitate 10 units:** Raises fibrinogen ~50-70 mg/dL (0.5-0.7 g/L) in 70 kg adult
- **Fibrinogen concentrate 2g IV:** Raises fibrinogen ~40-60 mg/dL (0.4-0.6 g/L) in 70 kg adult
- **FFP 4 units (1000 mL):** Raises fibrinogen ~20-30 mg/dL (0.2-0.3 g/L) -- less efficient for isolated hypofibrinogenemia

### 5.3 Timeline for GBS Patient Requiring PE + Tracheostomy

| Day | Action | Coagulation Consideration |
|-----|--------|--------------------------|
| Day 1 | PE session 1 + start IPC | Baseline labs; start LMWH (hold on PE day if concerned) |
| Day 3 | PE session 2 | Check fibrinogen pre-PE |
| Day 5 | PE session 3 | Check fibrinogen pre-PE; this is when levels often become critical |
| Day 7 | PE session 4 | If fibrinogen <1.0: consider FFP replacement or fibrinogen supplement |
| Day 9 | PE session 5 (last) | Check post-PE fibrinogen |
| Days 10-11 | Recovery window | Allow fibrinogen to recover; supplement if <1.5 g/L |
| Day 12+ | Tracheostomy (if indicated) | Requires fibrinogen >1.5 g/L, platelets >50, INR <1.5 |
| Day 12+ | Resume full LMWH | After tracheostomy site hemostasis confirmed (6-12h post) |
