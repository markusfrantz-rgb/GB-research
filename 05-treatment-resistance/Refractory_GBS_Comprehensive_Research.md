## 1. DEFINITION OF REFRACTORY GBS

### 1.1 Treatment-Related Fluctuations (TRF)

**Definition:** Treatment-related fluctuation (TRF) is defined as clinical deterioration within two months of symptom onset following previous stabilization or improvement with treatment (IVIg or plasma exchange).

**Formal diagnostic criteria** require either:
1. Improvement in the GBS disability score of at least 1 grade or MRC sum score of >5 points after completion of immunotherapy, followed by worsening of at least 1 grade on the GBS disability scale or a decrease of >5 points in MRC sum score within one month; OR
2. Clinical stability for >1 week post-treatment, followed by deterioration (at least 1 grade on GBS disability score or >5-point MRC sum score reduction).

**Frequency:** Approximately 5-10% of GBS patients (range 5-26% in older literature); recent IGOS data converges on approximately 5%.

**Timing:** Median time to first relapse is 9 days (range 2-15 days) after treatment. Median delay to second treatment is 16 days (2-25 days).

**Risk Factors for TRF:**
- Infectious mononucleosis (EBV/CMV) as a trigger (28.57% in TRF vs 8.55% without)
- Higher baseline disability scores (median 4 vs 2, p=0.01)
- Neuropathic pain at presentation (100% vs 60.68%, p=0.03)

> **Citation:** Alessandro L, Castiglione JI, Brand P, Bruno V, Barroso F. Treatment-related fluctuations in Guillain-Barre syndrome. *Arq Neuropsiquiatr*. 2022;80(5):516-522. DOI: 10.1590/0004-282X-ANP-2021-0226

### 1.2 Treatment Failure Criteria

**No consensus definition exists** for treatment failure in GBS. The practical working definitions include:

- **Non-responder:** Patients who are severely affected (Hughes grade 3 or higher) and do not respond to initial treatment, defined as improvement of less than one grade on the Hughes scale after completing standard therapy.
- **Poor prognosis patients:** Score of 6 or higher on the modified Erasmus GBS Outcome Score (mEGOS).
- **Residual disability:** Approximately 20-30% of treated patients remain unable to walk unaided at 6 months; 5% die.

### 1.3 Distinguishing TRF from Acute-Onset CIDP

Critical distinction: Patients with 3 or more fluctuations, or symptom progression beyond 8 weeks, should be reclassified as having acute-onset chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy (A-CIDP) or autoimmune nodopathy (AIN), NOT refractory GBS.

> **Citation:** Bellanti R, Rinaldi S. Guillain-Barre syndrome: a comprehensive review. *Eur J Neurol*. 2024;31:e16365. DOI: 10.1111/ene.16365

### 1.4 Hughes Functional Grading Scale (GBS Disability Score)

| Grade | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| 0 | Normal |
| 1 | Minor symptoms, able to run |
| 2 | Able to walk 10 m without assistance but unable to run |
| 3 | Able to walk 10 m with assistance |
| 4 | Bedridden or chair-bound |
| 5 | Requiring assisted ventilation |
| 6 | Dead |

## 2. SECOND-LINE TREATMENTS FOR REFRACTORY GBS

### 2.1 Second Course of IVIg

#### SID-GBS Trial (Pivotal Negative Trial)

The definitive randomized controlled trial on a second IVIg dose was the SID-GBS trial. This double-blind, placebo-controlled trial enrolled patients with poor-prognosis GBS (mEGOS score >=6) at 59 hospitals in the Netherlands.

**Design:** Patients received standard IVIg (2 g/kg over 5 days), then those with poor prognosis were randomized to a second IVIg course (2 g/kg over 5 days) or placebo, administered 7-9 days after inclusion.

**Results (n=93, 49 second dose vs 44 placebo):**
- **Primary endpoint:** Adjusted common odds ratio for improvement on GBS disability score at 4 weeks: 1.4 (95% CI 0.6-3.3; p=0.45) -- NOT significant
- **Serious adverse events:** 35% in second-dose group vs 16% in placebo group, including thromboembolic events
- **Deaths:** Four deaths occurred in the treatment group between weeks 13-24

**Conclusion:** A second IVIg course provides NO benefit and INCREASES risk of serious adverse events. The 2023 EAN/PNS guideline explicitly recommends against a second IVIg course.

> **Citation:** Walgaard C, Jacobs BC, Lingsma HF, et al.; Dutch GBS Study Group. Second intravenous immunoglobulin dose in patients with Guillain-Barre syndrome with poor prognosis (SID-GBS): a double-blind, randomised, placebo-controlled trial. *Lancet Neurol*. 2021;20(4):275-283. DOI: 10.1016/S1474-4422(20)30494-4

#### Pharmacodynamic Analysis of Second IVIg Course

A 2025 pharmacodynamic analysis of the SID-GBS trial confirmed that while a second IVIg dose increased serum IgG concentration, it did not improve clinical outcomes and increased risk of serious adverse events, particularly thromboembolic complications. The authors advocate for model-informed precision dosing rather than blanket second courses.

> **Citation:** van Tilburg SJ, Huizinga R, Kuitwaard K, et al. If it does not help, it might hurt: Pharmacodynamics of a second IVIg course in Guillain-Barre syndrome. *Ann Clin Transl Neurol*. 2025;12(5):966-975. DOI: 10.1002/acn3.52313

### 2.2 Combined IVIg + Plasmapheresis

**Evidence: NOT recommended.**

The landmark 1992 Randomized Trial of PE, IVIg, and combined treatments demonstrated that combination therapy (PE followed by IVIg) conferred NO additional therapeutic advantage over either treatment alone.

The 2023 EAN/PNS guideline recommends against combined PE followed immediately by IVIg.

> **Citation:** Plasma Exchange/Sandoglobulin Guillain-Barre Syndrome Trial Group. Randomised trial of plasma exchange, intravenous immunoglobulin, and combined treatments in Guillain-Barre syndrome. *Lancet*. 1997;349(9047):225-230.

### 2.3 Eculizumab (Complement C5 Inhibitor)

#### Phase 2 Trial: JET-GBS (Japanese Eculizumab Trial for GBS)

**Design:** 24-week, multicenter, double-blind, placebo-controlled, randomized phase 2 trial at 13 hospitals in Japan. Patients with severe GBS (functional grade 3-5, age >=18) randomized 2:1 to IVIg + eculizumab (900 mg weekly for 4 weeks) or IVIg + placebo.

**Results (n=34; eculizumab n=23, placebo n=11):**
- **Primary endpoint (walk independently at week 4):** 61% eculizumab vs 45% placebo (90% CI 42-78). The predefined success threshold was NOT met.
- **Week 24:** 74% of eculizumab group could run vs 18% placebo (descriptive).
- **Safety:** Serious AEs included anaphylaxis and intracranial hemorrhage with abscess in the eculizumab group.

> **Citation:** Misawa S, Kuwabara S, Sato Y, et al.; JET-GBS Study Group. Safety and efficacy of eculizumab in Guillain-Barre syndrome: a multicentre, double-blind, randomised phase 2 trial. *Lancet Neurol*. 2018;17(6):519-529. DOI: 10.1016/S1474-4422(18)30114-5

#### Phase 3 Trial

**Design:** Phase 3, multicenter, double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled clinical trial (NCT04752566) in Japan. Adults with severe GBS randomized 2:1 to eculizumab or placebo (weekly for 4 weeks) + IVIg, with 20 weeks follow-up.

**Results (n=57; eculizumab n=37, placebo n=20):**
- **Primary endpoint:** Hazard ratio 0.9 (95% CI 0.45-1.97; p=0.89) -- NOT significant
- **Secondary endpoints:** Did not reach statistical significance
- **Safety:** Well tolerated

**Conclusion:** Eculizumab failed to demonstrate efficacy in GBS in the definitive Phase 3 trial. The 2023 EAN/PNS guideline does NOT recommend eculizumab for GBS treatment.

> **Citation:** Kuwabara S, Kusunoki S, Kuwahara M, et al. Efficacy and safety of eculizumab in Guillain-Barre syndrome: A phase 3, multicenter, double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled clinical trial. *J Peripher Nerv Syst*. 2024;29(3):339-349. DOI: 10.1111/jns.12646

### 2.4 Ravulizumab

Ravulizumab is a long-acting C5 complement inhibitor (anti-C5 monoclonal antibody). While approved for PNH and other complement-mediated conditions, **no specific clinical trial for GBS with ravulizumab has been identified** in the current literature. Clinical trials involving C5 inhibitors in GBS have referenced ravulizumab only in the context of washout periods (41 weeks required due to its long half-life).

### 2.5 Rituximab (Anti-CD20)

Rituximab depletes CD20+ B cells and has been used off-label in refractory GBS cases, primarily in case reports.

#### Case Report: Refractory GBS with Multiple Myeloma

An 85-year-old female with PEX- and IVIg-refractory GBS and concurrent asymptomatic multiple myeloma (IgA kappa monoclonal gammopathy, 15 g/L). After failure of PE (5 sessions), IVIg, and anti-myeloma therapy (bortezomib, melphalan, prednisone), low-dose rituximab (100 mg/m^2/week x 4 doses) was administered. One week after initiating rituximab, dramatic improvement occurred -- the patient regained foot mobility with resolution of sensory loss. By 3 months, functional walking with assistance was achieved, and at 8-month follow-up neurological status remained stable.

> **Citation:** Krecak I, Gveric-Krecak V. Refractory Guillain-Barre syndrome in a patient with asymptomatic multiple myeloma successfully treated with low-dose rituximab. *Acta Clin Croat*. 2023;62(2):382-386. DOI: 10.20471/acc.2023.62.02.19

#### Rituximab in Anti-Neurofascin Nodopathies

Anti-NF155 IgG4-positive autoimmune nodopathies (which can present acutely resembling GBS) are characteristically refractory to IVIg but respond well to rituximab. This is particularly relevant when GBS-like presentations are later reclassified as autoimmune nodopathy. However, anti-rituximab antibodies (ARAs) can develop and should be monitored.

> **Citation:** Neurofascin antibodies in autoimmune, genetic, and idiopathic neuropathies. *Neurology*. 2018;90(1):e59-e68. DOI: 10.1212/WNL.0000000000004773

### 2.6 Cyclophosphamide

#### In SLE-Related GBS

A systematic review of 26 studies analyzing 28 patients with SLE-related GBS treated with cyclophosphamide found:
- **Response rate:** 85.7% showed resolution (46.4%) or improvement (39.3%) of neurological symptoms
- **Adverse events:** Infections in 7.1%; one death (3.6%) from posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome
- **Important caveat:** Cyclophosphamide is effective only for SLE-related GBS and NOT for primary/pure GBS

> **Citation:** Xiong A, Cui H, Deng R, Wei X. Cyclophosphamide in the treatment of systemic lupus erythematosus-related Guillain-Barre syndrome: a systematic review of case reports. *J Neuroimmune Pharmacol*. 2023;18(3):285-293. DOI: 10.1007/s11481-023-10075-w

#### In Primary GBS

A classic study of 13 patients with severe, progressive GBS polyneuritis treated with cyclophosphamide reported improvement in recovery processes, but evidence remains at the case series level.

> **Citation:** Gruener G, Bosch EP, Strauss RG, Klugman M, Kimura J. Clinical effects of cyclophosphamide in Guillain-Barre polyneuritis. *Acta Neurol Scand*. 1977;56(6):548-559.

### 2.7 Interferon-Beta

One small RCT (n=13) showed NO significant difference between interferon beta-1a and placebo for any outcome in GBS. Interferon-beta did not have any unexpected interaction with IVIg. Evidence quality rated as very low.

> **Citation:** Hughes RA, Pritchard J, Hadden RD. Pharmacological treatment other than corticosteroids, intravenous immunoglobulin and plasma exchange for Guillain-Barre syndrome. *Cochrane Database Syst Rev*. 2011;(3):CD008630. DOI: 10.1002/14651858.CD008630.pub2

### 2.8 CSF Filtration

CSF filtration involves withdrawal of CSF through a lumbar catheter and reinfusion via specialized filters. One trial of 37 participants showed NO significant difference in any outcome between CSF filtration and plasma exchange. Patients for whom CSF filtration was first-line treatment improved faster than those who received it as rescue therapy, but the evidence is insufficient to recommend this approach.

> **Citation:** Hughes RA, Pritchard J, Hadden RD. Pharmacological treatment other than corticosteroids, intravenous immunoglobulin and plasma exchange for Guillain-Barre syndrome. *Cochrane Database Syst Rev*. 2011;(3):CD008630. DOI: 10.1002/14651858.CD008630.pub2

### 2.9 Immunoabsorption (IA)

Case reports and small case series suggest IA may be safe and effective, but it is expensive and not widely available. Currently NOT recommended as first- or second-line therapy by any major guideline.

### 2.10 Rescue Treatment Outcomes

A study of 20 poorly responsive GBS patients (Hughes 3+, failing initial treatment) who received rescue therapy (7 re-treated with IVIg, 13 with PE) found:
- **1 month:** Statistically significant improvement in Hughes disability scores (p=0.033)
- **3-6 months:** No significant differences between rescue treatment and control groups
- **Hospital stay:** Rescue-treated patients required significantly longer stays (median 55 vs 11 days, p=0.005)

> **Citation:** Alboudi AM, Sarathchandran P, Geblawi SS, et al. Rescue treatment in patients with poorly responsive Guillain-Barre syndrome. *SAGE Open Med*. 2019;7:2050312119840195. DOI: 10.1177/2050312119840195

## 4. BIOMARKERS PREDICTING TREATMENT RESPONSE

### 4.1 Neurofilament Light Chain (NfL)

NfL is the most studied emerging biomarker in GBS. It is released upon axonal damage and is measurable in both serum and CSF.

**Key Findings:**
- GBS patients have significantly higher NfL levels than healthy controls: serum 55.49 pg/mL vs 9.83 pg/mL; CSF 1308.5 pg/mL vs 440.24 pg/mL
- Baseline serum NfL independently predicts poor outcome (inability to walk at 6 months)
- High serum NfL correlates with axonal neurophysiology and disease severity
- Time to regain ability to walk unaided is significantly longer for patients with highest baseline NfL
- NfL levels are elevated at admission and normalize approximately 1 year post-onset
- The serum NfL Z-score (age-adjusted) improves predictive precision

**Limitations:** NfL is highly non-specific -- elevated in at least 80 peripheral and central nervous system diseases.

> **Citation:** Martin-Aguilar L, Camps-Renom P, Lleixa C, et al. Serum neurofilament light chain predicts long-term prognosis in Guillain-Barre syndrome patients. *J Neurol Neurosurg Psychiatry*. 2021;92(1):70-77. DOI: 10.1136/jnnp-2020-323899

> **Citation:** Dynamics and prognostic value of serum neurofilament light chain in Guillain-Barre syndrome. *eBioMedicine*. 2024. (IGOS consortium study)

### 4.2 Anti-Ganglioside Antibodies

Anti-ganglioside antibodies are detectable in up to 50% of GBS cases and have both diagnostic and prognostic value.

**Clinical Associations:**
- **Anti-GM1, anti-GD1a, anti-GM1b, anti-GalNAc-GD1a:** Associated with AMAN (acute motor axonal neuropathy)
- **Anti-GQ1b:** Present in >90% of Miller Fisher syndrome (sensitivity 88-100%, specificity 100%)
- **Anti-GD1b:** Associated with sensory-predominant GBS

**Prognostic Value:**
- High anti-GM1 IgG and IgM titers at entry are associated with poor clinical recovery
- Persistent high anti-GM1 IgG titers predict worse outcome
- Multiple concurrent anti-ganglioside antibodies predict unfavorable outcome
- IgG subclass, fine specificity, and complement-activating capacity influence pathogenicity

**Mechanism:** Anti-ganglioside antibodies localize at nodes of Ranvier, activate complement, induce the membrane attack complex (MAC), leading to Nav channel loss, paranodal myelin detachment, Ca2+ influx, calpain activation, and neurofilament degradation.

> **Citation:** High and persistent anti-GM1 antibody titers are associated with poor clinical recovery in Guillain-Barre syndrome. *Neurol Neuroimmunol Neuroinflamm*. 2022;10(1):e200107. DOI: 10.1212/NXI.0000000000200107

### 4.3 Complement Markers

- **Serum C3:** Higher C3 levels paradoxically associated with poorer outcomes (lower MRCSS, higher GBSDS, longer hospitalization, more frequent TRF). Age, MRCSS at admission, and baseline serum C3 are independent predictors of 1- and 3-month disability.
- **C5b-9 (Membrane Attack Complex):** Complement activation leads to C5b-9 formation on target cells; this is the pathogenic endpoint in antibody-mediated GBS
- **sC5b-9:** Soluble terminal complement complex; measured as a marker of complement activation

> **Citation:** Serum C3 complement levels predict prognosis and monitor disease activity in Guillain-Barre syndrome. *J Neurol Sci*. 2022;442:120412.

### 4.4 Peripherin

A newer biomarker that can differentiate acute nodo-paranodal axonal injury from slowly progressive demyelination. Significantly elevated in axonal GBS cases. Uniquely discriminates peripheral vs central NfL elevations (rises in GBS but not in CNS disorders).

### 4.5 Total Tau (T-tau)

Plasma (but not CSF) T-tau levels elevated in GBS. Plasma and CSF levels do not correlate, suggesting peripheral nerve origin. Clinical role remains to be determined.

### 4.6 IVIg Pharmacokinetics: Delta-IgG as a Treatment Response Predictor

The increase in serum IgG 2 weeks after IVIg (DeltaIgG) varies considerably between patients (mean 7.8 g/L, SD 5.6 g/L) and is independently associated with outcome:
- Low DeltaIgG = significantly slower recovery
- Low DeltaIgG = fewer patients able to walk at 6 months (log-rank p<0.001)
- In multivariate analysis, low DeltaIgG independently predicts poor outcome (p=0.022)
- Rapid IVIg clearance is associated with poor recovery

This finding has spurred interest in model-informed precision dosing and FcRn-targeted therapies.

> **Citation:** Kuitwaard K, de Gelder J, Tio-Gillen AP, et al. Pharmacokinetics of intravenous immunoglobulin and outcome in Guillain-Barre syndrome. *Ann Neurol*. 2009;66(5):597-603. DOI: 10.1002/ana.21737

## 5. SPECIAL POPULATIONS

### 5.1 GBS in Immunodeficient Patients

#### General Principles
GBS occurrence in severely immunocompromised patients is unexpected given its autoimmune pathogenesis, yet it is well-documented. Key observations:
- GBS can occur in patients with severe T-cell suppression
- Incomplete recovery in immunocompromised patients is consistent with the importance of T-cells for recovery
- No established treatment guidelines exist for GBS in immunocompromised patients

> **Citation:** Qureshi AI, Cook AA, Mishu HP, Krendel DA. Guillain-Barre syndrome in immunocompromised patients: a report of three patients and review of the literature. *Muscle Nerve*. 1997;20(8):1002-1007. DOI: 10.1002/(sici)1097-4598(199708)20:8<1002::aid-mus10>3.0.co;2-x

#### HIV-Associated GBS
- Occurs in early and late stages of HIV infection, and may follow AIDS onset
- HIV-GBS may have more frequent recurrent episodes or evolution to CIDP compared to HIV-negative patients
- Treatment: Standard IVIg and PE used alongside antiretroviral therapy
- For CMV- or VZV-associated GBS in HIV: add ganciclovir or foscarnet

> **Citation:** Brannagan TH 3rd, Zhou Y. HIV-associated Guillain-Barre syndrome. *J Neurol Sci*. 2003;208(1-2):39-42. DOI: 10.1016/S0022-510X(02)00418-5

#### Transplant Recipients
- Documented in kidney and liver transplant recipients
- Treatment approach: IVIg or PE, with consideration of discontinuing the suspected immunosuppressant (e.g., tacrolimus)
- Case example: 44-year-old kidney transplant recipient on tacrolimus/mycophenolate/prednisone developed AMAN variant GBS. Managed with IVIg and switch from tacrolimus to cyclosporine A, achieving strength recovery to 4/5 in all limbs by day 10.

> **Citation:** Pina-Rosales A, Gonzalez-Salido J, Barron-Cervantes D, et al. Guillain-Barre syndrome in an immunocompromised patient: a case report. *Cureus*. 2024;16(2):e54380. DOI: 10.7759/cureus.54380

#### CVID (Common Variable Immunodeficiency)
- GBS has been reported as a complication of CVID, documented in 2/8 pediatric CVID patients in one series
- Presents a therapeutic paradox: CVID patients already receive IgG replacement therapy, which is also the standard GBS treatment
- Management requires individualized approach

### 5.2 GBS with Concurrent Autoimmune Conditions

#### SLE-Related GBS
- Polyneuropathy prevalence in SLE: 1.5-36%
- More frequent in patients with CNS involvement and high disease activity
- Diagnosis requires meticulous differentiation from SLE peripheral neuropathy
- Treatment: Cyclophosphamide + corticosteroids is first-line for SLE-related GBS (85.7% response rate)
- Standard GBS treatments (IVIg, PE) may be used but cyclophosphamide appears more effective for SLE-specific cases

> **Citation:** Is Guillain-Barre syndrome related to systemic lupus erythematosus or other autoimmune diseases? *Front Neurol*. 2024;14:1336794. DOI: 10.3389/fneur.2023.1336794

#### Anti-Neurofascin Autoimmune Nodopathies
- Anti-NF155 and anti-NF186 positive presentations can mimic acute GBS
- Characteristically REFRACTORY to IVIg
- Respond to rituximab and corticosteroids
- Key clinical pointer: young patients with severe demyelinating neuropathy who fail IVIg should be tested for nodal/paranodal antibodies

### 5.3 Pediatric Refractory GBS

**Guideline (2020 AWMF):**
- IVIg is first-line for severe or persistently progressive GBS in children
- Plasmapheresis recommended for IVIg intolerance or inefficacy
- Corticosteroids ineffective for GBS but may be considered if A-CIDP is suspected
- Apparent non-responders treated on individual decisions
- A "zipper method" (sequential PE + IVIg) has been explored for severe pediatric cases

**Outcomes:** Pediatric GBS generally has shorter illness and more complete recovery than adults, but two-thirds of children have persistent sequelae (paresthesia 43%, pain 35%, fatigue 31%).

> **Citation:** Korinthenberg R, Trollmann R, Felderhoff-Muser U, et al. Diagnosis and treatment of Guillain-Barre syndrome in childhood and adolescence: An evidence- and consensus-based guideline. *Eur J Paediatr Neurol*. 2020;25:5-16. DOI: 10.1016/j.ejpn.2020.01.003

### 5.4 Elderly Refractory GBS

**Risk Profile:**
- Age >60 years is an independent negative prognostic factor
- Higher mortality rates (approximately 20% in those requiring mechanical ventilation)
- More frequent need for mechanical ventilation (17-30% of all GBS patients)
- Significant predictors of poor outcome: older age, severe deficits at onset, cranial nerve involvement, mechanical ventilation requirement, axonal patterns on NCS

**Treatment Considerations:**
- Standard IVIg and PE remain first-line
- Higher risk of thromboembolic complications with IVIg (especially relevant given SID-GBS findings about second-dose risks)
- Longer ICU and hospital stays
- Greater risk of secondary complications (pneumonia, sepsis, autonomic dysfunction)

**Prognostic Tools:** The modified EGOS (mEGOS) incorporates age and is validated for predicting inability to walk at 6 months.

## 6. EXPERT CONSENSUS AND GUIDELINES

### 6.1 EAN/PNS Guideline (2023) -- PRIMARY CURRENT GUIDELINE

The most comprehensive and current guideline was published jointly by the European Academy of Neurology (EAN) and Peripheral Nerve Society (PNS) in 2023 using GRADE methodology.

**Key Treatment Recommendations:**

| Recommendation | Strength |
|----------------|----------|
| IVIg (0.4 g/kg x 5 days) within 2 weeks of weakness onset for non-ambulatory patients | Strong |
| PE (12-15 L across 4-5 sessions) within 4 weeks of onset for non-ambulatory patients | Strong |
| AGAINST second IVIg course for poor prognosis | Strong |
| AGAINST oral corticosteroids | Strong |
| AGAINST IV corticosteroids alone | Weak |
| AGAINST combined PE followed immediately by IVIg | Recommendation |
| Eculizumab NOT recommended | Guideline statement |
| Gabapentinoids, TCAs, or carbamazepine for pain | Weak |
| Use mEGOS and mEGRIS for prognostication | Recommendation |

**Notable Changes:**
- Removed the AIDP vs AMAN/AMSAN distinction for treatment purposes (no impact on clinical management)
- Benefits of vaccination significantly outweigh marginal GBS risk

> **Citation:** van Doorn PA, Van den Bergh PYK, Hadden RDM, et al. European Academy of Neurology/Peripheral Nerve Society guideline on diagnosis and treatment of Guillain-Barre syndrome. *Eur J Neurol*. 2023;30(12):3646-3674. DOI: 10.1111/ene.16073

### 6.2 IGOS (International GBS Outcome Study)

**Overview:** Initiated in 2012, IGOS is the largest real-world GBS database worldwide, enrolling 2,000 patients from 21 countries across 5 continents (enrollment target reached May 2024 with 3-year follow-up).

**Data Collected:** Demographics, preceding infections, clinical features, diagnostic findings, treatment, course, outcomes, CSF, serial blood samples (serum and DNA), electrophysiology, and patient-reported outcome measures.

**Research Output:** 18 published studies and 28 ongoing studies covering:
- Preceding events and genetic studies
- Proteomics and biomarkers
- Diagnostic criteria validation
- Electrophysiology
- Prognostic modelling
- IVIg pharmacokinetics
- Anti-neural antibodies
- CSF biomarkers
- Trial design methodology

**Key IGOS Contributions:**
- Validated the 5% TRF frequency in GBS
- Established the real-world comparator cohort used in the imlifidase Phase 2 analysis
- Informed development of Bayesian adaptive platform trial designs for future GBS studies

> **Citation:** Jacobs BC, van den Berg B, Stridh P, et al. International Guillain-Barre Syndrome Outcome Study: protocol of a prospective observational cohort study on clinical and biological predictors of disease course and outcome in Guillain-Barre syndrome. *J Peripher Nerv Syst*. 2017;22(2):68-76. DOI: 10.1111/jns.12209

### 6.3 Cochrane Systematic Reviews

#### IVIg for GBS
Seven trials (n=623 severely affected): IVIg and PE are equally effective (MD 0.02 grades improvement favoring IVIg, not significant). IVIg significantly more likely to be completed than PE. IVIg probably hastens recovery vs supportive care alone in children. No benefit from adding IVIg after PE.

> **Citation:** Hughes RA, Swan AV, van Doorn PA. Intravenous immunoglobulin for Guillain-Barre syndrome. *Cochrane Database Syst Rev*. 2014;(9):CD002063. DOI: 10.1002/14651858.CD002063.pub6

#### Plasma Exchange for GBS
PE is the first treatment proven superior to supportive care alone. Mild GBS: 2 PE sessions > none. Moderate GBS: 4 sessions > 2. Severe GBS: 6 sessions no better than 4. More beneficial when started within 7 days but still beneficial up to 30 days after onset.

> **Citation:** Chevret S, Hughes RA, Annane D. Plasma exchange for Guillain-Barre syndrome. *Cochrane Database Syst Rev*. 2017;(2):CD001798. DOI: 10.1002/14651858.CD001798.pub3

#### Other Pharmacological Treatments
Evaluated interferon beta-1a (n=13, no benefit), brain-derived neurotrophic factor (n=10, no benefit), CSF filtration (n=37, no benefit over PE). Evidence quality rated as very low for all.

> **Citation:** Hughes RA, Pritchard J, Hadden RD. Pharmacological treatment other than corticosteroids, intravenous immunoglobulin and plasma exchange for Guillain-Barre syndrome. *Cochrane Database Syst Rev*. 2011;(3):CD008630. DOI: 10.1002/14651858.CD008630.pub2

### 6.4 AAN Practice Parameter

The American Academy of Neurology endorses the EAN/PNS 2023 guideline. Historical AAN practice parameters (2003) established the Level A evidence for PE and IVIg and recommended against combination therapy.

### 6.5 Pediatric Guideline (AWMF 2020)

German-Speaking Society of Neuropediatrics (GNP) evidence- and consensus-based guideline covering childhood and adolescent GBS diagnosis and treatment. See Section 5.3 for details.

## 7. EMERGING THERAPIES (2022-2025)

### 7.1 Tanruprubart (ANX005) -- C1q Complement Inhibitor ***MOST PROMISING***

Tanruprubart is a first-in-class humanized monoclonal antibody that blocks C1q, the initiating molecule of the classical complement cascade. This represents the most advanced and promising new therapy for GBS.

#### Phase 1 Study (Bangladesh)
- **Design:** 50 patients (38 ANX005, 12 placebo) with recent-onset GBS
- **Results:** Well tolerated, no dose-limiting toxicities. Effective C1q inhibition and NfL reduction. Improvements in MRC sum scores, GBS-DS, and ONLS vs placebo.
- **Conclusion:** Supports ANX005 as targeted therapy modulating the classical complement pathway.

> **Citation:** Mohammad QD, Islam Z, Papri N, et al. Results from a Phase 1 study evaluating the safety, tolerability, pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics, and efficacy of ANX005, a C1q inhibitor, in patients with Guillain-Barre syndrome. *J Peripher Nerv Syst*. 2025;30(1):e70009. DOI: 10.1111/jns.70009

#### Phase 3 Study (Bangladesh/Philippines) -- NCT04701164
- **Design:** 242 patients, randomized 1:1:1 to single IV infusion of ANX005 30 mg/kg, 75 mg/kg, or placebo (WITHOUT standard IVIg or PE)
- **Primary Endpoint (Week 8):** 30 mg/kg dose: OR 2.4 (95% CI 1.29-4.50; p=0.0058) -- STATISTICALLY SIGNIFICANT
- **Key Secondary Outcomes:**
  - Improved function evident by Week 1 (OR 7.2, 95% CI 3.07-16.96; p<0.001)
  - 2.5x more patients returned to normal health (OR 4.1, p=0.0092)
  - Walk independently 31 days earlier (p=0.0211)
  - 28 fewer days on mechanical ventilation (p=0.0356)
  - 29% of 30 mg/kg group achieved GBS-DS 0-1 at Week 8 vs 12% placebo
- **Safety:** Comparable across groups; infusion reactions in 35% (transient)
- **Note:** The 75 mg/kg dose did NOT meet primary endpoint (inverted dose-response)

**Regulatory Status (as of March 2026):**
- FDA Fast Track and Orphan Drug designations
- EMA Orphan Drug designation
- EMA MAA submitted January 2026
- FDA BLA planned for 2026
- FORWARD open-label study ongoing in US/Europe (supporting broad label including pediatric patients)
- Would be the FIRST FDA-approved targeted therapy for GBS if approved

### 7.2 Imlifidase (IdeS) -- IgG-Degrading Enzyme

Imlifidase is a unique IgG-cleaving enzyme from *Streptococcus pyogenes* that rapidly cleaves all four IgG subclasses into F(ab')2 and Fc fragments within hours.

#### Phase 2 Study (15-HMedIdeS-09) -- NCT03943589
- **Design:** Open-label, single-arm, multicenter (UK, France, Netherlands), 30 adult GBS patients
- **Intervention:** Single dose 0.25 mg/kg imlifidase + standard IVIg
- **Results (n=27 evaluable):**
  - Median time to 1-grade improvement on GBS-DS: 6 days
  - Median time to independent walking (GBS-DS <=2): 16 days
  - Week 1: 37% walking independently; mean MRC improvement 10.7 points
  - Week 8: 67% walking independently; 40.7% could run
  - 6 months: 63% could run or had no functional disability
- **Comparison vs IGOS cohort (754 severe GBS patients on IVIg alone):**
  - 3 weeks sooner to improve by 1 GBS-DS step (p=0.002)
  - 6 weeks sooner to return to independent walking (p=0.03)
  - Week 1: 6.4x more likely to walk independently (p<0.001)
  - Week 4: 4.2x more likely to walk independently (p=0.005)
- **Safety:** Well tolerated; no serious infusion-related adverse events
- **Sponsor:** Hansa Biopharma

### 7.3 Efgartigimod -- FcRn Inhibitor

Efgartigimod is an IgG1 Fc fragment with enhanced FcRn binding that accelerates endogenous IgG catabolism. Already FDA-approved for generalized myasthenia gravis and CIDP (Vyvgart).

#### Case Reports in GBS (2024-2025)
- Two Chinese GBS patients treated with efgartigimod (10 mg/kg x 2 doses within 5 days): muscle strength improved gradually; independent walking achieved at 4 weeks.
- One MFS-GBS overlap patient who worsened despite IVIg and developed respiratory failure: efgartigimod (800 mg x 4 doses) led to symptom relief with gradual IgG reduction.
- Case report of efgartigimod as add-on in refractory GBS.

#### Ongoing Phase II RCT
- Comparing efgartigimod to IVIg in GBS
- Location: United States
- Status: Active

> **Citation:** Zhang H, Ma J, Feng Y, et al. Efgartigimod in the treatment of Guillain-Barre syndrome. *J Neurol*. 2024;271(6):3506-3511. DOI: 10.1007/s00415-024-12321-4

### 7.4 Crovalimab -- Long-Acting C5 Inhibitor

- Phase III RCT ongoing: crovalimab + IVIg vs placebo + IVIg in severe GBS
- Subcutaneous C5 inhibitor (every 4 weeks dosing)
- Already approved for PNH in US (June 2024), EU (August 2024), Japan (April 2024), China (February 2024)
- Sponsor: Genentech/Roche/Chugai

### 7.5 Precision Medicine / Pharmacokinetic-Guided Dosing

A paradigm shift is emerging toward individualized IVIg dosing based on pharmacokinetic profiles:
- FcRn saturation capacity varies between patients
- Population PK models have been developed from 177 GBS patients with 589 sequential IgG samples
- FCGRT promoter gene polymorphisms do NOT predict IVIg PK or clinical course (ruling out a simple genetic predictor)
- Model-informed precision dosing may optimize IVIg therapy and reduce over-/under-treatment

> **Citation:** Population pharmacokinetic modelling of intravenous immunoglobulin treatment in patients with Guillain-Barre syndrome. *Clin Pharmacokinet*. 2022. DOI: 10.1007/s40262-022-01136-z

### 7.6 Methodological Innovations in GBS Trial Design

Emerging approaches to improve efficiency of GBS research:
- **Bayesian adaptive platform studies:** Enable simultaneous evaluation of multiple treatments
- **Ordinal outcome analyses:** Preserve statistical power vs dichotomized scales
- **Observational comparative studies:** Using propensity score matching (e.g., IGOS comparator cohort)
- **Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs):** Capturing quality-of-life beyond functional disability

> **Citation:** Wiegers EJA, Jacobs BC. New insights in the immune treatment of Guillain-Barre syndrome. *Curr Opin Neurol*. 2025;38(5):471-477. DOI: 10.1097/WCO.0000000000001408

### 7.7 Summary of Therapeutic Pipeline

| Agent | Target | Phase | Status | Key Finding |

## KEY SUMMARY FOR CLINICIANS

### Current Standard of Care
1. **IVIg** (0.4 g/kg/day x 5 days) or **PE** (4-5 sessions) -- equally effective
2. Start within 2 weeks (IVIg) or 4 weeks (PE) of weakness onset
3. Do NOT give second IVIg course, do NOT combine PE+IVIg, do NOT give corticosteroids alone

### For Treatment-Resistant Cases
1. First, reassess diagnosis -- consider A-CIDP or autoimmune nodopathy if >=3 fluctuations or progression >8 weeks
2. Test for anti-neurofascin antibodies (NF155, NF186) in IVIg non-responders
3. No proven second-line therapy exists in guidelines
4. For TRF (1-2 fluctuations within 8 weeks): repeat IVIg or switch to PE (practice-based, not evidence-based)
5. Rescue PE after IVIg failure may provide short-term benefit at 1 month but not at 3-6 months
6. For SLE-related GBS: cyclophosphamide + corticosteroids
7. Rituximab may be considered for anti-neurofascin positive cases or selected refractory cases (off-label, case report level evidence)

### On the Horizon
- **Tanruprubart** may become the first FDA-approved targeted GBS therapy (2026-2027)
- **Imlifidase** awaiting Phase 3 trial
- **Efgartigimod** Phase II RCT ongoing in US
- **Pharmacokinetic-guided IVIg dosing** may optimize first-line therapy
