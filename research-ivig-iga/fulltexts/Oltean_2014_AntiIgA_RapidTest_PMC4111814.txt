DETECTION OF ANTI-IGA ANTIBODIES USING THE PARTICLE GEL IMMUNOASSAY: A RAPID TEST FOR INCREASED PATIENT SAFETY

Simona Oltean, Andreea Epure, Karin Lindstrom, Cecilia Pardi
Transfusion Centre, Department of Clinical Immunology and Transfusion Medicine, Sahlgrenska University Hospital, Gothenburg, Sweden

ABSTRACT

Patient safety in transfusion medicine requires reliable methods to prevent serious complications. Anti-IgA antibodies can cause anaphylactic reactions in IgA-deficient individuals. Standard quantitative testing is time-intensive; emergency situations demand rapid results. This study validated a fast assay for identifying patients with anti-IgA antibodies.

Forty-six samples (six controls, forty IgA-deficient patients) underwent blind analysis using DiaMed particle gel immunoassays (ID-PaGIA) for IgA deficiency and anti-IgA detection, with results compared against fluorescence enzyme immunoassay reference testing.

ID-PaGIA demonstrated 91.7% sensitivity and 97.1% specificity for IgA deficiency; 89.3% sensitivity and 100% specificity for anti-IgA detection. Test reproducibility reached 100%.

INTRODUCTION

Emergency transfusion in IgA-deficient individuals presents medical and logistical challenges. If undiagnosed, patients with anti-IgA antibodies may experience severe transfusion reactions and anaphylaxis when receiving blood components containing even minute amounts of IgA. Rapid recognition of IgA-related reactions is essential for patient outcomes.

Current diagnostic methods prove time-consuming and labor-intensive. Hemagglutination and flow cytometry offer reliability but demand technical expertise and extended processing times. Particle gel immunoassay (PaGIA) represents a newer, qualitative approach.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Serum Samples: Study spanned September 2010 through February 2011. Forty serum samples from patients previously tested for IgA deficiency and anti-IgA antibody presence via FEIA underwent analysis. Six blood donor samples served as healthy controls.

Standard Diagnostic Method: Reference laboratory employed enzyme fluoroimmunoassays from Phadia, executed on automated ImmunoCAP 250 analyzer (Thermo Fisher Scientific/Phadia, Uppsala, Sweden).

Reference classifications:
- IgA values <0.0021 mg/dL: total IgA-deficiency
- IgA values 0.0021-0.2 mg/dL: partial IgA deficiency
- Anti-IgA <=5 units/mL: negative
- Anti-IgA 6-50 units/mL: low positive
- Anti-IgA >=50 units/mL: intensely positive

Particle Gel Immunoassays: Three clinical laboratory technologists blindly analyzed samples using DiaMed GmbH kits (IgA deficiency test B020701, Anti-IgA antibody test kit B020601). Manufacturer-specified IgA detection limit: <=0.05 mg/dL.

Procedure: 10 microL sample pipetted into reaction chamber, 50 microL particle suspension added. Five-minute incubation, then centrifugation at 1,174 rpm for ten minutes. Results read within fifteen minutes.

Result interpretation:
- Positive: Complete agglutination as red line on top of gel or just below surface
- Weak positive: Particles reaching bottom with visible upper gel agglutinates
- Negative: Complete sedimentation of particles as pellet at bottom with no agglutinated particles visible within gel
- Mixed field: Fine red surface lines with pelletted bottom particles

RESULTS

Overview:
- 8 patients with total IgA deficiency, no anti-IgA: All correctly identified on both cards
- 26 patients with total IgA deficiency and anti-IgA: 23 correctly positive on both; 3 false-negative on anti-IgA card (all had low antibody levels)
- 4 patients with partial IgA deficiency, no anti-IgA: 3 correctly identified; 1 false-negative on IgA card
- 1 patient with partial IgA deficiency and anti-IgA: Correctly identified on both
- 6 healthy controls: All correctly identified

ID-PaGIA Accuracy:
IgA detection: Sensitivity 91.7% (95% CI: 61.5-99.8%), Specificity 97.1% (95% CI: 84.7-99.9%), PPV 91.7%, NPV 97.1%, p<0.0001
Anti-IgA detection: Sensitivity 89.3% (95% CI: 71.8-97.7%), Specificity 100% (95% CI: 81.5-100%), PPV 100%, NPV 85.7%, p<0.0001

Test reproducibility: 100% across three different technologists.

False results: 4 false-negative reactions (1 IgA card, 3 anti-IgA cards) and 1 false-positive IgA card reaction. Two of four patients with very low anti-IgA levels (6-7 U/mL) were correctly identified positive by ID-PaGIA.

DISCUSSION

Selective IgA deficiency represents the most prevalent primary immunodeficiency in Western populations, varying between 1:406 and 1:886 in various series of blood donors. Approximately one-third of IgA-deficient individuals may develop anti-IgA antibodies.

Clinical significance of specific anti-IgA levels remains unclear. Severe IgA-deficient patients with anti-IgA antibodies experience severe transfusion complications when receiving IgA-containing blood components. Emergency situations intensify urgency when patients require immediate continuing transfusion after anaphylactic reactions.

Preventing serious transfusion reactions and rapid interpretation during reactions present challenges. Quantitative IgA assays are faster and more widely accessible than anti-IgA assays, so many anaphylaxis evaluation algorithms begin with pre-transfusion serum IgA determination, though definitive IgA-mediated allergic reaction diagnosis requires anti-IgA demonstration.

Results demonstrated very high sensitivity and specificity for both IgA and anti-IgA gel cards. The IgA test detects minute IgA amounts. Among five partial IgA-deficiency patients, only one false-negative appeared, suggesting ID-PaGIA and FEIA share similar IgA detection limits.

Based on findings, the authors introduced ID-PaGIA anti-IgA antibody testing into transfusion practice for all anaphylactic transfusion reaction emergency diagnosis and for testing known IgA-deficient individuals requiring blood transfusions.

Although ID-PaGIA lacks anti-IgA level quantification, it is readily available and provides an effective and simple strategy for the diagnosis or prevention of anaphylaxis related to the presence of anti-IgA antibodies. The approach could generate significant blood bank savings regarding staffing and rare IgA-depleted component utilization.
