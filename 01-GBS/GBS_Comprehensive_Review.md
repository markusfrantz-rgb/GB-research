## 1. Pathophysiology

### 1.1 Overview

Guillain-Barre syndrome (GBS) is an acute immune-mediated polyradiculoneuropathy characterized by rapid-onset limb weakness with loss of tendon reflexes. The pathophysiology involves two pivotal stages: (1) initiation by an immunological trigger, and (2) immune-mediated disruption of axons and/or myelin in the peripheral nervous system (Hughes, 2024; Bellanti & Rinaldi, 2024).

### 1.2 Subtypes

GBS encompasses a spectrum of clinicopathological variants, classified primarily by electrodiagnostic and immunological features:

#### 1.2.1 Acute Inflammatory Demyelinating Polyradiculoneuropathy (AIDP)

- **Prevalence**: Most common form in Europe and North America (~69% of cases) (Hughes, 2024)
- **Pathology**: Segmental demyelination of peripheral nerves with multifocal mononuclear cell infiltration throughout the peripheral nervous system
- **Mechanism**: Macrophage-mediated demyelination rather than antibody-driven alone. Macrophages invade via multiple routes:
  - Internodes via Schmidt-Lanterman incisures
  - Paranodes separating terminal Schwann cell loops from axons
- **Complement involvement**: Complement components are deposited along myelin sheaths and at the paranodes, consistent with a role for antibody and complement (Koike et al., 2020)
- **T-cell autoimmunity**: Recent landmark research identified autoreactive CD4+ T cells targeting myelin proteins P2, P0, and PMP22 in the blood of 12 of 15 patients with AIDP but not in AMAN or other neuropathies. These cells display Th1-like genes, a cytotoxic phenotype, and genes characteristic of autoimmunity, and persist in cerebrospinal fluid and nerve biopsies (Sukenikova et al., 2024)
- **Antibody targets**: Few patients with AIDP have antibodies to single gangliosides (~11.8%), but the proportion increases to 62.4% when gangliosides are presented in heterodimeric complexes (Rinaldi et al., 2013)

#### 1.2.2 Acute Motor Axonal Neuropathy (AMAN)

- **Prevalence**: More common in Asia, particularly China, Japan, and Bangladesh (~30-65% in some Asian cohorts vs. ~5-10% in Western countries)
- **Pathology**: Primary axonal degeneration of motor fibers with minimal sensory involvement; macrophages penetrate the nodes of Ranvier with complement deposition
- **Mechanism**: Antibodies bind to gangliosides at the nodes of Ranvier, activating complement and forming the membrane attack complex (MAC), causing calcium influx, calpain-mediated cytoskeletal disruption, and ultimately nodal/paranodal damage (McGonigal et al., 2010; Susuki et al., 2007)
- **Key antibodies**: Anti-GM1 IgG (associated with pure motor forms), anti-GD1a IgG (detectable in ~60% of AMAN cases vs. only 4% of AIDP)
- **Reversible conduction failure**: In some AMAN cases, conduction block at nodes of Ranvier is reversible without Wallerian degeneration, explaining rapid recovery in a subset of patients

#### 1.2.3 Acute Motor-Sensory Axonal Neuropathy (AMSAN)

- **Pathology**: Affects both motor and sensory axons with axonal degeneration and minimal inflammation/demyelination
- **Clinical significance**: Shares many similarities with AMAN but the attack is more severe or longer lasting, resulting in more intense and ultimately diffuse Wallerian-like degeneration
- **Prognosis**: Generally worse than AMAN due to involvement of both motor and sensory axons

#### 1.2.4 Miller Fisher Syndrome (MFS)

- **Prevalence**: 1-7% of GBS in Western countries; 15-25% in Asian populations (Taiwan ~18%, Hong Kong 9%) (Arancibia-Garavilla et al., 2023)
- **Clinical triad**: Ophthalmoplegia, ataxia, and areflexia (all three present in ~80% of cases)
- **Associated features**: Ptosis (60%), facial nerve palsy (30-50%), sensory deficits (20-50%)
- **Key antibody**: Anti-GQ1b IgG present in ~85-90% of cases with 100% specificity
- **GQ1b localization**: Ganglioside GQ1b is concentrated in paranodal myelin of oculomotor nerves (CN III, IV, VI), dorsal root ganglia, and neuromuscular spindle fibers, explaining the selective vulnerability
- **Prognosis**: Generally self-limiting; ataxia resolves at median 35 days, ophthalmoplegia at ~93 days, full recovery at ~6 months
- **Recurrence**: Documented in ~12% of patients

#### 1.2.5 Anti-GQ1b Antibody Spectrum

MFS exists within a continuous spectrum of anti-GQ1b-associated disorders:

| Variant | Key Features | Anti-GQ1b Positivity |
|---------|-------------|---------------------|
| Miller Fisher syndrome | Ophthalmoplegia + ataxia + areflexia | ~85-90% |
| Partial MFS | Two of three MFS features | ~85% |
| GBS/MFS overlap | Both GBS and MFS features | ~70% |
| Bickerstaff brainstem encephalitis (BBE) | Ataxia + ophthalmoplegia + hyperreflexia + altered consciousness | ~66% |
| Acute ophthalmoplegia | Isolated extraocular/intrinsic eye muscle paralysis | Variable |
| Acute ataxic neuropathy | Ataxia without ophthalmoplegia | Variable |

(Bellanti & Rinaldi, 2024; Shahrizaila & Yuki, 2013)

#### 1.2.6 Other Variants

- **Pharyngeal-cervical-brachial (PCB)**: Bilateral facial weakness with arm paresthesias/weakness; no lower limb involvement; associated with anti-GT1a antibodies and bulbar involvement
- **Paraparetic GBS**: Isolated lower limb involvement
- **Pure sensory GBS**: Sensory symptoms with reduced/absent reflexes, no motor weakness
- **Bifacial weakness with paresthesias**: Bilateral facial palsy with distal limb paresthesias

#### 1.2.7 Panneurofascin Antibody-Associated GBS

A recently identified distinct subgroup with IgG1 antibodies targeting both nodal (NF186) and paranodal (NF155) neurofascin:
- Severe acute polyradiculoneuropathy
- Poor response to standard IVIg and PE treatments
- Improvement after rituximab therapy
- Suggests antibody-based biomarkers may stratify treatment responses (Hughes, 2024)

### 1.3 Molecular Mimicry

The molecular mimicry hypothesis provides the most well-established pathogenic model for GBS:

**Mechanism**: Structural similarity between microbial surface components (particularly lipo-oligosaccharides [LOS]) and human nerve gangliosides triggers cross-reactive immune responses. The best-characterized example involves *Campylobacter jejuni*:

1. *C. jejuni* LOS contains ganglioside-like glycan structures (e.g., GM1, GD1a, GQ1b epitopes)
2. Host immune response generates antibodies against these LOS structures
3. These antibodies cross-react with structurally similar gangliosides on peripheral nerve membranes
4. Antibody binding triggers complement activation and nerve damage

**Specificity of mimicry determines clinical phenotype**:
- LOS mimicking GM1/GD1a --> AMAN (motor axonal)
- LOS mimicking GQ1b --> MFS (ophthalmoplegia/ataxia)
- LOS mimicking GM1 + GD1a --> Severe motor involvement

(Hafer-Macko et al., 1996; Yuki et al., 2004; Hughes, 2024)

### 1.4 Ganglioside Antibodies

Gangliosides are sialic acid-containing glycosphingolipids abundant in the peripheral nervous system. Anti-ganglioside antibodies are detected in approximately 60% of GBS patients; absence does not exclude the diagnosis.

| Antibody | Associated Subtype/Variant | Clinical Correlates | Frequency |
|----------|---------------------------|--------------------|-----------|
| Anti-GM1 IgG | AMAN, motor GBS | Pure motor weakness; elevated IgG correlates with poor recovery | 20-69% of GBS |
| Anti-GM1 IgM | Multifocal motor neuropathy (MMN) | Important differential: MMN does NOT respond to PE | ~80% of MMN |
| Anti-GM2 | GBS (CMV-associated) | Suggests CMV as preceding infection | Variable |
| Anti-GD1a IgG | AMAN, severe motor GBS | Younger patients, facial weakness, axonal damage, poor outcomes | ~60% of AMAN |
| Anti-GD1b | Sensory ataxic GBS | Sensory-predominant symptoms | Variable |
| Anti-GQ1b IgG | MFS, BBE, GBS/MFS overlap | Ophthalmoplegia, ataxia; ~100% specificity for MFS | ~85-90% of MFS |
| Anti-GT1a | PCB variant | Bulbar involvement | Variable |
| Anti-GalNAc-GD1a | AMAN | Most potent target antigen in AMAN; located at nodes of Ranvier | Variable |

**Important clinical note**: At least 40% of GBS patients do not have identifiable serum or CSF autoantibodies. Absence does not exclude GBS, does not impact clinical management, and has no monitoring or prognostic utility (Bellanti & Rinaldi, 2024).

### 1.5 Immune Cascade

#### Complement-Mediated Damage (Axonal GBS)

The complement cascade is central to axonal GBS pathophysiology:

1. **Antibody binding**: IgG1/IgG3 anti-ganglioside antibodies bind to nodal gangliosides (GM1, GD1a, GalNAc-GD1a)
2. **Classical complement activation**: Antibody-antigen complexes activate C1q, initiating the classical complement pathway
3. **C3d and MAC deposition**: Complement component C3d and membrane attack complex (MAC, C5b-9) deposit on the axolemma at nodes of Ranvier
4. **Sodium channel disruption**: Loss of voltage-gated sodium channel (Nav) clusters at nodes of Ranvier, causing conduction failure
5. **Paranodal disruption**: Detachment of paranodal myelin loops from the axolemma
6. **Calcium influx and cytoskeletal damage**: MAC pore formation allows calcium influx, activating calpain-mediated cytoskeletal disruption
7. **Macrophage infiltration**: Macrophages enter the periaxonal space, contributing to Wallerian degeneration
8. **Final common pathway**: Regardless of whether initial injury is axonal or glial, there is a common final pathway leading to axonal disruption and functional failure

(McGonigal et al., 2010; Susuki et al., 2007; Halstead et al., 2004; McGonigal et al., 2022)

#### Demyelination Cascade (AIDP)

1. **T-cell priming**: Autoreactive CD4+ T cells recognizing myelin proteins (P0, P2, PMP22) are activated (Sukenikova et al., 2024)
2. **Blood-nerve barrier breach**: Activated T cells cross the blood-nerve barrier
3. **Macrophage recruitment**: T cells recruit macrophages via cytokine/chemokine signaling
4. **Complement deposition**: Complement components deposit on myelin sheaths
5. **Myelin vesicular degeneration**: MAC-mediated myelin vesiculation
6. **Macrophage-mediated stripping**: Macrophages strip damaged myelin from axons
7. **Secondary axonal damage**: Severe/prolonged demyelination leads to secondary axonal loss

### 1.6 Genetic Susceptibility

Meta-analyses have identified genetic polymorphisms in immune pathways more common in GBS patients:
- FcgammaR IIA (Fc receptor)
- TNF-alpha gene polymorphisms (moderate association with GBS risk)
- TLR4 (Toll-like receptor 4)
- HLA DRB alleles
- Mannose-binding lectin (MBL) gene polymorphisms (associated with GBS severity; contributes to complement activation)

These involve B-cell, T-cell, and innate immunity pathways (Wu et al., 2012; Geleijns et al., 2006).

## 2. Epidemiology

### 2.1 Incidence

- **Global estimate**: Approximately 100,000 new cases of GBS annually worldwide (Bellanti & Rinaldi, 2024)
- **Overall incidence**: 0.8-1.9 per 100,000 person-years in Europe and North America (Sejvar et al., 2011)
- **Range across studies (1985-2020)**: 0.30-6.08 per 100,000 inhabitants across 72 studies in 44 countries (Wachira et al., 2023)

**Regional variation**:

| Region | Incidence (per 100,000/year) |
|--------|-----|
| Japan | 0.44 |
| China | 0.67 |
| Tanzania | 0.83 |
| Finland | 0.84 |
| Europe/North America (typical) | 0.8-1.9 |
| Chile | 2.12 |
| Bangladesh | 3.25 |

(Bellanti & Rinaldi, 2024; Wachira et al., 2023)

### 2.2 Demographics

- **Age**: Incidence increases with age; peak incidence at 50-70 years. Incidence in adults >=65 years can reach 12.97/100,000 person-years (Wachira et al., 2023)
- **Sex**: Male predominance with male:female ratio of approximately 1.5:1 (range 1.0-3.3:1 across studies). Males: 0.53-4.95/100,000 person-years; Females: 0.31-3.4/100,000 person-years
- **Seasonal variation**: Documented in some regions, likely reflecting seasonal patterns of triggering infections
- **Pediatric incidence**: 0.37-4.7/100,000 depending on age group and region

### 2.3 Geographic Variation in Clinical Phenotype

| Feature | Europe/Americas | Bangladesh | Asia (excl. Bangladesh) |
|---------|----------------|------------|------------------------|
| Motor-sensory | 69% | 31% | 43% |
| Pure motor | 31% | 69% | 57% |
| Axonal | ~10-15% | 36% | Variable |
| MFS prevalence | ~5% | Rare | 15-25% (Japan ~22%) |
| Mortality | ~5% | ~17% | ~9% |

(Hughes, 2024)

### 2.4 Infectious Triggers

GBS typically follows an antecedent infection by 1-6 weeks. Approximately two-thirds of patients report a preceding respiratory or gastrointestinal infection.

#### 2.4.1 Campylobacter jejuni

- **Most commonly identified pathogenic trigger** worldwide
- Found in ~30% of GBS cases (IGOS-1000 cohort; Doets et al., 2022)
- Approximately 1 in 1,000 *C. jejuni* infections leads to GBS
- Molecular mimicry between *C. jejuni* LOS and nerve gangliosides (GM1, GD1a, GQ1b) is the best-characterized mechanism
- Associated with AMAN subtype, preceding diarrhea, anti-GM1/GD1a antibodies
- Preceding diarrhea is a negative prognostic factor (indicates more severe axonal damage)

(Hafer-Macko et al., 1996; Griffin et al., 1996; Doets et al., 2022)

#### 2.4.2 Cytomegalovirus (CMV)

- Found in ~4% of GBS cases (IGOS-1000)
- Associated with anti-GM2 antibodies
- Often causes more severe sensorimotor involvement and cranial nerve deficits
- Younger patient population

#### 2.4.3 Other Established Infections

- **Epstein-Barr virus (EBV)**: ~1% of GBS cases (IGOS-1000)
- **Mycoplasma pneumoniae**: ~10% of GBS cases (IGOS-1000); associated with anti-galactocerebroside antibodies, more frequent in children (Meyer Sauteur et al., 2016)
- **Hepatitis E virus (HEV)**: ~3% (IGOS-1000)
- **Haemophilus influenzae**: Associated with anti-GQ1b and MFS
- **Influenza A virus**

#### 2.4.4 Zika Virus

- First associated with GBS during the French Polynesian outbreak (2013-2014): 42 GBS cases vs. expected 3-8/year
- During the 2015-2016 epidemic in the Americas, significant increases in GBS incidence were documented in multiple countries
- Predominantly associated with AMAN subtype

(Styczynski et al., 2017; Shahrizaila et al., 2021)

#### 2.4.5 SARS-CoV-2 (COVID-19)

- The relationship between COVID-19 infection and GBS risk remains debated
- Hughes (2024) concludes: "there is no, or only a very small, increase in risk of GBS after SARS-CoV-2"
- Some case series reported GBS following COVID-19 infection, but large population-based studies have not confirmed a significant increase above background rates
- Initial case reports during the pandemic may have been subject to reporting bias

### 2.5 Vaccine-Associated GBS

#### Historical Context

- **1976 Swine Flu vaccine (A/New Jersey/76)**: 4.9-5.9 GBS cases per million vaccinations -- the original vaccine-GBS association (Langmuir et al., 1984)
- **2009 H1N1 pandemic vaccine**: Lower risk at 1-1.6 cases per million doses

#### COVID-19 Vaccines

A major meta-analysis of 15 studies (1,450 GBS cases across >1 billion vaccine doses) found (Censi et al., 2024):

| Vaccine Type | GBS Cases per Million Doses | Relative Risk vs. Background |
|-------------|---------------------------|------------------------------|
| Overall (all COVID vaccines) | 1.25 (95% CI 0.21-2.83) | -- |
| Adenoviral-vectored (all) | 3.93 (95% CI 2.54-5.54) | 2.37x (95% CI 1.67-3.36) |
| Vaxzevria (AstraZeneca) | 2.23 | Elevated |
| Jcovden (Janssen/J&J) | 6.63 | Significantly elevated |
| mRNA vaccines (all) | 0.69 (95% CI 0.38-1.06) | 0.32x (protective) |
| Comirnaty (Pfizer-BioNTech) | 0.64 | Not elevated |
| Spikevax (Moderna) | 0.66 | Not elevated |

- In July 2021, FDA and EMA issued warnings regarding increased GBS risk after adenovirus-vectored vaccines
- GBS incidence within 21 days after Ad26.COV2.S (Janssen): 32.4/100,000 person-years (vs. background 1-2/100,000/year) (Goud et al., 2021)
- First dose risk ~2.6x higher than second dose
- Mortality among post-vaccine GBS: ~4.6%

#### Other Vaccines

- **Recombinant zoster vaccine (RZV)**: ~3 excess cases per million vaccinations (Abara et al., 2023)
- **Seasonal influenza vaccines**: Small but measurable increase (1-2 excess cases per million)

#### Guideline Position

The 2023 EAN/PNS GBS guidelines conclude that "the advantages of vaccination (reduction in morbidity and mortality related to infection and infection-associated GBS) significantly outweigh any marginal elevation in the risk of post-vaccine GBS" (van Doorn et al., 2023).

### 2.6 Other Risk Factors

- **Surgery**: Elevated risk within first 6 weeks post-surgery, particularly orthopedic or gastrointestinal procedures; higher risk with concurrent active malignancy (Gensicke et al., 2012; Rudant et al., 2018)
- **Immune checkpoint inhibitors**: GBS reported as immune-related adverse event
- **Stem cell transplant**: Patients on tacrolimus prophylaxis for GVHD at risk (Gu et al., 2017)
- **Pregnancy**: Possible slight increase in risk during postpartum period

## 3. Diagnosis

### 3.1 Clinical Diagnostic Criteria

#### NINDS Diagnostic Criteria (Asbury & Cornblath, 1990)

**Required features**:
1. Progressive bilateral weakness of upper and lower limbs (may begin distally or proximally)
2. Absent or decreased deep tendon reflexes at some point during illness

**Supportive features**:
- Progressive phase: days to 4 weeks (usually <2 weeks)
- Relative symmetry of symptoms
- Mild sensory involvement relative to motor
- Cranial nerve involvement (especially bilateral facial palsy)
- Recovery beginning 2-4 weeks after progression ceases
- Autonomic dysfunction
- Absence of fever at onset

#### 3.1.1 Brighton Collaboration Criteria

The Brighton criteria provide graded levels of diagnostic certainty, widely used in vaccine safety surveillance and clinical research (Sejvar et al., 2011):

**Level 1 (Highest certainty)**: Requires ALL of:
- Bilateral and flaccid weakness of limbs
- Decreased or absent deep tendon reflexes in weak limbs
- Monophasic illness pattern with interval between onset and nadir of 12 hours to 28 days, followed by clinical plateau or improvement
- Absence of identified alternative diagnosis
- Cytoalbuminologic dissociation (elevated CSF protein with cell count <50/microL)
- Electrodiagnostic findings consistent with GBS

**Level 2**: Same clinical features as Level 1, plus EITHER CSF findings OR electrodiagnostic findings (not both required)

**Level 3**: Clinical features alone (bilateral flaccid weakness + decreased/absent reflexes + monophasic course + no alternative diagnosis)

**Level 4**: Reported as GBS by a physician but insufficient information for classification into Levels 1-3

(Sejvar et al., 2011; Fokke et al., 2014)

### 3.2 Electrodiagnostic Criteria

Nerve conduction studies (NCS) and electromyography (EMG) are essential for:
- Supporting GBS diagnosis
- Classifying electrophysiological subtype
- Excluding mimics
- Providing prognostic information

**Optimal timing**: Repeat electrodiagnostic studies 2-3 weeks after symptom onset if initial studies are normal or equivocal. More than 85% of patients show consistent electrodiagnostic signs of GBS after the second week (Leonhard et al., 2019).

#### AIDP (Demyelinating) Features

- Prolonged distal motor latencies (>115-125% of upper limit of normal [ULN])
- Reduced motor conduction velocities (<80-90% of lower limit of normal [LLN])
- Prolonged or absent F-wave latencies (>120% ULN)
- Conduction block (>50% proximal-to-distal CMAP amplitude drop)
- Temporal dispersion (prolonged distal CMAP duration >=8.5 ms)
- **Sural-sparing pattern**: Preserved sural sensory nerve action potential (SNAP) with abnormal median/ulnar SNAPs -- highly suggestive of AIDP and helps differentiate from other neuropathies (Derksen et al., 2014)

#### AMAN (Axonal) Features

- Reduced distal CMAP amplitudes (<80% LLN)
- Preserved or near-normal distal motor latencies and conduction velocities
- Preserved SNAPs
- Absent F-waves may occur without significant latency prolongation

#### AMSAN Features

- Reduced both CMAP and SNAP amplitudes
- Preserved or mildly abnormal conduction velocities

#### Key Electrodiagnostic Findings

- **H-reflex**: Absence is highly sensitive (95-100%) for GBS; its presence makes GBS diagnosis unlikely (Vucic et al., 2004)
- **Inexcitable nerves**: May be seen early and indicate severe damage; associated with poor prognosis
- Approximately one-third of patients remain "equivocal" or "inexcitable" by electrodiagnostic criteria

**Important guideline note**: The 2023 EAN/PNS guideline states that "the distinction between AIDP and AMAN/AMSAN does not impact on clinical management, and there is no gold standard to select among the various published criteria" (van Doorn et al., 2023).

### 3.3 CSF Findings

#### Albuminocytologic Dissociation

The hallmark CSF finding in GBS:
- **Elevated protein** (typically >0.45 g/L) with **normal white cell count** (<10 cells/microL)
- Reflects disruption of the blood-nerve barrier at proximal nerve roots, allowing proteins to exude into the subarachnoid space

#### Timing Considerations

| Timing | Protein Elevation | Clinical Implication |
|--------|-------------------|---------------------|
| Week 1 | Present in ~50% | Normal CSF does NOT exclude GBS |
| Week 2 | Present in ~80% | Sensitivity improves |
| Week 3+ | Present in ~90% | Most reliable timing |

(Bellanti & Rinaldi, 2024)

**Critical practical note**: CSF must be sampled BEFORE IVIg treatment, which itself elevates both protein and white cell count.

#### CSF Red Flags

- **Pleocytosis >10 cells/microL**: Consider alternative diagnoses
- **Marked pleocytosis >50 cells/microL**: Strongly suggests alternative pathology:
  - HIV infection
  - Lyme disease (*Borrelia burgdorferi*)
  - Leptomeningeal malignancy
  - Sarcoidosis
  - CMV polyradiculopathy

#### CSF in MFS

- Albuminocytologic dissociation present in ~47% of MFS patients early; increases to 82% by week 3
- Anti-GQ1b antibody measurement in CSF may be more useful early than protein measurement

### 3.4 Anti-Ganglioside Antibody Testing

**Clinical utility is limited for diagnosis** (sensitivity 20-69% for anti-GM1 in overall GBS population):

| Antibody | Clinical Value |
|----------|---------------|
| Anti-GQ1b IgG | Highly specific for MFS/BBE/GBS-MFS overlap (~100% specificity); supports diagnosis |
| Anti-GM1 IgG | Supports AMAN diagnosis; prognostic value (poor recovery when persistently elevated) |
| Anti-GD1a IgG | Associated with poor outcomes, facial weakness, axonal damage |
| Anti-GT1a | Suggests PCB variant with bulbar involvement |
| Anti-GM2 | May suggest CMV etiology |

**Key points** (van Doorn et al., 2023; Bellanti & Rinaldi, 2024):
- Do NOT wait for antibody results before starting treatment
- Negative antibodies do NOT exclude GBS
- Absence does not impact clinical management

### 3.5 Neuroimaging

- **MRI**: Not routine but helpful for excluding differential diagnoses. Gadolinium-enhanced MRI showing nerve root enhancement is a nonspecific but sensitive feature of GBS (Leonhard et al., 2019)
- **Nerve ultrasound**: Emerging tool showing enlarged cervical nerve roots early in disease course

### 3.6 Differential Diagnosis

#### Peripheral Nerve Disorders
| Condition | Distinguishing Features |
|-----------|------------------------|
| **Chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy (CIDP) / Acute-onset CIDP** | Slow onset (>2 weeks), progressive symptoms, responds to corticosteroids; >=3 treatment-related fluctuations or deterioration >=8 weeks suggests CIDP |
| **Autoimmune nodopathy** | Multiple treatment-related fluctuations, severe features, poor response to standard GBS therapy; may benefit from rituximab |
| **Vasculitic neuropathy** | Asymmetrical, multifocal pattern (mononeuritis multiplex) |
| **Lyme disease** | CSF pleocytosis, erythema migrans history, *Borrelia* serology |
| **HIV polyradiculopathy** | CSF pleocytosis, immunosuppressed patient |
| **Diphtheria** | Oropharyngeal membrane, palatal palsy |
| **Heavy metal poisoning** | Thallium, lead, arsenic, mercury; chronic exposure history |
| **Vitamin B1 deficiency** | No albuminocytologic dissociation |
| **Acute intermittent porphyria** | Axonal on electrophysiology, abdominal pain, urine porphyrins, no albuminocytologic dissociation |

#### Neuromuscular Junction Disorders
| Condition | Distinguishing Features |
|-----------|------------------------|
| **Myasthenia gravis** | Fatigable weakness, diurnal fluctuations, preserved reflexes |
| **Lambert-Eaton myasthenic syndrome** | Weakness improves with exercise, incremental response on repetitive nerve stimulation |
| **Botulism** | Descending paralysis, mydriasis, foodborne/wound exposure |
| **Organophosphate intoxication** | Cholinergic syndrome (diarrhea, lacrimation, miosis, bradycardia) |

#### Myelopathies
| Condition | Distinguishing Features |
|-----------|------------------------|
| **Acute transverse myelitis** | Sensory level (absent in GBS), bladder dysfunction early |
| **Spinal cord compression** | Upper motor neuron signs, imaging abnormalities |
| **MOGAD** | May present with conus syndrome mimicking GBS; prominent sphincter disturbance at onset |

#### Myopathies and Other
| Condition | Distinguishing Features |
|-----------|------------------------|
| **Hypokalemic periodic paralysis** | No sensory deficits, no sphincter/bulbar involvement, low potassium |
| **Viral myositis / rhabdomyolysis** | Elevated CK, fever, myalgia |
| **Critical illness polyneuropathy/myopathy** | ICU setting, sepsis, multi-organ failure |
| **Acute flaccid myelitis** | Anterior horn cell involvement; enterovirus, poliovirus, West Nile virus |
| **Brainstem stroke** | Hypertonia, hyperreflexia (unlike GBS) |

(Bellanti & Rinaldi, 2024; Leonhard et al., 2019)

## 4. Standard Treatment

### 4.1 General Principles

- Treatment should be initiated as early as possible in patients with significant disability
- **Treatment threshold**: Patients unable to walk independently (GBS disability scale >=3)
- **Consider treatment in ambulatory patients** with rapidly progressive weakness, respiratory insufficiency, bulbar dysfunction, or severe autonomic dysfunction
- **IVIg and PE are equally effective** -- choice depends on availability, logistics, and patient factors
- **Corticosteroids are NOT effective** and should not be used alone for GBS
- **Combined PE followed by IVIg provides no additional benefit** over either alone

(van Doorn et al., 2023; Hughes et al., 2014; Chevret et al., 2017)

### 4.2 Intravenous Immunoglobulin (IVIg)

#### Dosing Protocol

- **Standard regimen**: 0.4 g/kg body weight per day for 5 consecutive days (total dose: 2 g/kg)
- Administered within 2 weeks of weakness onset (evidence-based timeframe; likely effective up to 4 weeks)
- 5-day regimen preferred over 2-day regimen (lower relapse risk)

#### Mechanism of Action

IVIg exerts immunomodulatory effects through multiple mechanisms:
- Fc receptor blockade on macrophages and other immune cells
- Neutralization of pathogenic autoantibodies via anti-idiotypic antibodies
- Inhibition of complement activation and MAC formation
- Modulation of cytokine production
- Interference with Fc receptor-mediated phagocytosis
- Enhancement of IgG catabolism via FcRn saturation

#### Evidence Base

**Cochrane Systematic Review** (Hughes et al., 2014):
- **12 RCTs** included
- **7 trials** compared IVIg vs. PE (623 severely affected participants)
- **3 trials** compared IVIg vs. supportive care (75 children)

**Key efficacy data (IVIg vs. PE)**:
- Mean disability grade improvement: IVIg 0.8 (SD 1.3) vs. PE 0.9 (SD 1.3)
- Mean difference: 0.02 grade more improvement with IVIg (95% CI: -0.20 to 0.25) -- **no significant difference**
- Participants improving >=1 grade: RR 1.08 (95% CI 0.94-1.23)
- Dead or disabled at 12 months: 163/1000 (IVIg) vs. 167/1000 (PE)
- Mortality: RR 0.78 (95% CI 0.31-1.95), slightly favoring IVIg (not significant)

**Time to recovery of walking**:
- One trial: Median 51 days (IVIg) vs. 49 days (PE)
- Another trial: Median 55 days (IVIg) vs. 69 days (PE), P=0.07

**Treatment completion (major practical advantage for IVIg)**:
- IVIg is significantly more likely to be completed than PE: RR 0.14 (95% CI 0.05-0.36) for treatment discontinuation
- Evidence quality: HIGH (GRADE)

**Evidence quality** (GRADE):
- IVIg vs. PE (disability grade change): MODERATE
- Adverse events: MODERATE
- Dead/disabled at 12 months: LOW

#### Adverse Effects

- Headache, chills, fever (infusion-related)
- Thromboembolic events (DVT, PE, stroke, MI)
- Aseptic meningitis
- Renal impairment (especially sucrose-containing preparations)
- Hemolytic anemia
- Anaphylaxis (rare; higher risk in IgA-deficient patients)

#### Special Considerations

- **Second IVIg course**: Not recommended routinely. A second dose in severe GBS increases thromboembolic/vascular complications without demonstrated benefit (Bellanti & Rinaldi, 2024)
- **Pharmacokinetic variability**: Variable IVIg pharmacokinetics are associated with poor recovery in some patients; this is an area of active research

### 4.3 Plasma Exchange (Plasmapheresis)

#### Protocol

- **Volume exchanged**: 200-250 mL plasma/kg body weight total (or ~40-55 mL/kg per session)
- **Sessions**: Typically 3 liters per session
- **Schedule by severity**:
  - Mild GBS (ambulatory): 2 sessions within first 2 weeks
  - Moderate GBS (unable to walk): 4 sessions over 1-2 weeks
  - Severe GBS (ventilator-dependent): 4-5 sessions over 1-2 weeks (6 sessions showed no additional benefit over 4)
- **Replacement fluid**: Albumin with colloids/crystalloids preferred over fresh frozen plasma (fewer adverse events, equivalent efficacy)
- Effective when initiated within 4 weeks of weakness onset (broader window than IVIg evidence)

#### Evidence Base

**Cochrane Systematic Review** (Chevret et al., 2017):
- **6 RCTs** comparing PE vs. supportive care (649 participants)

**Key efficacy data at 4 weeks** (PE vs. supportive care):

| Outcome | Risk Ratio (95% CI) |
|---------|-------------------|
| Walking with aid | 1.60 (1.19-2.15) |
| Improvement >=1 disability grade | 1.64 (1.37-1.96) |
| Ventilator dependency (reduced) | 0.53 (0.39-0.74) |
| Walking without aid | 1.72 (1.06-2.79) |

**Time to recovery of walking with aid**: Median 30 days (PE) vs. 44 days (supportive care) -- **14-day acceleration**

**One-year outcomes** (PE vs. supportive care):

| Outcome | Risk Ratio (95% CI) |
|---------|-------------------|
| Full muscle strength recovery | 1.24 (1.07-1.45) |
| Severe motor sequelae (reduced) | 0.65 (0.44-0.96) |
| Deaths | 0.86 (0.45-1.65) -- NS |

**Evidence quality**: MODERATE (all trials lacked blinding; sham PE considered unethical)

**Relapses**: PE-treated patients had higher relapse rates: RR 2.89 (95% CI 1.05-7.93) in first 6-12 months -- a notable finding suggesting some patients may need retreatment

**Timing**: Greater efficacy demonstrated when initiated within 7 days; still beneficial at 7-30 days

#### Adverse Effects

- Hypotension and hemodynamic instability
- Catheter-related infections/thrombosis
- Citrate-related hypocalcemia
- Coagulopathy
- Allergic reactions (especially to FFP)
- Pneumonia, atelectasis

#### PE vs. IVIg Practical Comparison

| Feature | IVIg | PE |
|---------|------|-----|
| Efficacy | Equivalent | Equivalent |
| Completion rate | Higher | Lower (more discontinuations) |
| Availability | Wider | Requires specialized equipment/expertise |
| Venous access | Peripheral IV possible | Central venous catheter usually needed |
| ICU requirement | No | Often needed |
| Anticoagulation | Not needed | Often needed |
| Pregnancy safety | Preferred (less monitoring) | Not contraindicated |
| Pediatric preference | First-line (better tolerated) | Second-line |

### 4.4 Corticosteroids

- **NOT recommended** for GBS treatment
- Eight RCTs showed no significant benefit (Leonhard et al., 2019)
- Oral corticosteroids may delay recovery or cause adverse effects
- IV methylprednisolone combined with IVIg provides no additional benefit
- **Exception**: Immune checkpoint inhibitor-related GBS may warrant concurrent corticosteroids (IVIg 0.4 g/kg/day x 5 days + methylprednisolone 1 g/day x 5 days, then taper)

### 4.5 Supportive Care and ICU Management

#### ICU Admission Criteria

Indications for ICU admission (Leonhard et al., 2019):
- Evolving respiratory distress with imminent insufficiency
- Severe autonomic cardiovascular dysfunction (arrhythmias, blood pressure instability)
- Severe swallowing/cough dysfunction
- Rapid weakness progression

Approximately one-third of GBS patients require mechanical ventilation.

#### Respiratory Monitoring and Management

**Assessment tools**:
- Accessory muscle use
- Single breath count (<=19 predicts ventilation need)
- Vital capacity (VC) -- serial measurements essential
- Maximum inspiratory pressure (MIP) and maximum expiratory pressure (MEP)

**20/30/40 Rule** -- intubation indicated when:
- Vital capacity <20 mL/kg
- Maximum inspiratory pressure <30 cmH2O
- Maximum expiratory pressure <40 cmH2O

**EGRIS (Erasmus GBS Respiratory Insufficiency Score)** (Walgaard et al., 2010):

| Variable | Score |
|----------|-------|
| Days from weakness onset to admission: <=3 days | 2 |
| Days from weakness onset to admission: 4-7 days | 1 |
| Days from weakness onset to admission: >7 days | 0 |
| Facial/bulbar weakness at admission: present | 1 |
| Facial/bulbar weakness at admission: absent | 0 |
| MRC sum score at admission (graded 0-4) | 0-4 |

**Risk stratification**:
- EGRIS 0-2: 4% mechanical ventilation risk
- EGRIS 3-4: 24% risk
- EGRIS >=5: 65% risk

**Tracheostomy timing**: Half of intubated GBS patients ultimately require tracheostomy. Delay beyond 14 days associated with higher ventilator-associated pneumonia rates, but earlier intervention results in some unnecessary tracheostomies. Most experts recommend considering at 10-14 days post-intubation based on individual trajectory.

**Prolonged ventilation predictors**: Inability to lift arms from bed at 1 week post-intubation; axonal subtype or inexcitable nerves.

#### Autonomic Dysfunction Management

Autonomic dysfunction occurs in approximately 65% of GBS patients:

| Manifestation | Management |
|--------------|------------|
| Sinus tachycardia | Often does not require specific treatment |
| Episodic bradycardia / sinus arrest | Cardiac monitoring; temporary pacemaker in severe cases |
| Episodic hypertension | Short-acting antihypertensives; avoid beta-blockers (may worsen bradycardia) |
| Orthostatic hypotension | Volume expansion, gradual position changes |
| Ileus / gastroparesis | Prokinetic agents, nasogastric decompression |
| Urinary retention | Intermittent catheterization |
| Sweating abnormalities | Supportive care |

**Warning**: Vagotonic stimulation (tracheal suctioning) can trigger life-threatening bradycardia or asystole.

#### Pain Management

Pain is a major and often underrecognized symptom in GBS:
- Present in >=66% of patients during the acute phase
- Can precede weakness in up to 36% of patients
- Persists in >=33% at 1 year; can continue >10 years
- Types: Muscle pain (lower back/limbs), painful paresthesias, arthralgia, radicular pain, neuropathic pain

**Treatment approaches**:
- Gabapentin or pregabalin (first-line for neuropathic pain)
- Carbamazepine
- Tricyclic antidepressants (amitriptyline, nortriptyline)
- Opioids for severe acute pain (short-term)
- NSAIDs for musculoskeletal pain
- Early mobilization

(Ruts et al., 2010)

#### Other ICU Complications

| Complication | Prevention/Management |
|-------------|----------------------|
| DVT/PE | Pharmacologic thromboprophylaxis; compression stockings |
| Hospital-acquired infections | Standard infection control; minimize invasive lines |
| Pressure ulcers | Regular repositioning; pressure-relieving surfaces |
| Corneal ulceration | Eye lubrication; taping if facial palsy |
| Nutritional deficiency | Early enteral nutrition; speech therapy assessment for dysphagia |
| Psychological distress | Patient communication support; anxiolytic/antidepressant therapy; explain that patients often have intact consciousness |
| Delirium | Non-pharmacologic measures first; low-dose antipsychotics if needed |
| Contractures | Early range-of-motion exercises |

### 4.6 Rehabilitation

Rehabilitation is a crucial component of GBS management and should begin early during the hospital course.

#### Multidisciplinary Team

- Rehabilitation physician
- Physiotherapist
- Occupational therapist
- Speech-language pathologist
- Dietitian/nutritionist
- Psychologist/psychiatrist
- Social worker

#### Exercise Programs

- **Range-of-motion exercises**: Begin during acute phase to prevent contractures
- **Progressive strengthening**: Graduated resistance training as recovery permits
- **Stationary cycling**: Improves cardiovascular fitness and functional capacity
- **Walking training**: Gait rehabilitation with appropriate assistive devices
- **Aerobic conditioning**: Stationary cycling, swimming, or walking programs

**Important caution**: Exercise intensity must be closely monitored as overwork can cause fatigue and potentially worsen weakness (overwork weakness) (Leonhard et al., 2019).

#### Fatigue Management

- Affects 60-80% of GBS patients
- Graded, supervised exercise programs have been shown to reduce fatigue
- Energy conservation techniques
- Structured rest periods

#### Psychological Support

- Early recognition and management of psychological distress is essential
- Patients often experience trauma from rapid physical loss with preserved consciousness
- Providing accurate information on generally good recovery prognosis (80% walk independently) and low recurrence risk (2-5%) can reduce anxiety
- Patient support networks (e.g., GBS/CIDP Foundation International) are beneficial
- Mental health status influences physical recovery trajectory

#### Long-Term Rehabilitation

- Recovery may continue for >5 years post-onset
- Outpatient rehabilitation programs should continue as long as functional gains are being made
- Return-to-work support and vocational rehabilitation

### 4.7 Treatment of Specific Variants

#### Miller Fisher Syndrome
- Mild, self-limiting disease course in most patients
- Treatment generally NOT recommended for uncomplicated MFS
- Close monitoring required due to risk of developing limb weakness, bulbar palsy, or respiratory failure (~15% progress to GBS overlap)
- If overlap features develop, treat as per standard GBS protocols

#### Bickerstaff Brainstem Encephalitis
- Treatment with IVIg or PE justified due to severity, though evidence is limited
- ICU monitoring often required

#### Pediatric GBS
- IVIg is first-line (greater comfort, fewer complications than PE)
- Standard 5-day IVIg regimen preferred; 2-day regimens associated with higher treatment-related fluctuation rates
- No indication to deviate from standard adult dosing (2 g/kg total)

#### GBS in Pregnancy
- Neither IVIg nor PE is contraindicated
- IVIg preferred (requires less additional monitoring than PE)

## 5. Prognosis

### 5.1 General Recovery Patterns

- Patients typically reach nadir (maximum disability) within 10-14 days
- Symptoms rarely progress beyond 4 weeks (if progression >4 weeks, consider CIDP)
- Recovery begins 2-4 weeks after plateau
- Most recovery occurs in the first year
- **Recovery may continue for >=5 years** post-onset

**Recovery milestones**:

| Time Point | Complete Recovery or Minor Limitations |
|-----------|---------------------------------------|
| 1 month | 41% |
| 3 months | 71% |
| 6 months | 86% |
| 12 months | 92% |

- About 80% of patients walk independently at 6 months (Bellanti & Rinaldi, 2024)
- >50% return to previous baseline within 1 year
- 77% ambulate independently at 6 months; 81% at 12 months post-immunotherapy

### 5.2 GBS Disability Scale (Hughes Functional Grading Scale)

The most widely used outcome measure in GBS clinical trials and practice:

| Grade | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| 0 | Healthy |
| 1 | Minor symptoms and capable of running |
| 2 | Able to walk >=10 meters without assistance but unable to run |
| 3 | Able to walk >=10 meters across an open space with help (one/two sticks, frame, or one person -- not two persons) |
| 4 | Bedridden or chair-bound |
| 5 | Requires invasive mechanical ventilation for at least part of the 24 hours |
| 6 | Death |

**Notes on scoring**:
- Grade 2-3: "assistance" includes sticks, canes, waist-level frame, or help from one person
- Grade 5: "assisted ventilation" should be interpreted as "invasive ventilation" (not non-invasive mask ventilation)
- The primary outcome in most RCTs is improvement by >=1 grade on this scale at 4 weeks

(Hughes et al., 1978; GBS Disability Scale, PMC12573220)

### 5.3 Other Outcome Measures

| Scale | Description | Application |
|-------|-------------|-------------|
| **MRC Sum Score** | Measures strength of 6 bilateral muscle groups (0-60) | Disease activity monitoring |
| **I-RODS** | Inflammatory Rasch-built Overall Disability Scale | Activity limitations; used in recent trials |
| **INCAT Disability Scale** | Upper/lower limb disability assessment | Immune-mediated neuropathies |
| **ONLS** | Overall Neuropathy Limitation Scale | Functional outcome |

### 5.4 Prognostic Scoring Systems

#### Modified Erasmus GBS Outcome Score (mEGOS)

Predicts inability to walk independently (GBS disability >=3) at 4 weeks, 3 months, and 6 months (Walgaard et al., 2011):

**At hospital admission (Score 0-9)**:

| Variable | Categories | Points |
|----------|-----------|--------|
| Age (years) | <=40 | 0 |
| | 41-60 | 1 |
| | >60 | 2 |
| Preceding diarrhea | No | 0 |
| | Yes | 1 |
| MRC sum score | 51-60 | 0 |
| | 41-50 | 3 |
| | 31-40 | 6 |
| | <=30 | 6 |

**At day 7 of admission (Score 0-12)**:

| Variable | Categories | Points |
|----------|-----------|--------|
| Age (years) | <=40 | 0 |
| | 41-60 | 1 |
| | >60 | 2 |
| Preceding diarrhea | No | 0 |
| | Yes | 1 |
| MRC sum score day 7 | 51-60 | 0 |
| | 41-50 | 3 |
| | 31-40 | 6 |
| | <=30 | 9 |

**Discriminative ability (AUC)**:
- At admission: 0.73-0.77
- At day 7: 0.84-0.87 (superior predictive ability)

**International validation (IGOS cohort)**: mEGOS validated in 809 patients from 115 centers across 19 countries. AUC >0.7 in all regional subgroups. Region-specific recalibration (mEGOS-Eu/NA) improves accuracy for European/North American populations (Doets et al., 2022).

#### EGRIS (Erasmus GBS Respiratory Insufficiency Score)

Predicts mechanical ventilation need (see Section 4.5).

Internationally validated in IGOS cohort (Walgaard et al., 2022).

### 5.5 Prognostic Factors

#### Factors Associated with Poor Outcome

| Factor | Mechanism/Explanation |
|--------|----------------------|
| **Advanced age (>60 years)** | Reduced regenerative capacity; higher comorbidity burden |
| **Rapid onset** (weakness within <=7 days) | Suggests aggressive immune attack |
| **Severe weakness at admission** (low MRC sum score) | Indicates extensive nerve damage |
| **Preceding diarrhea** | Indicates *C. jejuni* infection, associated with axonal subtypes and severe damage |
| **Mechanical ventilation requirement** | Marker of disease severity |
| **Axonal electrophysiology** | Direct nerve fiber damage harder to repair than demyelination |
| **Inexcitable nerves on NCS** | Severe axonal destruction |
| **Anti-GM1/GD1a antibodies** | Associated with axonal damage and poor recovery |
| **Persistently elevated anti-GM1 IgG** | Ongoing immune attack |
| **Low serum IgG increase after IVIg** | Poor IVIg bioavailability |

#### Factors Associated with Better Outcome

- Younger age
- Demyelinating (AIDP) subtype
- Preceding upper respiratory infection (vs. diarrhea)
- Early treatment initiation
- Preserved CMAP amplitudes

### 5.6 Mortality

- **Overall mortality**: 3-7% (up to 10% in some series)
- **Ventilated patients**: ~20% mortality (older estimates; Thille 2025, n=513 GBS across 47 ICUs, found 9.4% ICU mortality — likely reflecting improved modern ICU care)
- **Primary causes of death**: Sepsis, acute respiratory distress syndrome, pulmonary embolism, cardiac arrest (arrhythmias from autonomic dysfunction)
- **Important**: Up to two-thirds of deaths occur during the recovery phase, not the acute phase (Leonhard et al., 2019)

### 5.7 Long-Term Outcomes and Residual Deficits

Despite overall favorable recovery:
- **Fatigue**: 60-80% of patients; often most disabling residual symptom
- **Neuropathic pain**: >=33% at 1 year; can persist >10 years
- **Residual weakness**: Distal weakness and reduced reflexes common
- **Sensory disturbances**: Paresthesias, numbness may persist
- **Psychological sequelae**: Anxiety, depression, PTSD
- **Reduced quality of life**: Even patients who appear functionally recovered may have significant residual complaints

At 1-14 years follow-up, 73% of patients reported being completely symptom-free, but 27% had persistent symptoms (Bernsen et al., 1997).

### 5.8 Recurrence

- 2-5% experience recurrent GBS episodes (vs. 0.1% lifetime risk in general population)
- Recurrence is more common in younger patients
- Prior GBS is not a strict contraindication to vaccination
- Risk-benefit discussion advisable for vaccinations <1 year post-diagnosis or if GBS occurred shortly after prior vaccination

### 5.9 Treatment-Related Fluctuations (TRF)

- Occur in 6-10% of patients
- Defined as: disease worsening within 2 months after initial treatment-induced improvement/stabilization
- Management: Repeating full IVIg or PE course is common practice, though evidence is lacking
- **Important distinction**: >=3 TRFs and/or deterioration >=8 weeks after onset suggests acute-onset CIDP rather than GBS (~5% of initial GBS diagnoses)

## 6. Treatment-Resistant/Refractory GBS

### 6.1 Definition and Scope

Approximately 40% of GBS patients show no improvement in the first 4 weeks after treatment. "Treatment-resistant" or "refractory" GBS refers to cases not responding to standard first-line therapies (IVIg or PE). Currently, no high-quality evidence exists to guide management of these patients (van Doorn et al., 2023).

### 6.2 Second-Course Therapy

- **Repeat IVIg**: Commonly used in clinical practice, but evidence is limited. The SID-GBS trial (Walgaard et al., *Lancet Neurol* 2021, PMID: 33743237, n=93, RCT) definitively showed no benefit from a second IVIg course (OR 1.4, 95% CI 0.6-3.3, p=0.45) with significantly more serious adverse events (35% vs 16%, including thromboembolic events and 4 deaths in the intervention group). EAN/PNS 2023 recommends against routine second IVIg
- **Switch from IVIg to PE (or vice versa)**: Practiced clinically, but no RCT evidence supports this approach
- **Combined PE then IVIg**: Not more effective than either treatment alone (Plasma Exchange/Sandoglobulin GBS Trial Group, 1997)

### 6.3 Complement Inhibitors

#### Eculizumab (Anti-C5 Monoclonal Antibody)

**Rationale**: Complement activation and MAC formation are central to axonal GBS pathophysiology, particularly in AMAN.

**Phase 2 Trial** (Japan, 2015-2016; Misawa et al., 2018):
- Design: Randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled
- N = 34 patients unable to walk, receiving IVIg + eculizumab vs. IVIg + placebo
- Primary endpoint: Proportion able to walk independently at week 4
- Result: Primary endpoint not met; some secondary outcomes favored eculizumab
- Citation: Misawa S, et al. *Lancet Neurol*. 2018;17(6):519-529. DOI: 10.1016/S1474-4422(18)30114-5

**Phase 3 Trial** (Japan, 2021-2022; Kuwabara et al., 2024):
- N = 57 patients (eculizumab n=37, placebo n=20)
- Primary endpoint: Time to achieve ability to run
- Result: No clinical benefit despite reducing serum C5 concentration (HR 0.9; 95% CI 0.45-1.97; p=0.89)
- Citation: Kuwabara S, et al. *J Peripher Nerv Syst*. 2024;29(3):339-349. DOI: 10.1111/jns.12646

**Current status**: Not recommended by EAN/PNS guideline. Larger, adequately powered trials in different populations would be needed.

#### Tanruprubart / ANX005 (Anti-C1q Monoclonal Antibody)

**Phase 3 RCT** (Bangladesh/Philippines, 2021-2024):
- Design: Randomized, placebo-controlled, 3-arm trial
- N = 241 patients unable to walk independently, within 10 days of symptom onset
- Population: Settings without access to standard IVIg/PE
- Arms: Tanruprubart 30 mg/kg, tanruprubart 75 mg/kg, or placebo
- Primary endpoint: GBS Disability Scale at week 8
- **Key result**: Patients receiving 30 mg/kg demonstrated significantly better neurological outcomes at week 8 compared to placebo and the 75 mg/kg dose
- **Significance**: This is the **first positive trial of complement-targeted therapy in GBS**
- Preliminary NfL reduction and early GBS disability score improvement also observed
- Results presented at Neurology Congress 2025

### 6.4 Rituximab (Anti-CD20 Monoclonal Antibody)

**Mechanism**: Depletes CD20+ B cells, reducing pathogenic antibody production.

**Evidence**: Limited to case reports and small series:
- Refractory GBS in a patient with multiple myeloma successfully treated with low-dose rituximab (100 mg/m^2/week x 4 doses) after failure of PE and IVIg (PMC10969645, 2024)
- Rituximab-responsive GBS following allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplant (PMC4445357)
- Resolution of GBS in a patient with dual primary tumors after rituximab (PMC10915274, 2024)

**Specific indication**: Panneurofascin antibody-positive GBS -- a distinct subgroup that responds poorly to IVIg/PE but shows improvement with rituximab (Hughes, 2024)

**Current status**: Not established as standard therapy; may be considered in specific refractory cases, particularly those with identifiable antibody targets.

### 6.5 Cyclophosphamide

- Case reports and small series suggest possible benefit in refractory GBS
- Mechanism: Broad immunosuppression targeting both B and T cells
- Significant toxicity profile limits routine use
- No RCT data available
- Generally reserved as last-resort option

### 6.6 Immunoabsorption (IA)

- Selective removal of IgG via plasma absorption columns (tryptophan or protein A columns)
- More targeted than PE (removes IgG specifically rather than all plasma components)
- Case reports suggest safety and efficacy
- Currently not recommended due to cost, limited availability, and insufficient evidence
- Cochrane review found insufficient evidence to recommend (Lehmann et al., 2006)

### 6.7 Interferon-beta

- Small trials suggested possible benefit when combined with IVIg
- Cochrane review found insufficient evidence to recommend (Hughes et al., 2013)
- Not in current clinical practice for GBS

## 7. Recent Advances (2020-2025)

### 7.1 2023 EAN/PNS Guideline

The first GRADE-based international guideline on GBS diagnosis and treatment was published by the European Academy of Neurology and Peripheral Nerve Society (van Doorn et al., 2023):
- 14 PICO questions covering diagnosis, treatment, and prognosis
- Confirms IVIg and PE as only proven effective therapies
- Does not endorse rigid AIDP/AMAN classification for clinical management decisions
- Provides guidance on monitoring, prognosis prediction, and variant management

Citation: van Doorn PA, et al. European Academy of Neurology/Peripheral Nerve Society Guideline on diagnosis and treatment of Guillain-Barre syndrome. *Eur J Neurol*. 2023;30:3646-3674. DOI: 10.1111/ene.16073

### 7.2 Discovery of T-Cell Autoimmunity in AIDP

A landmark 2024 study in *Nature* identified autoreactive CD4+ T cells targeting myelin proteins (P0, P2, PMP22) in AIDP patients (Sukenikova et al., 2024):
- Found in blood, CSF, and nerve biopsies of 12/15 AIDP patients
- Not found in AMAN patients, suggesting distinct pathogenic mechanisms
- Cells display Th1-like cytotoxic phenotype with autoimmunity-associated genes
- May open new therapeutic targets (anti-T-cell agents)
- Challenges the traditional view that GBS is primarily antibody-mediated

Citation: Sukenikova L, et al. *Nature*. 2024;626:160-168. DOI: 10.1038/s41586-023-06916-6

### 7.3 Novel Therapeutic Agents in Clinical Trials

#### Imlifidase (IgG-Cleaving Enzyme)

**Mechanism**: Enzyme derived from *Streptococcus pyogenes* that cleaves and inactivates all four human IgG subclasses, blocking complement activation.

**Phase 2 Trial** (France/Netherlands/UK, 2019-2024):
- Design: Single-arm; imlifidase 0.25 mg/kg on day 1, followed by IVIg >=48 hours later
- N = 30 patients unable to walk, within 10 days of onset
- Results:
  - Median time to one-grade improvement: 6 days
  - Median time to two-grade improvement: 16 days
  - Ability to walk: 67% at 8 weeks; 85% at 6 months
  - Well tolerated with no serious adverse events

**Preclinical data**: Reduces axonal degeneration in rabbit GBS models with axonal injury; facilitates recovery.

#### Efgartigimod (FcRn Inhibitor)

**Mechanism**: Human IgG1 Fc fragment targeting the neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn), enhancing IgG degradation and reducing circulating pathogenic IgG levels.

**Case reports** (China, 2024): Two patients with severe GBS exhibited rapid clinical improvement and favorable outcomes after efgartigimod treatment.

**Phase 2 RCT** (USA, 2023-ongoing):
- Design: Randomized; efgartigimod vs. IVIg
- Target N = 30
- Primary endpoint: GBS-DS at week 4
- Status: Ongoing recruitment

**Supporting evidence**: Phase 2 CIDP trial demonstrated efficacy in reducing relapse risk, supporting the FcRn inhibition concept in immune-mediated neuropathies.

#### Tanruprubart (ANX005) -- See Section 6.3

First positive Phase 3 complement inhibition trial in GBS.

### 7.4 Emerging Biomarkers

#### Neurofilament Light Chain (NfL)

NfL has emerged as the most promising fluid biomarker in GBS:

- **2024 EBioMedicine study** (Longitudinal dynamics): Serum NfL levels measured before treatment and at 1, 2, 4, and 12 weeks using Simoa HD-X Analyzer showed NfL correlates with disease severity, axonal involvement, and poor outcome (Bos et al., 2024)
- **Prognostic value**: High baseline serum NfL associated with inability to run and lower I-RODS scores at 1 year; returns to physiological levels by 1 year post-onset
- **Age-adjusted NfL Z-score**: Improves prognostic precision (2025 study, Neurology)
- **Limitation**: Non-specific (elevated in >80 CNS/peripheral disorders)

Citation: Bos D, et al. Dynamics and prognostic value of serum neurofilament light chain in Guillain-Barre syndrome. *eBioMedicine*. 2024;102:105072. DOI: 10.1016/j.ebiom.2024.105072

#### Peripherin

- Type III intermediate filament protein almost exclusively expressed in the peripheral nervous system
- Significantly elevated in GBS patients with electrophysiological axonal damage
- Differentiates peripheral axonal injury from central nervous system pathology (elevated in GBS but not MS/dementia)
- Discriminates acute nodal-paranodal injury from slowly progressive demyelination
- May complement NfL as a PNS-specific biomarker

#### Total Tau (T-tau)

- Plasma (but not CSF) levels elevated in GBS vs. disease-free controls
- Plasma and CSF levels do not correlate, suggesting blood origin from distal peripheral nerve
- Prognostic and diagnostic utility remains undetermined

### 7.5 International GBS Outcome Study (IGOS)

IGOS is the largest prospective observational GBS cohort study:
- Started May 2012; >1,400 participants from 143 centers in 19 countries
- Key published findings:
  - **IGOS-1000 cohort**: Defined infection frequencies (*C. jejuni* 30%, *M. pneumoniae* 10%, HEV 3%, CMV 4%, EBV 1%) and showed that associations between infections and clinical phenotype differ across geographic regions (Doets et al., 2022)
  - **mEGOS international validation**: Validated in 809 patients; AUC >0.7 in all regional subgroups (Doets et al., 2022)
  - **EGRIS international validation**: Validated across the IGOS cohort (Walgaard et al., 2022)
  - **Electrodiagnostic standardization**: Highlighted significant methodology variations between 115 centers across countries and regions

IGOS data are now being used as **external control arms** for clinical trials, with propensity score matching to reduce confounding -- a novel trial design approach for rare diseases.

### 7.6 Emerging Research Paradigms

#### Novel Trial Designs
- **Bayesian adaptive platform studies**: Allow simultaneous evaluation of multiple GBS treatments under one protocol, reducing enrollment timelines
- **Ordinal outcome analysis**: Using the full GBS-DS scale rather than dichotomization (e.g., walk/not walk) preserves statistical information
- **Longitudinal modeling**: Characterizes temporal treatment response variations

#### Therapies in Preclinical/Early Development
- **Hypersialylated IVIg (hsIVIg)**: Enhanced anti-inflammatory potency through modified sialylation
- **Recombinant Fc multimers (rFc)**: Synthetic Fc fragments mimicking IVIg mechanism without blood product dependency
- **Anti-T-cell agents**: Under investigation following the T-cell autoimmunity discovery in AIDP

#### COVID-19 Impact on GBS Research
- The pandemic paradoxically decreased GBS incidence in many regions (likely due to reduced exposure to typical triggers through social distancing/masking)
- Provided natural experiment data on infection vs. vaccine triggers
- COVID-19 vaccine surveillance systems yielded high-quality data on vaccine-associated GBS rates

### 7.7 Small-Volume Plasma Exchange (SVPE)

- Evaluated as a low-cost alternative to standard PE and IVIg in resource-limited settings where standard treatments are unavailable
- Uses smaller exchange volumes manageable without specialized equipment
- Requires RCT validation
- Particularly relevant for high-incidence, low-resource settings (e.g., Bangladesh)
