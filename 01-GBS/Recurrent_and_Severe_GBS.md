## 1. Recurrent GBS (R-GBS)

### 1.1 Definition and Diagnostic Criteria

Recurrent Guillain-Barre syndrome (R-GBS) is defined as two or more episodes of GBS, each independently meeting the NINCDS (National Institute of Neurological and Communicative Disorders and Stroke) diagnostic criteria, separated by a defined recovery interval. The key temporal requirements are:

- **Minimum interval of 2 months** if complete remission occurs between episodes
- **Minimum interval of 4 months** if only partial remission occurs between episodes

Each episode must individually satisfy the Brighton Collaboration criteria or NINCDS criteria for GBS, including:
- Acute onset with progressive bilateral weakness
- Hyporeflexia or areflexia
- Nadir reached within 4 weeks
- Subsequent stabilization or improvement
- CSF albuminocytologic dissociation (elevated protein, normal cell count; present in approximately 80% of GBS cases)
- Supportive electrodiagnostic findings

> Kuitwaard K, van Koningsveld R, Ruts L, et al. Recurrent Guillain-Barre syndrome. *J Neurol Neurosurg Psychiatry*. 2009;80(1):56-59. doi:10.1136/jnnp.2008.156588

> Grand'Maison F, Bhatt M, Bhargava R, et al. Recurrent Guillain-Barre syndrome: clinical and laboratory features. *Brain*. 1992;115(Pt 4):1093-1106. doi:10.1093/brain/115.4.1093

### 1.2 Incidence

- GBS is primarily a **monophasic** illness; recurrence is uncommon
- Recurrence rate: **2-5%** of GBS patients (reported range 1-6% across studies) *[Level 3 — populationsbaserad studie]*
- In the largest population-based study (Kuitwaard et al., PMID: 19091706), 32 patients with recurrent GBS were identified, having a total of 81 episodes
- Das et al. reported 11 of 200 GBS patients (5.5%) experienced recurrence

> Kuitwaard K, et al. *J Neurol Neurosurg Psychiatry*. 2009;80(1):56-59.
> Das A, Kalita J, Misra UK. Recurrent Guillain-Barre syndrome. *Electromyogr Clin Neurophysiol*. 2004;44(2):95-102.

### 1.3 Time Intervals Between Episodes

The interval between recurrences is highly variable:

| Study | Range | Mean/Median |
|-------|-------|-------------|
| Kuitwaard et al. (2009) | 2 months - 37 years | Mean 7 years |
| Das et al. (2004) | 4 months - 10 years | Variable |
| Wijdicks & Ropper (1990) | 4, 10, 15, 17, 36 years | Variable |
| Imam et al. (2020) | 12 years (case report) | N/A |

Patients with R-GBS had 2-7 episodes (median 3) occurring at irregular intervals over decades. The exceptionally long intervals (up to 37 years) documented in the literature support the concept that immunological predisposition, rather than specific trigger exposure, underlies recurrence.

**Clinical implication**: A 36-year interval between episodes (e.g., first episode at age 16, recurrence at age 52) is consistent with documented R-GBS intervals *[Level 3 — populationsbaserad studie, Kuitwaard 2009]* and does not by itself argue against a diagnosis of recurrent GBS.

> Wijdicks EF, Ropper AH. Acute relapsing Guillain-Barre syndrome after long asymptomatic intervals. *Arch Neurol*. 1990;47(1):82-84. doi:10.1001/archneur.1990.00530010098024

### 1.4 Severity of Recurrent Episodes

Evidence on whether recurrent episodes are more or less severe than the index episode is mixed:

**Evidence suggesting variable severity:**
- Kuitwaard et al.: "Clinical symptoms in a first episode were similar to the following episodes in individual patients... although neurological symptoms in subsequent episodes were often similar, the severity of the symptoms and the nature of the preceding infections varied."
- Das et al.: The first and second episodes of GBS in 11 recurrent cases were "similar in course and outcome."

**Evidence suggesting episodes can be more severe:**
- Imam et al. (2020): A case with 12-year interval showed the second episode progressing to complete locked-in state (GCS 3, fixed dilated pupils) requiring prolonged ventilation, whereas the first had a more typical course
- A 2024 case report (80-year-old woman, 17-year interval): Recurrent episode was "markedly more severe" with fulminant respiratory failure within 12 hours vs. gradual onset without respiratory compromise in the first episode

**Evidence suggesting decreasing severity over multiple episodes:**
- Liu et al. (2025): A patient with three episodes showed progressively decreasing Hughes scores at nadir: 5 (first), 4 (second), 3 (third), with only the first episode requiring mechanical ventilation

**Summary**: Severity is not reliably predictable from previous episodes. Clinicians should be prepared for any severity level in recurrent episodes.

> Imam A, Carpenter CCB, Bhatt RP, et al. A case of Recurrent Guillain-Barre Syndrome observed by the same clinician 12 years apart. *Clin Case Rep*. 2020;8(10):1824-1828. doi:10.1002/ccr3.2903

> Liu G, Zhou H, He Y, et al. Case Report: Guillain-Barre syndrome with three episodes and literature review. *Front Neurol*. 2025;16:1526506. doi:10.3389/fneur.2025.1526506

### 1.5 Prognosis Compared to First-Episode GBS

- The majority of R-GBS patients **achieve full or near-full recovery** from each episode
- Wijdicks & Ropper reported five R-GBS patients (intervals 4-36 years) who all showed "total recuperation"
- Recurrent patients tend to be **younger** (mean age 34.2 years) compared to non-recurrent GBS patients (mean age 46.9 years)
- Recurrences are more frequent in patients under 30 years, those with milder initial symptoms, and those with Miller Fisher syndrome (MFS)
- Permanent disability risk: approximately 10% across recurrent episodes (comparable to single-episode GBS)

> Kuitwaard K, et al. *J Neurol Neurosurg Psychiatry*. 2009;80(1):56-59.

### 1.6 Anti-Ganglioside Antibody Patterns in R-GBS

Anti-ganglioside antibody profiles can differ substantially between recurrent episodes in the same patient:

**Case evidence (Shiino et al., 2023):**
A 21-year-old with two episodes triggered by *Campylobacter jejuni*:
- *First episode*: IgM GalNAc-GD1a; IgG GD1a, GQ1b, GM1, GD1a/GD1b, GalNAc-GD1a, Gal-C
- *Second episode*: IgG GD1a, GD3, GT1a, GT1b, GQ1b
- Only GD1a and GQ1b IgG were common to both episodes

**Case evidence (Xie et al., 2022):**
A 77-year-old woman with pharyngeal-cervical-brachial variant R-GBS:
- Had GT1a, GD1a, and sulfatide antibodies during recurrence
- Prior episode 13 years earlier had been classified as AMAN

**Key findings:**
- Different antibodies can be produced even when the same pathogen (*C. jejuni*) triggers both episodes
- Multiple (2+) types of IgG anti-ganglioside antibodies were positive in 56% of GBS patients overall
- The coexistence of several antibody subtypes in R-GBS has been described by multiple investigators
- Persistent or high anti-GM1 antibody titers are associated with poor clinical recovery

> Shiino M, Yanagida A, Uchigami H, et al. Recurrent Guillain-Barre Syndrome Associated with the Second Episode of Campylobacter jejuni Infection. *Intern Med*. 2023;62(23):3531-3536. doi:10.2169/internalmedicine.2223-23

> Xie Q, Chen W, Lin Y, et al. Recurrent Guillain-Barre syndrome presenting as pharyngeal-cervical-brachial variant with three species of ganglioside antibodies: case report. *BMC Neurol*. 2022;22(1):446. doi:10.1186/s12883-022-02955-0

> Caudie C, Bret MC, Barral FG, et al. High and Persistent Anti-GM1 Antibody Titers Are Associated With Poor Clinical Recovery in Guillain-Barre Syndrome. *Neurol Neuroimmunol Neuroinflamm*. 2022;9(5):e200107. doi:10.1212/NXI.0000000000200107

### 1.7 Does the Same Subtype Recur or Can It Change?

**Both patterns occur:**

- **Same subtype recurrence**: Kuitwaard et al. found that clinical symptoms "were either GBS or its variant Miller Fisher syndrome but never both" across episodes in the same patient. Most patients tend to recur with the same general pattern.

- **Subtype change**: Documented cases demonstrate subtype transitions:
  - AMAN to PCB (pharyngeal-cervical-brachial) variant (Xie et al., 2022)
  - Demyelinating to axonal pattern (Shiino et al., 2023: first episode showed demyelinating pattern with normal CMAPs; second showed AMAN with decreased CMAPs)
  - Liu et al. (2025): All three episodes showed "severe axonal damage with concurrent demyelinating features" -- the subtype remained consistent

**Clinical relevance**: Clinicians should not rule out R-GBS solely because the electrophysiological subtype differs from the prior episode. Phenotypic heterogeneity between episodes is well documented.

### 1.8 Risk Factors for Recurrence

A retrospective study of 320 GBS patients (302 monophasic, 18 RGBS) at Zhengzhou University (2014-2020) identified independent risk factors for recurrence through multivariate logistic regression analysis: *[Level 3 — retrospektiv analys, n=320]*

**Independent risk factors (multivariate analysis):**

| Risk Factor | Odds Ratio | 95% CI | P value |
|-------------|-----------|--------|---------|
| Antecedent illness | 5.37 | 1.32-21.90 | .019 |
| AIDP subtype | 5.37 | 1.09-26.58 | .039 |
| Elevated TSH | 3.90 | 2.07-7.34 | <.001 |

**TSH as a biomarker for recurrence risk:**
- RGBS patients had significantly higher TSH: median 4.42 vs 1.81 uIU/mL (P < .001)
- ROC analysis: TSH > 3.87 uIU/mL predicted recurrence with **88.9% sensitivity and 90.4% specificity** (AUC 0.899, Youden index 0.793)
- **FT3 was significantly lower in RGBS patients:** median 4.40 vs 4.89 pmol/L (P = .022), though FT3 was not independently significant in multivariate analysis
- Proposed mechanism: elevated TSH is associated with increased oxidative stress (elevated malondialdehyde, increased reactive oxygen species), which mediates demyelination and axonal injury via MAP kinase pathway activation in Schwann cells

**Other univariate associations with recurrence (not independent in multivariate):**
- Younger age at onset: median 46.5 vs 51.5 years (P = .044)
- Shorter time from onset to nadir: median 5 vs 7 days (P = .039)
- Lower HFGS at nadir: median 2 vs 3 (P = .041)

**Recurrence rate:** 6.0% in this cohort (18/320), consistent with previously reported rates of 2-6%.

> Wang S, Luo Z, Peng T. Serum thyroid-stimulating hormone is an independent risk factor of recurrent Guillain-Barre syndrome. *Muscle Nerve*. 2022;65(6):688-692. PMID: 35342963

**Cross-reference:** For detailed analysis of TSH, FT3, thyroid autoantibodies, and the levothyroxine-trigger hypothesis, see [Hashimoto-IgAD-GBS Autoimmune Cluster](/doc/04-related-autoimmune/Hashimoto-IgAD-GBS-Autoimmune-Cluster.md), Section 2.3.

## 2. Critical Differential: Recurrent GBS vs Acute-Onset CIDP

### 2.1 Why This Distinction Matters

This differentiation **determines treatment strategy entirely**:
- **GBS**: Acute, monophasic -- treated with a single course of IVIg (2 g/kg over 5 days) or plasma exchange (4-5 sessions over 7-14 days). No ongoing immunotherapy.
- **A-CIDP**: Chronic, relapsing -- requires **long-term immunosuppressive therapy** (chronic IVIg, corticosteroids, or other immunosuppressants)

Misdiagnosis of A-CIDP as recurrent GBS leads to undertreatment. Misdiagnosis of R-GBS as CIDP leads to unnecessary chronic immunosuppression.

> Ruts L, Drenthen J, Jacobs BC, van Doorn PA. Distinguishing acute-onset CIDP from fluctuating Guillain-Barre syndrome: a prospective study. *Neurology*. 2010;74(21):1680-1686. doi:10.1212/WNL.0b013e3181e07d14

### 2.2 Diagnostic Criteria for Distinction (Ruts Criteria, 2010)

The landmark prospective study by Ruts et al. (Neurology, 2010) established the key criteria:

**Consider reclassification to A-CIDP when:**
1. **The "8-week rule"**: Clinical deterioration occurs again **after 8 weeks from onset** of weakness
2. **The "rule of three"**: Deterioration occurs **3 or more times** (regardless of timing)
3. Patient remains able to walk independently (less severe than GBS-TRF)
4. Cranial nerve dysfunction is absent or rare
5. Electrophysiologic features are compatible with CIDP

**GBS with Treatment-Related Fluctuation (GBS-TRF):**
- First TRF always occurred within 8 weeks (median 18 days; range 10-54 days)
- Maximum of 2 fluctuations (31% had a second TRF; none had more)
- Patients were more severely affected (often requiring ventilation)
- Cranial nerve involvement was common

**A-CIDP characteristics (Ruts et al., n=8):**
- Patients were less severely affected than GBS-TRF patients
- None required artificial ventilation
- Rarely had cranial nerve dysfunction
- Had more CIDP-like electrophysiologic abnormalities
- Prominent sensory abnormalities, vibratory hypoesthesia, and sensory ataxia

| Feature | GBS-TRF | A-CIDP |
|---------|---------|--------|
| Severity | More severe | Less severe |
| Mechanical ventilation | Common | Never needed |
| Cranial nerve involvement | Common | Rare |
| Fluctuations | 1-2, within 8 weeks | 3+, may extend beyond 8 weeks |
| Sensory ataxia | Less common | Prominent |
| Electrophysiology | GBS pattern | CIDP-like features |

> Ruts L, Drenthen J, Jacobs BC, van Doorn PA. *Neurology*. 2010;74(21):1680-1686.

### 2.3 A 36-Year Interval Between Episodes: What It Suggests

A very long interval (e.g., first episode at age 16, recurrence at age 52 -- 36 years apart) **favors recurrent GBS** over A-CIDP for several reasons:

1. **Complete recovery between episodes**: A decades-long asymptomatic interval with full neurological recovery is characteristic of monophasic R-GBS, not CIDP
2. **Documented precedent**: The Kuitwaard population-based study documented intervals up to 37 years as the upper range for R-GBS
3. **A-CIDP by definition** presents as an acute monophasic-appearing episode that then evolves into chronicity or relapses within weeks to months, not decades
4. **True CIDP recurrences** typically occur at shorter intervals (months to a few years)

However, each episode must be evaluated on its own merits. If the current episode shows features more consistent with CIDP (progressive course beyond 8 weeks, prominent sensory ataxia, CIDP-like NCS), reclassification should be considered regardless of the interval.

### 2.4 Biomarkers Distinguishing GBS from CIDP

#### CSF Interleukin-8 (IL-8)

The most promising biomarker to date for acute-phase differentiation:

**Breville et al. (2021):** Retrospective immunoassay, n=75 (45 GBS, 30 CIDP)

| Parameter | GBS | CIDP | p-value |
|-----------|-----|------|---------|
| Median CSF IL-8 | 83.9 pg/mL | 41.0 pg/mL | <0.001 |
| AIDP subgroup | 101.8 pg/mL | -- | -- |
| Acute-onset CIDP | 38.9 pg/mL | -- | -- |

**Optimal cutoff: 70 pg/mL**
- Sensitivity: 64.4% (78.8% for AIDP subgroup)
- Specificity: 96.7% (100% for AIDP vs A-CIDP)
- PPV: 96.7%
- NPV: 64.4%
- AUC: 0.86 (95% CI: 0.77-0.94)

**Clinical significance**: All 6 patients initially misdiagnosed as GBS who were later confirmed as A-CIDP had CSF IL-8 < 70 pg/mL. CSF IL-8 provides Class II evidence for distinguishing GBS from CIDP.

> Breville G, Lascano AM, Roux-Lombard P, Vuilleumier N, Lalive PH. Interleukin 8, a Biomarker to Differentiate Guillain-Barre Syndrome From CIDP. *Neurol Neuroimmunol Neuroinflamm*. 2021;8(5):e1031. doi:10.1212/NXI.0000000000001031

#### Serum Neurofilament Light Chain (NfL)

NfL is a marker of axonal damage, not a GBS-vs-CIDP differentiator per se, but provides important prognostic information:

- GBS patients: median 55.49 pg/mL vs healthy controls 9.83 pg/mL
- AMAN patients had highest levels: 190.55 pg/mL vs 46.79 pg/mL for other variants
- **Prognostic cutoffs**: >319 pg/mL predicts inability to walk independently; >248 pg/mL predicts inability to run; <34 pg/mL predicts ability to run at 6 months
- NfL at 2 weeks predicted inability to walk unaided at 4 weeks: OR 1.74 (95% CI 1.27-2.45)

> van Tilburg SJ, Teunissen CE, Maas CCHM, et al. Dynamics and prognostic value of serum neurofilament light chain in Guillain-Barre syndrome. *EBioMedicine*. 2024;102:105072. doi:10.1016/j.ebiom.2024.105072

#### Anti-Neurofascin Antibodies (NF155, NF186)

**NF155 IgG4 antibodies** are relatively specific for CIDP:
- Found in CIDP patients but **not** in GBS patients or healthy controls
- Associated with: younger age of onset, sensory ataxia, tremor, DADS phenotype, high CSF protein
- **Poor response to IVIg** but responsive to rituximab
- Some NF155-positive patients present acutely, mimicking GBS -- but these are classified as autoimmune nodopathy/CIDP, not true GBS

**NF186 antibodies** are less specific and can be found in both conditions.

**Clinical utility**: If anti-NF155 IgG4 is positive in a patient presenting with acute polyneuropathy, consider A-CIDP/autoimmune nodopathy rather than GBS, even if the presentation appears acute.

> Querol L, Devaux J, Roesler K, et al. Neurofascin antibodies in autoimmune, genetic, and idiopathic neuropathies. *Neurology*. 2017;89(24):2459-2468. doi:10.1212/WNL.0000000000004773

> Ogata H, Yamasaki R, Hiwatashi A, et al. Anti-Neurofascin 155 Antibody-Positive Chronic Inflammatory Demyelinating Polyneuropathy/Combined Central and Peripheral Demyelination. *Front Neurol*. 2021;12:665136. doi:10.3389/fneur.2021.665136

#### Nerve Ultrasound

- **GBS**: Ultrasonic sensory sparing pattern (uSSP) with enlarged roots/vagus nerve -- sensitivity, specificity, and PPV >85%
- **CIDP**: Enlarged sensory nerves and multifocally enlarged sensorimotor nerves

> Grimm A, Vittore D, Gollmann-Tepekoylu U, et al. Differentiation Between Guillain-Barre Syndrome and Acute-Onset Chronic Inflammatory Demyelinating Polyradiculoneuritis -- a Prospective Follow-up Study Using Ultrasound and Neurophysiological Measurements. *Neurotherapeutics*. 2019;16(3):838-847. doi:10.1007/s13311-019-00716-5

### 2.5 Fundamental Challenge

A 2024 systematic literature review concluded: "Existing evidence questions the categorization of recurrent GBS as a distinct entity, challenging its very existence." The review notes that "distinguishing recurrent GBS from A-CIDP remains an ongoing challenge" and that electrophysiological studies, ultrasonography, and immunological markers "have been explored for discrimination; however, definitive criteria for differentiation remain elusive."

> Kamel SR, Young CA. Differentiating recurrent Guillain-Barre syndrome and acute-onset chronic inflammatory polyneuropathy: literature review. *Muscle Nerve*. 2024;70(2):199-207. doi:10.1002/mus.28113

## 3. Severe and Fulminant GBS

### 3.1 Rapidly Progressive (Fulminant) GBS

**Definition**: GBS with hyperacute onset, reaching severe disability (complete or near-complete paralysis) within 24-48 hours.

**Clinical features:**
- Rapid ascending paralysis reaching all four limbs and respiratory muscles within hours
- May mimic clinical brain death (GCS 3, absent brainstem reflexes, absent motor responses)
- Cranial nerve involvement is common (facial palsy, bulbar weakness)
- Severe autonomic dysfunction frequently accompanies fulminant course

**Risk factors for fulminant course:**
- Preceding diarrheal illness (suggesting *C. jejuni* with molecular mimicry)
- Older age (mean ~60 years in severe cohorts)
- Rapid progression from symptom onset to admission (< 3 days)
- High anti-GM1 or anti-GQ1b antibody titers
- Axonal subtypes (AMAN, AMSAN) tend to have more severe course

> Nabi S, Arif MS, Chinnam C, et al. Hyperacute and Fulminant Guillain-Barre Syndrome Requiring Emergent Intubation. *Cureus*. 2023;15(5):e39445. doi:10.7759/cureus.39445

### 3.2 The EGRIS Score (Erasmus GBS Respiratory Insufficiency Score)

The EGRIS predicts the probability of respiratory insufficiency requiring mechanical ventilation within the first week of admission.

**Components:**

| Variable | Category | Points |
|----------|----------|--------|
| Days from weakness onset to admission | >7 days | 0 |
| | 4-7 days | 1 |
| | <=3 days | 2 |
| Facial and/or bulbar weakness | Absent | 0 |
| | Present | 1 |
| MRC sum score at admission | 51-60 | 0 |
| | 41-50 | 1 |
| | 31-40 | 2 |
| | 21-30 | 3 |
| | <=20 | 4 |

**Total score: 0-7 points**

**Estimated respiratory failure risk: 1-90%** depending on score

**International validation (n=1,023 from 155 hospitals, 19 countries):**
- Full cohort AUC: 0.86 (95% CI 0.80-0.91)
- Europe/North America AUC: 0.86 (95% CI 0.80-0.93)
- Asia AUC: 0.80 (95% CI 0.62-0.91)

**Caveat**: The model systematically overestimates ventilation risk (10% observed vs 21% predicted in validation cohort).

**Modified EGRIS (mEGRIS)**: A simplified version uses presence of bulbar palsy, weakness of neck flexion and hip flexion, and time from onset to admission. AUC 0.84 (95% CI 0.80-0.88).

> Walgaard C, Lingsma HF, Ruts L, et al. Prediction of respiratory insufficiency in Guillain-Barre syndrome. *Ann Neurol*. 2010;67(6):781-787. doi:10.1002/ana.21976

> Walgaard C, Lingsma HF, Ruts L, et al. International Validation of the Erasmus Guillain-Barre Syndrome Respiratory Insufficiency Score. *Ann Neurol*. 2022;91(6):885-891. doi:10.1002/ana.26360

### 3.3 The mEGOS Score (Modified Erasmus GBS Outcome Score)

Predicts inability to walk independently at 4 weeks, 3 months, and 6 months.

**Components (at admission, 0-9 points):**

| Variable | Category | Points |
|----------|----------|--------|
| Age at onset | <=40 years | 0 |
| | 41-60 years | 1 |
| | >60 years | 2 |
| Preceding diarrhea | Absent | 0 |
| | Present | 1 |
| MRC sum score | 51-60 | 0 |
| | 41-50 | 2 |
| | 31-40 | 4 |
| | 0-30 | 6 |

**At day 7 (0-12 points):** MRC sum score is weighted more heavily (0, 3, 6, or 9 points for the same categories).

**The SID-GBS trial used mEGOS >= 6 at day 7** to define "poor prognosis" patients for randomization to second IVIg dose.

**Predictive performance (day 7 version):**
- AUC for 4-week outcome: 0.78 (95% CI 0.71-0.85)
- AUC for 26-week outcome: 0.70 (95% CI 0.64-0.75)

> van Koningsveld R, Steyerberg EW, Hughes RA, et al. A clinical prognostic scoring system for Guillain-Barre syndrome. *Lancet Neurol*. 2007;6(7):589-594. doi:10.1016/S1474-4422(07)70130-8

> Walgaard C, Lingsma HF, Ruts L, et al. Early recognition of poor prognosis in Guillain-Barre syndrome. *Neurology*. 2011;76(11):968-975. doi:10.1212/WNL.0b013e3182104407

### 3.4 Ventilator-Dependent GBS Outcomes

**Mechanical ventilation requirement**: Approximately 25-30% of all GBS patients require ICU admission and mechanical ventilation.

**Mortality data:**

| Study | Period | n (ventilated) | Mortality |
|-------|--------|---------------|-----------|
| Dhar et al. (2008) | 1984-2007 | 273 | 12.1% overall |
| | 1984-1995 | 100 | 15.0% |
| | 1995-2007 | 173 | 10.4% |
| Lawn et al. (2001) | 1976-1996 | 114 | 20% |
| Ali et al. (2014) | -- | 44 (of 102) | 11.4% |

**Causes of death (Dhar et al.):**
- Sepsis (most common)
- Pneumonia
- Cardiac arrhythmias
- Pulmonary embolism
- Cerebral anoxia
- Bleeding

**Independent predictors of mortality (logistic regression):**

| Factor | Odds Ratio | 95% CI |
|--------|-----------|--------|
| Pneumonia | 4.69 | -- |
| Dysautonomia | 3.14 | -- |
| Hypokalemia | 2.44 | -- |
| Older age | 0.97 per year | -- |

**Recovery of ambulation:**
- Lawn et al. (2001): 79% of surviving ventilated patients eventually regained independent ambulation
- Ali et al. (2014): 23% of ventilated patients achieved complete recovery at 3 months vs 41.8% of non-ventilated
- Recovery may continue for 1-3 years or longer after onset

> Dhar R, Stitt L, Hahn AF. Mortality in mechanically ventilated patients of Guillain-Barre Syndrome. *J Peripher Nerv Syst*. 2007;12(s1):36. (published in Annals Indian Academy Neurology 2012;15:54-58. doi:10.4103/0972-2327.94997)

> Lawn ND, Fletcher DD, Henderson RD, Wolter TD, Wijdicks EF. Long-term outcome in patients with Guillain-Barre syndrome requiring mechanical ventilation. *Neurology*. 2000;54(12):2311-2315. doi:10.1212/WNL.54.12.2311

### 3.5 Dysautonomia in Severe GBS

Autonomic dysfunction occurs in up to **70%** of GBS patients and is a significant contributor to morbidity and mortality:

**Common manifestations (Mukerji et al., 2019):**
- Ileus: 42%
- Hypertension: 39%
- Hypotension: 37%
- Fever: 29%
- Tachycardia or bradycardia: 27%
- Urinary retention: 24%
- Cardiovascular complications: 54.2% of dysautonomic patients

**Mortality impact**: 6% mortality in patients with dysautonomia vs 2% in the overall cohort (P = 0.02)

> Mukerji S, Aloka F, Farooq M, et al. Dysautonomia in Guillain-Barre Syndrome: Prevalence, Clinical Spectrum, and Outcomes. *Neurocrit Care*. 2019;31(3):462-469. doi:10.1007/s12028-019-00770-3

## 4. GBS Not Responding to Plasmapheresis

### 4.1 Expected Timeline for Response to PE

Based on the Cochrane systematic review (Chevret et al., 2017) and major clinical trials:

**Early markers of response (within 4 weeks):**
- Median time to onset of motor recovery: 8 days (PE) vs 15 days (no treatment) in severe GBS
- Median time to walking with assistance: 30 days (PE) vs 44 days (no treatment)
- At 4 weeks, 76.2% of PE-treated patients improved by >= 1 grade on the disability scale

**Natural disease course to consider:**
- Most GBS patients reach nadir within 2-4 weeks
- The plateau phase may last days to weeks before recovery begins
- Some patients, particularly with axonal damage, may not show improvement for weeks to months

**Standard PE regimen:**
- Mild GBS: 2 sessions over 3 days
- Moderate-severe GBS: 4 sessions over 8-9 days
- Ventilator-dependent: 4 sessions (6 sessions showed no additional benefit but increased complications)
- ASFA Guidelines (2019): 1-1.5 plasma volumes per session, 5-6 sessions over 10-14 days, every other day

> Chevret S, Hughes RA, Annane D. Plasma exchange for Guillain-Barre syndrome. *Cochrane Database Syst Rev*. 2017;2(2):CD001798. doi:10.1002/14651858.CD001798.pub3

### 4.2 What Constitutes "Not Responding" After 3 PE Sessions

There is no universally accepted definition of PE failure in GBS. Clinical assessment should consider:

**Signs suggesting inadequate response after 3 sessions:**
- Continued progressive weakness despite 3 sessions over 5-7 days
- No stabilization (continued worsening)
- New development of ventilator dependence during treatment
- Failure to show any improvement by one disability grade after completing the full course

**Important caveat -- Treatment-Related Fluctuation (TRF):**
- 5-6% of GBS patients experience TRF: clinical deterioration within 2 months of onset after initial stabilization or improvement with treatment
- Median time to first TRF: 9 days (range 2-15 days)
- TRF does not mean treatment failure -- it indicates partial treatment efficacy
- TRF is an indication to **repeat first-line treatment** (IVIg or PE)

> Kuitwaard K, de Gelder J, Tio-Gillen AP, et al. Treatment-related fluctuations in Guillain-Barre syndrome after intravenous immunoglobulin or plasma exchange. *J Peripher Nerv Syst*. 2022;27(2):113-120. doi:10.1111/jns.12495

### 4.3 Can Patients Deteriorate During PE and Still Ultimately Respond?

**Yes.** Several patterns are recognized:

1. **Treatment-related fluctuation**: 5-6% of patients deteriorate after initial improvement from PE, then improve with repeat treatment
2. **Continued progression to nadir**: Some patients have not yet reached nadir when PE begins; they may worsen during the treatment course but then stabilize and improve
3. **Rebound phenomenon**: PE-associated relapses occur more frequently than with supportive care alone (RR 2.89, 95% CI 1.05-7.93 in Cochrane analysis), likely due to redistribution of pathogenic antibodies from extravascular to intravascular compartments after removal

**Predictors of TRF (higher risk):**
- Preceding infectious mononucleosis (EBV/CMV): 28.57% vs 8.55% (p=0.01)
- Higher initial disability score: median 4 vs 2 (p=0.01)
- Demyelinating subtype (AIDP): 85.71% of TRF cases
- Neuropathic pain: 100% in TRF group

### 4.4 What To Do When PE Fails

#### Step 1: Reassess the Diagnosis
- Rule out A-CIDP (see Section 2)
- Consider other mimics: critical illness polyneuropathy, vasculitic neuropathy, lymphomatous polyradiculopathy, diphtheria

#### Step 2: Options After PE Failure

**a) IVIg (2 g/kg over 5 days):**
- Can be given after PE (but NOT during PE, as PE would remove the administered immunoglobulin)
- The 2023 EAN/PNS guideline advises consideration of IVIg or repeat PE in patients with GBS-TRF
- Evidence for benefit after PE failure is limited and largely anecdotal

**b) Second course of IVIg (if IVIg was first-line):**

The **SID-GBS trial** (Walgaard et al., Lancet Neurol 2021) is the definitive evidence:
- Design: Double-blind, RCT, n=93 poor-prognosis patients (mEGOS >= 6)
- Intervention: Second IVIg dose (2 g/kg over 5 days) vs placebo, 7-9 days after first course
- **Result: No benefit** -- adjusted common OR for improvement 1.4 (95% CI 0.6-3.3; p=0.45)
- **Harms**: More serious adverse events (35% vs 16%), including thromboembolic events; 4 deaths in intervention group
- **Conclusion**: "A second intravenous immunoglobulin course should not be considered"

**c) Combined PE + IVIg -- Current Evidence:**

The PE/Sandoglobulin GBS Trial (1997) tested combined PE + IVIg vs each alone:
- **No benefit** from combination over either treatment alone
- The 2023 EAN/PNS guidelines strongly advocate against combination therapy
- Sequential PE followed by IVIg (or vice versa) is similarly not recommended

**d) Rescue therapies under investigation:**

| Agent | Mechanism | Evidence | Status |
|-------|-----------|----------|--------|
| Eculizumab | Anti-C5 complement inhibitor | Phase 2 (JET-GBS): 74% vs 15% running at 24 weeks; Phase 3: **no significant benefit** | Not recommended by EAN/PNS 2023 |
| Rituximab | Anti-CD20 B-cell depletion | Case reports only | No RCT evidence |
| Alemtuzumab | Anti-CD52 lymphocyte depletion | Single case reports | Experimental only |
| Imlifidase (IdeS) | IgG-cleaving enzyme | Under investigation | Pre-clinical/early trials |

> Walgaard C, Jacobs BC, Lingsma HF, et al; Dutch GBS Study Group. Second intravenous immunoglobulin dose in patients with Guillain-Barre syndrome with poor prognosis (SID-GBS): a double-blind, randomised, placebo-controlled trial. *Lancet Neurol*. 2021;20(4):275-283. doi:10.1016/S1474-4422(20)30494-4

> Misawa S, Kuwabara S, Sato Y, et al. Safety and efficacy of eculizumab in Guillain-Barre syndrome: a multicentre, double-blind, randomised phase 2 trial. *Lancet Neurol*. 2018;17(6):519-529. doi:10.1016/S1474-4422(18)30114-5

> Kuwabara S, Misawa S, Mori M, et al. Efficacy and safety of eculizumab in Guillain-Barre syndrome: a phase 3, multicenter, double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled clinical trial. *J Peripher Nerv Syst*. 2024;29(3):326-335. doi:10.1111/jns.12646

> Plasma Exchange/Sandoglobulin Guillain-Barre Syndrome Trial Group. Randomised trial of plasma exchange, intravenous immunoglobulin, and combined treatments in Guillain-Barre syndrome. *Lancet*. 1997;349(9047):225-230.

### 4.5 The 2023 EAN/PNS Guideline Summary on Treatment

Key recommendations from the GRADE-based guideline:
1. **IVIg and PE are equally effective** first-line treatments for GBS patients who cannot walk unaided
2. **Combination therapy (PE + IVIg) is not recommended**
3. **A second IVIg course is not recommended** for patients with poor prognosis
4. **Corticosteroids are not effective** in GBS (and may worsen outcomes)
5. For **GBS-TRF**: Repeat IVIg or PE may be considered (as A-CIDP may be indistinguishable from GBS in the acute phase)
6. **Eculizumab is not recommended**

> Leonhard SE, Mandarakas MR, Gondim FAA, et al. Diagnosis and management of Guillain-Barre syndrome in ten steps. *Nat Rev Neurol*. 2019;15(11):671-683. doi:10.1038/s41582-019-0250-9

## 5. Coagulopathy During Plasmapheresis in GBS

### 5.1 Incidence and Mechanism

Coagulopathy is an expected consequence of therapeutic plasma exchange when albumin is used as the replacement solution:

- **Each 1.0 plasma volume exchange depletes approximately 65% of circulating coagulation factors**
- Approximately **85% of fibrinogen** is removed per session when albumin is the replacement fluid
- In a study by Norda et al.: fibrinogen levels decreased from 332 +/- 106 mg/dL to 96 +/- 44 mg/dL after the first session

**Bleeding rates during PE (Ward et al., 2015):**
- TPE alone: 2.3% of treatments
- Immunoadsorption alone: 1.3%
- Combined TPE/IAS: 3.1%

Despite significant coagulation factor depletion, clinically significant bleeding is uncommon with appropriate monitoring and scheduling.

> Ward DM. Conventional apheresis therapies: a review. *J Clin Apher*. 2011;26(5):230-238.

> Norda R, Stegmayr BG. Therapeutic apheresis in Sweden: update of epidemiology and adverse events. *Transfus Apher Sci*. 2003;29(2):159-166.

### 5.2 Which Clotting Factors Are Depleted

**Most significantly affected:**

| Factor | Depletion | Recovery Time |
|--------|-----------|---------------|
| Fibrinogen (Factor I) | ~85% per session | 48-72 hours; full recovery may take 96-120 hours |
| Factor XIII | Significant | 96-120 hours |
| Factor V | ~65% | 24-48 hours |
| Factor VIII | ~65% | 24-48 hours |
| Antithrombin III | ~65% | 24-48 hours |
| von Willebrand factor | ~65% | 24-48 hours |

**Key point**: While most coagulation factors recover to hemostatic levels within 24 hours, **fibrinogen and Factor XIII have the longest recovery times** (96-120 hours) and are the primary drivers of PE-associated coagulopathy.

**Effect on coagulation tests:**
- Significant prolongation of PT, aPTT, and thrombin time immediately post-exchange
- INR elevation (clinically relevant for 24-48 hours post-session)
- These normalize faster than fibrinogen levels

> Ward DM, Grupp C, Tiede A. Fibrinogen reduction and bleeding complications in plasma exchange, immunoadsorption and a combination of the two. *Vox Sang*. 2015;108(3):262-270. doi:10.1111/vox.12219

### 5.3 Management of Coagulopathy

**Monitoring protocol:**
- Check fibrinogen level before each PE session
- Monitor PT/INR, aPTT, and platelet count
- Assess for clinical bleeding signs before and after each session

**Fibrinogen thresholds and replacement:**

| Fibrinogen Level | Action |
|-----------------|--------|
| >150 mg/dL | Proceed with PE; albumin replacement |
| 100-150 mg/dL | Proceed with caution; consider adding FFP as partial replacement |
| <100 mg/dL | Traditional threshold for supplementation with cryoprecipitate or FFP |
| <80 mg/dL | Recent evidence (Wodajo et al., 2024) suggests this threshold may be safe without supplementation in patients not on anticoagulation |

**Replacement options:**
1. **Cryoprecipitate**: Preferred for fibrinogen replacement (high fibrinogen content per volume); typically 10 units
2. **Fresh Frozen Plasma (FFP)**: Contains all coagulation factors; indicated as partial or sole replacement when PE is performed immediately before surgery
3. **Fibrinogen concentrate**: Increasingly used; avoids infectious disease risks of pooled products

**Recent evidence (Wodajo et al., 2024):** A prospective single-center analysis showed that a pre-procedure fibrinogen threshold of 80-100 mg/dL without plasma supplementation does not increase bleeding risk in patients not receiving anticoagulation, challenging the traditional 100 mg/dL threshold. However, **no evidence-based guidelines exist** for fibrinogen supplementation thresholds during PE.

> Wodajo AB, Goel R, Engel ER, et al. A lower fibrinogen threshold does not lead to increased bleeding risk in patients receiving therapeutic plasma exchange: A prospective single-center analysis. *Transfusion*. 2024;64(7):1215-1223. doi:10.1111/trf.17865

### 5.4 When to Pause and Restart PE

**Indications to pause PE:**
- Fibrinogen <80 mg/dL with active bleeding or planned invasive procedure
- Active clinically significant bleeding from any site
- Hemodynamic instability during the procedure (hypotension unresponsive to fluid bolus)
- Anaphylaxis or severe allergic reaction to replacement fluid
- Vascular access failure

**Scheduling to minimize coagulopathy:**
- **Every-other-day regimen** is recommended (allows hepatic synthesis to regenerate coagulation factors between sessions)
- Minimum 48-hour interval between sessions to allow fibrinogen recovery
- ASFA recommends: 1-1.5 plasma volumes per session, 5-6 sessions over 10-14 days

**Restarting after pause:**
- Confirm fibrinogen >100 mg/dL (or >150 mg/dL if invasive procedure is planned)
- Ensure PT/INR and aPTT are trending toward normal
- Consider using partial FFP replacement for the next 1-2 sessions
- Resume every-other-day schedule

### 5.5 Implications for Tracheostomy Timing

This creates a direct clinical tension:
- **PE requires ongoing sessions** with associated coagulopathy
- **Tracheostomy is a surgical procedure** requiring adequate hemostasis
- The optimal tracheostomy window in ventilated GBS patients often overlaps with the PE treatment window

**Practical approach:**
1. Complete the PE course (or at least 3-4 sessions) before tracheostomy if clinically feasible
2. If tracheostomy cannot wait, schedule it on a PE-free day (>48 hours after last session)
3. Check fibrinogen (target >150 mg/dL), PT/INR (<1.5), and platelet count (>50,000/uL) before proceeding
4. Use FFP as replacement fluid for the PE session immediately before the planned tracheostomy
5. Resume PE 24-48 hours after uncomplicated tracheostomy

## 6. Tracheostomy in GBS

### 6.1 Optimal Timing

**General ICU guidance:**
- Translaryngeal intubation preferred for expected ventilation <= 10 days
- Tracheostomy should be considered when expected ventilation exceeds 14 days
- Tracheostomy after 21 days of intubation is associated with higher weaning failure rate, longer ICU stay, and higher ICU mortality

**GBS-specific decision making at day 7 post-intubation** (Walgaard et al., 2017):

The critical assessment point is **day 7 after intubation**, using arm strength and nerve conduction studies:

| Risk Factor | Probability of Prolonged MV (>=14 days) |
|-------------|------------------------------------------|
| Unable to lift arms from bed (bilateral deltoid MRC 0-2) | **87%** |
| Able to lift arms (MRC 3-10) | 69% |
| Unable to lift arms + axonal/unexcitable NCS | **96%** |
| Able to lift arms + axonal/unexcitable NCS | **90%** |
| Able to lift arms + AIDP/equivocal NCS | 67% |

**Recommendation**: Ventilated GBS patients who are unable to lift the arms from the bed and/or have axonal degeneration or unexcitable nerves at 1 week are at **high risk of prolonged MV, and tracheostomy should be considered**.

**Effect of early tracheostomy on mortality in GBS (Meng et al., 2021):**
A nationwide observational study found:
- Early tracheostomy was not associated with lower in-hospital mortality
- However, the early group had lower 90-day mortality (31.2% vs 47.8%), shorter ICU and hospital stays, and shorter ventilator length of stay

> Walgaard C, Lingsma HF, van Doorn PA, et al. Tracheostomy or Not: Prediction of Prolonged Mechanical Ventilation in Guillain-Barre Syndrome. *Neurocrit Care*. 2017;26(2):228-235. doi:10.1007/s12028-016-0311-5

> Meng SH, Lin J, Jiang JJ, et al. Effect of Early Tracheostomy on Mortality of Mechanically Ventilated Patients with Guillain-Barre Syndrome: A Nationwide Observational Study. *Neurocrit Care*. 2021;34(1):203-211. doi:10.1007/s12028-020-00965-9

### 6.2 Impact on Outcomes

**Tracheostomy itself does not worsen prognosis** in GBS; rather, the need for tracheostomy reflects disease severity:

- Patients requiring prolonged MV (>=14 days) have significantly longer time to regain independent walking compared to short-duration MV
- Those ventilated <14 days achieve recovery comparable to unventilated patients
- Mortality: no significant difference between prolonged vs short-duration MV groups (~6% vs 5%) in the Walgaard et al. study
- Tracheostomy prior to active weaning may hasten liberation from ventilation

**Complications of prolonged intubation/tracheostomy in GBS:**
- Ventilator-associated pneumonia: 34.4% of ventilated patients
- Tracheal stenosis (late complication)
- Vocal cord paralysis (may be both GBS-related and iatrogenic)

### 6.3 Weaning Protocols for GBS

**Key principles:**
- Weaning in GBS is dictated by **recovery of respiratory muscle strength**, particularly diaphragmatic function
- This parallels overall neurological recovery and cannot be accelerated by weaning protocols alone
- Daily spontaneous breathing trials (SBT) should begin when clinically appropriate

**Weaning success data (Plant et al., 2021):**
- 29 GBS patients with prolonged ventilation referred to a specialist weaning center
- **76% (22/29) successfully weaned** from invasive ventilation
- 59% (13/22) used noninvasive ventilation (NIV) as a bridge therapy during weaning
- 14% (3/22) required long-term nocturnal NIV post-discharge

**Recent multicenter study (Thille et al., 2025, n=513 GBS, 47 ICUs):** *[Level 2]*
- GBS: 64% prolonged weaning (≥7 days) vs 35% MG
- 57% tracheostomy, 26% reintubation, 9.4% mortality
- Ineffective cough strongest predictor of reintubation (61%)

**Weaning assessment criteria:**
- Adequate cough strength and secretion clearance
- Ability to tolerate SBT for increasing durations (initially 30 minutes, progressing to 2-4 hours)
- Negative inspiratory force (NIF) > -20 cm H2O
- Vital capacity > 10-15 mL/kg
- Stable hemodynamics and oxygen saturation during trials
- Resolution of active pulmonary infection

> Plant CL, Young CA, Baikie C, et al. Prolonged Ventilatory Support for Patients Recovering From Guillain-Barre Syndrome. *Neurol Clin Pract*. 2021;11(4):e473-e479. doi:10.1212/CPJ.0000000000000793

> Thille AW, Bayon C, Gauvrit M, et al. Characteristics of ventilator weaning in patients intubated for Guillain-Barré syndrome or myasthenia gravis: a nationwide multicenter study. *Intensive Care Med*. 2025;51:2054-2064. PMID: 41114833

### 6.4 Recovery Timeline for Ventilator-Dependent Patients

**Median duration of mechanical ventilation**: 21-28 days (range 1-527 days in severe cases)

**Decannulation timeline:**
- Median time to tracheostomy decannulation: **193 days** (range 49-527 days) in severely affected patients requiring specialist weaning center transfer (Plant et al., 2021)
- 4 of 29 patients required >12 months for weaning

**Functional recovery milestones:**

| Timepoint | Expected Recovery |
|-----------|-------------------|
| 1-2 weeks | Nadir; assessment for tracheostomy |
| 4-6 weeks | Initial signs of motor recovery in some patients |
| 3 months | 23% of ventilated patients achieve complete recovery |
| 6-12 months | Majority of motor recovery occurs |
| 1-2 years | Continued slow improvement; 75% reach pre-GBS function |
| 3+ years | Some residual weakness in ~30% of adults |

**Outcome by phenotype (Plant et al.):**
- **Axonal disease** (n=13): 10/13 (77%) successfully weaned; 6 regained functional mobility
- **Demyelinating disease** (n=10): 6/10 (60%) weaned; 3 gained functional mobility
- **Overall 36-month mortality**: 17% (5/29)

**Long-term perspective:**
- About 20% of GBS patients will have long-term disability requiring assistive devices
- Recovery may continue for years; even patients requiring >12 months of ventilation can ultimately achieve independent ambulation
- Rehabilitation intensity is a major determinant of outcome; high-intensity multidisciplinary rehabilitation beneficial up to 12 months from onset

## 7. SCIG Discontinuation and GBS Recurrence — Diagnostic and Therapeutic Implications

### 7.1 Immunoglobulin as Immunomodulator (Beyond Infection Prevention)

Even at replacement doses (400–600 mg/kg/month), immunoglobulin exerts immunomodulatory effects:
- **FcRn saturation**: Supraphysiological IgG reduces autoantibody half-life by ~40% (Dalakas, *Neurotherapeutics* 2021)
- **Complement inhibition**: Prevents C3/C4 deposition and MAC formation
- **Fc-γ-RIIb upregulation**: Enhanced inhibitory receptor expression blocks ADCC
- **Cytokine suppression**: Dose-dependent downregulation of TNF-α, IL-1β, MMP-2/9
- **Gene expression**: IVIg alters >2,200 genes in responsive autoimmune conditions

SCIG at replacement doses (e.g., Gamanorm ~100 mg/kg/week) provides continuous low-level immunomodulation — a constant anti-inflammatory baseline, mechanistically distinct from simple infection prophylaxis.

### 7.2 CIDP Parallel: Maintenance Immunoglobulin Prevents Relapses

| Study | Finding |
|-------|---------|
| **PATH trial** (van Schaik, *Lancet Neurol* 2018; PMID: 29122523) | Phase 3 RCT, n=172, 69 centers. IgPro20 (20% SCIg) weekly x 24 wk. Relapse/withdrawal: placebo 63%, low dose (0.2 g/kg/wk) 39% (p=0.007), high dose (0.4 g/kg/wk) 33% (p=0.001). NNT ~4 (low dose), ~3.3 (high dose). Well tolerated. |
| **ICE trial** (Hughes, *Lancet Neurol* 2008) | IVIg maintenance: significantly fewer CIDP relapses over 48 weeks |
| **Adrichem** (*Brain* 2022) | After IVIg withdrawal in stable CIDP: 59% relapsed (majority within 12 weeks). Only 28% stable at 76 weeks |

**For R-GBS**: No RCTs exist (2–5% recurrence rate makes trials impractical). The CIDP parallel is the strongest available evidence by analogy.

### 7.3 Immune Rebound After SCIG/IVIg Discontinuation

Direct evidence of autoimmune rebound after SCIG discontinuation is limited:

- **Stiff Person Syndrome**: In crossover trial, all patients rebounded during placebo phase (Dalakas, *Neurology* 2001)
- **CIDP withdrawal**: Mean time to nadir 3.5 months (abrupt) to 8.8 months (gradual). 94% restabilized within 12 weeks of restarting (Adrichem 2022)
- **Estimated 60%** of patients on chronic IVIg who stop experience objective neurological deterioration (Dalakas 2021)

**For this patient**: The ~2-year interval between SCIG discontinuation (~2023) and GBS recurrence (March 2026) is longer than typical 3–6 month CIDP rebound window. This makes direct "rebound" less likely, but does not exclude loss of chronic protective immunomodulation that was suppressing a latent autoimmune predisposition.

### 7.4 SCIG Discontinuation and the R-GBS vs A-CIDP Differential

**Factors favoring R-GBS:**
- 36-year interval (first episode at 16, recurrence at 52). Kuitwaard et al. (*JNNP* 2009) documented R-GBS intervals up to 37 years.
- A-CIDP by definition evolves into chronicity within weeks to months, not decades later (Ruts, *Neurology* 2010)
- R-GBS patients characteristically achieve complete recovery between episodes

**Factors that could favor A-CIDP:**
- Treatment dependency pattern: stable on SCIG, relapsed after withdrawal — exactly the pattern in CIDP dependency testing
- If current episode shows progression >8 weeks or ≥3 fluctuations → reclassify as A-CIDP
- Inan et al. (*Acta Neurol Belg* 2024) noted that recent evidence challenges whether R-GBS exists as truly distinct from CIDP variants

**The SCIG discontinuation adds a new dimension:**
This patient appears to have been "SCIG-dependent" in retrospect — clinically stable during ~2–3 years of treatment, relapse ~2 years after cessation. This is the pattern of immunoglobulin dependency.

### 7.5 Diagnostic Workup to Resolve

| Test | Purpose | Interpretation |
|------|---------|----------------|
| CSF IL-8 | GBS vs CIDP | <70 pg/mL: 96.7% specificity for CIDP |
| Anti-NF155 IgG4 | Autoimmune nodopathy/A-CIDP | Positive → strongly favors CIDP |
| Serial NCS | Electrophysiological pattern | Temporal dispersion, conduction block in non-entrapment → CIDP |
| Clinical course >8 weeks | Ruts criteria | Progression beyond 8 weeks → reclassify as A-CIDP |

### 7.6 Recommendation: Restart SCIG Regardless of Classification

After the acute phase resolves, SCIG reinstatement is supported by dual indication:
- **If R-GBS**: Prophylaxis by analogy to CIDP evidence + original IgA-deficiency indication
- **If A-CIDP**: Maintenance immunoglobulin is standard of care
- **IgA safety**: Patient previously tolerated Gamanorm; SCIG is inherently safer than IVIg for IgA-deficient patients (slow absorption, no serum IgA spike)
