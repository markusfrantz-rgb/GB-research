## 1. Ventilator Management in GBS

### 1.1 Epidemiology of Respiratory Failure in GBS

Approximately 17-30% of GBS patients develop respiratory failure requiring mechanical ventilation, making GBS one of the leading causes of acute neuromuscular respiratory failure (Walgaard et al., 2017; Weiss et al., 2025). Among those admitted to the ICU, roughly 20% require mechanical ventilation (Weiss et al., 2025). The mechanisms of respiratory compromise are multifactorial:

- **Diaphragmatic and intercostal muscle weakness** reducing vital capacity and causing microatelectasis
- **Pharyngeal and laryngeal muscle weakness** impairing secretion clearance and airway protection
- **Reduced cough effectiveness** increasing infection risk
- **Autonomic dysfunction** contributing to bronchospasm and secretion production

(Patel & Gwathmey, 2017)

### 1.2 Predicting the Need for Mechanical Ventilation

#### Clinical Predictors

Six independent risk factors for requiring mechanical ventilation have been identified:

| Risk Factor | Odds Ratio |
|---|---|
| Inability to cough | 9.09 |
| Neck flexion weakness | 4.34 |
| Inability to abduct shoulder horizontally | 2.99 |
| GBS disability grade >=4 (wheelchair-bound/bedridden) | 2.53 |
| Symptom onset to admission <7 days | 2.51 |
| Liver cytolysis | 2.09 |

The presence of four of these criteria is associated with an 85% risk of requiring mechanical ventilation (Weiss et al., 2025).

#### The "20/30/40 Rule" (Bedside Pulmonary Thresholds)

- Vital capacity <20 mL/kg (or <60% predicted, or >30% decline in 24 hours)
- Maximum inspiratory pressure (MIP) <-30 cmH2O (less negative)
- Maximum expiratory pressure (MEP) <+40 cmH2O
- Single breath count <15

(Lawn et al., 2001; Patel & Gwathmey, 2017)

#### EGRIS (Erasmus GBS Respiratory Insufficiency Score)

The EGRIS predicts the probability of mechanical ventilation within the first week of admission (Walgaard et al., 2010). It has been internationally validated with AUC >= 0.80 across subgroups (Kanikannan et al., 2022).

**EGRIS Components and Scoring:**

| Component | Category | Points |
|---|---|---|
| **Days from weakness onset to admission** | >7 days | 0 |
| | 4-7 days | 1 |
| | <=3 days | 2 |
| **Facial/bulbar weakness at admission** | Absent | 0 |
| | Present | 1 |
| **MRC sum score at admission** | 51-60 | 0 |
| | 41-50 | 1 |
| | 31-40 | 2 |
| | 21-30 | 3 |
| | <=20 | 4 |

**Risk Stratification (Total Score 0-7):**

| EGRIS Score | Risk Category | Probability of MV |
|---|---|---|
| 0-2 | Low | ~4% |
| 3-4 | Intermediate | ~24% |
| 5-7 | High | ~65% |

(Walgaard et al., 2010; Kanikannan et al., 2022)

### 1.3 Intubation Considerations

**Critical clinical pearl:** Do not rely solely on FVC cutoffs; integrate clinical assessment including work of breathing, secretion control, bulbar function, and overall weakness trajectory (Farkas, EMCrit IBCC).

**Red flags for imminent need for intubation:**
- Neck flexion weakness (inability to lift head off pillow)
- Bulbar/facial weakness with aspiration risk
- Inability to cough effectively
- Progressive multi-muscle weakness
- Dysautonomia (cardiac, bladder, pupillary dysfunction)
- FVC declining >30% in 24 hours

**Procedural precautions during intubation:**
- **Avoid succinylcholine** -- risk of lethal hyperkalemia from denervated muscle
- **Prepare push-dose epinephrine** for potential vagal episodes (dysautonomia)
- **Mild fluid resuscitation** if volume-depleted from poor oral intake
- **Use non-hypotensive induction agents** when significant dysautonomia is present
- **Non-invasive ventilation (NIV) is contraindicated** in patients with swallowing disorders, bilateral facial paralysis, or ineffective cough

(Farkas, EMCrit IBCC; Weiss et al., 2025)

### 1.4 Ventilation Strategies

There is no evidence that GBS patients benefit from any specific ventilation mode over another. Standard ICU ventilation protocols apply (Farkas, EMCrit IBCC). Key principles include:

- **Lung-protective ventilation** with tidal volumes of 6-8 mL/kg ideal body weight
- **Avoid SIMV** due to poor overall evidence support
- **Pressure support ventilation** during daytime with volume-controlled ventilation at night may reduce atelectasis risk
- **Regular assessment of respiratory mechanics** (FVC, MIP, MEP) to track recovery

### 1.5 Weaning Criteria and Protocols

Weaning from mechanical ventilation in GBS is a complex process. The median duration of mechanical ventilation in severe GBS is **28 days (IQR 12-60 days)** based on a large Dutch cohort of 552 patients, of whom 150 (27%) required ventilation (Walgaard et al., 2017). Among those ventilated, 71% required prolonged ventilation (>14 days).

#### Prerequisites for Initiating Weaning

- Completion of immunotherapy (IVIg or plasma exchange)
- Effective cough capability returning
- Low secretion volume
- Absence of active respiratory infection
- Ability to lift head off the bed

(Weiss et al., 2025)

#### Respiratory Parameters for Weaning

- **FVC >15 mL/kg** suggests readiness for extubation
- Alternatively, begin weaning at **FVC 7 mL/kg** with progressively prolonged spontaneous breathing trials (SBTs); extubate when FVC exceeds 15 mL/kg with 24 consecutive hours of spontaneous breathing
- **MIP > -20 cmH2O** and/or **MEP > +40 cmH2O** before performing SBTs
- FVC increase of >=4 mL/kg from pre-intubation baseline

(Weiss et al., 2025; Farkas, EMCrit IBCC)

#### SBT Protocol

1. Use prolonged cuff-inflated T-piece SBTs with ZEEP (zero end-expiratory pressure)
2. Progressively increase duration over days (target 6-8 hours)
3. After demonstrating normal ventilation during cuff-inflated trials, assess secretion drainage with cuff-deflated T-piece SBTs
4. Patient must tolerate deflated cuff and clear secretions independently to avoid extubation failure

(Weiss et al., 2025)

#### Post-Extubation Support

- Extubate to **BiPAP or high-flow nasal cannula** to reduce work of breathing and reintubation risk
- NIV as a bridge post-extubation may improve outcomes

(Farkas, EMCrit IBCC; Orlikowski et al., 2021)

**Important:** Diaphragm strength may recover before extremity muscle strength. It is possible to successfully extubate patients who still have significant limb weakness (Farkas, EMCrit IBCC).

### 1.6 Tracheostomy Timing and Outcomes

#### Predictors of Prolonged Ventilation (and Tracheostomy Need)

- **Severe limb weakness** (MRC sum score <=4 bilaterally in deltoids) at 1 week post-intubation -- strongest predictor (87% chance of prolonged MV)
- **Axonal neuropathy (AMAN) or unexcitable nerves** on nerve conduction studies -- 100% required prolonged MV in one cohort
- **Combined axonal/unexcitable NCS with arm weakness** -- 96% chance of prolonged MV
- Older age, underlying lung disease
- Lack of foot flexion ability at completion of immunotherapy
- Rapid progression to severe weakness (<1 week)

(Walgaard et al., 2017; Weiss et al., 2025)

#### Timing of Tracheostomy

A landmark nationwide observational study from Japan (919 ventilated GBS patients, 2010-2018) compared early tracheostomy (within 7 days) versus late/no tracheostomy (Kanda et al., 2020):

| Outcome | Early Tracheostomy | Non-Early | Difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| In-hospital mortality (adjusted) | 8.1% | 7.5% | 0.4% (NS) |
| 28-day mortality (adjusted) | -- | -- | -1.3% (NS) |
| Nosocomial pneumonia | 13.3% | 15.7% | -2.6% (NS) |
| ICU length of stay (median) | 13 days | 14 days | -0.2 days (NS) |
| MV duration (median, survivors) | 30 days | 28 days | 11.2 days (NS) |

**Conclusion:** Early tracheostomy (within 7 days) was not associated with decreased mortality or morbidity. No outcome reached statistical significance (Kanda et al., 2020).

#### Current Recommendations

It is reasonable to consider tracheostomy in a patient who:
- Remains ventilator-dependent after **2 weeks** and has failed multiple weaning trials
- Has had at least **one failed extubation**
- Has completed immunotherapy with poor respiratory recovery

(Weiss et al., 2025)

#### Benefits of Tracheostomy in Conscious GBS Patients

- Enhanced patient comfort, reduced sedation needs
- Lower delirium risk
- Safer, earlier mobilization to chair
- Improved oral hygiene
- Facilitated communication (speaking valves)
- Better assessment of swallowing and bulbar function
- Gradual weaning from respiratory assistance while maintaining the cannula

(Weiss et al., 2025; Farkas, EMCrit IBCC)

#### Decannulation

- Median time from presentation to tracheostomy decannulation: **193 days** (range 49-527 days) in severely affected patients
- Some patients require >12 months to wean
- Persistent laryngeal sensory deficits may delay decannulation
- Mini-tracheostomy tubes may be retained for secretion management after weaning from ventilation

(Cheng et al., 2021)

## 2. Autonomic Dysfunction in Severe GBS

### 2.1 Prevalence

Autonomic dysfunction (dysautonomia) is reported in **two-thirds (approximately 65-70%)** of GBS patients overall, though estimates range from 38-70% depending on diagnostic criteria and study population (Zochodne, 1994; Chakraborty et al., 2020; Weiss et al., 2025). In ventilated patients specifically, the prevalence rises to **75%** (Weiss et al., 2025).

Dysautonomia is more prevalent in:
- Patients with severe motor weakness (tetraplegia)
- Those requiring mechanical ventilation
- Patients with illness duration >5 weeks (55.8% vs 30.3% in shorter illness, p=0.005)
- AMAN and AMSAN subtypes

(Chakraborty et al., 2020; Mushtaq et al., 2021)

### 2.2 Types of Autonomic Dysfunction

#### Cardiovascular Manifestations (Most Dangerous)

| Manifestation | Approximate Prevalence |
|---|---|
| Sinus tachycardia (most common arrhythmia) | 23-75% (higher in ventilated) |
| Hypertension | 28-39% |
| Blood pressure lability | 12.5-37% |
| Hypotension/orthostatic hypotension | 37% |
| Bradycardia (including severe bradycardia to asystole) | 7-34% |

(Zochodne, 1994; Mushtaq et al., 2021; Chakraborty et al., 2020)

**Dangerous arrhythmias:** Life-threatening bradyarrhythmias ranging from severe bradycardia to **asystole** occur in 7-34% of patients. These can be precipitated by minimal stimulation such as tracheal suctioning, intubation, mouth care, or bathing (Weiss et al., 2025).

**Takotsubo cardiomyopathy** is a rare but life-threatening complication that can occur in the context of severe catecholamine surges from sympathetic dysregulation (Weiss et al., 2025).

#### Gastrointestinal Manifestations

| Manifestation | Approximate Prevalence |
|---|---|
| Constipation | 22% |
| Diarrhea | 21.2% |
| Ileus / functional bowel obstruction | 17-42% |
| Gastroparesis | Variable |
| GI hemorrhage | 7% |

(Mushtaq et al., 2021; Weiss et al., 2025)

Ileus is frequently problematic in ICU-bound GBS patients and can lead to functional bowel obstruction. It may be exacerbated by opioid use, immobility, and autonomic nerve dysfunction (Weiss et al., 2025).

#### Urological Manifestations

- **Urinary retention:** 15-24% of patients
- Usually self-limiting but may require Foley catheterization during the acute phase

(Mushtaq et al., 2021; Zochodne, 1994)

#### Other Autonomic Manifestations

- **Sudomotor dysfunction** (abnormal sweating)
- **Pupillary abnormalities**
- **Fever** (29% of patients, attributed to autonomic dysfunction rather than infection in some cases)
- **Posterior Reversible Encephalopathy Syndrome (PRES):** Occurs in 7% of dysautonomic patients, potentially exacerbated by IVIg treatment

(Mushtaq et al., 2021; Weiss et al., 2025)

### 2.3 Diagnostic Criteria for Dysautonomia

Some authors suggest diagnosing dysautonomia if **systolic blood pressure varies by more than 85 mmHg within the same day** without confounding cause. This threshold demonstrates greater sensitivity than previously proposed 40-50 mmHg daily variation criteria (Weiss et al., 2025).

### 2.4 Management of Autonomic Dysfunction

#### General Principles

- **Conservative management is preferred** for most autonomic manifestations
- **Avoid over-treatment:** Hemodynamic swings are often self-limited; aggressive treatment can cause iatrogenic oscillations
- **Maintain 1 mg IV atropine at bedside** at all times for patients with severe dysautonomia
- Mortality is **three times higher** (6% vs 2%) among patients with dysautonomia

(Weiss et al., 2025)

#### Blood Pressure Instability

**Hypertension:**
- Avoid treatment unless MAP >120 mmHg or evidence of target-organ damage
- **First-line:** Remove stimulus (treat pain, agitation, full bladder)
- **Second-line:** Short-acting IV vasodilators (nicardipine or clevidipine infusions) or oral calcium-channel blockers (nifedipine XR, isradipine)
- **Avoid** long-acting antihypertensives (risk of precipitating hypotension during swings)

**Hypotension:**
- Fluid resuscitation if truly hypovolemic
- Vasopressors at lowest possible dose, only if necessary
- **Discontinue beta-blockers** (increase risk of bradycardia)

(Farkas, EMCrit IBCC; Weiss et al., 2025)

#### Bradycardia and Cardiac Arrest

- Avoid vagal triggers: gentle suctioning, minimize oral care stimulation
- **IV atropine** for symptomatic bradycardia
- **Temporary cardiac pacing** for severe, refractory bradycardia
- Remove all negative chronotropes
- Maintain normal potassium levels
- Avoid sedatives that may worsen bradycardia

(Farkas, EMCrit IBCC; Weiss et al., 2025)

#### Gastrointestinal Dysautonomia

- Aggressive bowel regimen for constipation/ileus
- **Pro-motility agents** (metoclopramide, erythromycin) for gastroparesis
- **Post-pyloric feeding** if gastroparesis prevents gastric feeding
- **Limit opioids** (worsen ileus)
- Consider **oral naloxone or IV methylnaltrexone** for opioid-induced ileus
- **Neostigmine** with caution for pseudo-obstruction; pre-treat with **glycopyrrolate 0.4 mg** to reduce bradycardia risk
- Rectal tube for distension

(Farkas, EMCrit IBCC)

### 2.5 Monitoring Requirements

- **Continuous cardiac monitoring** (telemetry) for all ICU-admitted GBS patients
- **Continuous blood pressure monitoring** (arterial line preferred in severe dysautonomia)
- **Daily 12-lead ECG** to detect interval changes
- **Strict intake/output** monitoring
- **Bladder scan** to assess for urinary retention
- **Abdominal assessment** for ileus
- **Electrolyte monitoring** (especially potassium, sodium)

**Note:** There is currently no consensus on standardized dysautonomia assessment protocols, representing a significant management gap (Weiss et al., 2025).

### 2.6 Hyponatremia

Hyponatremia in GBS may be caused by salt-wasting syndrome (possibly due to altered adrenal autoregulation from autonomic dysfunction) or pseudohyponatremia from IVIg-related protein elevation. It typically responds to sodium chloride administration (IV or oral), with dosage adjusted based on urinary sodium losses (Weiss et al., 2025).

## 3. Pain Management in GBS

### 3.1 Prevalence and Types of Pain

Pain is one of the most underappreciated aspects of GBS. It occurs in **up to 89% of patients** during the course of the disease and can be the presenting symptom in some cases (Ruts et al., 2010; Pandey et al., 2002).

#### Types of Pain

| Pain Type | Characteristics | Timing |
|---|---|---|
| **Neuropathic pain** | Burning, tingling, electric-shock sensations, allodynia, paresthesia | Acute and chronic phases |
| **Radicular pain** | Low back pain, sciatica-like pain from nerve root inflammation | Often precedes weakness |
| **Musculoskeletal pain** | Deep aching, muscle cramps, joint pain from immobility | Throughout course |
| **Visceral pain** | Abdominal discomfort from autonomic dysfunction/ileus | Acute phase |
| **Meningism-like pain** | Neck stiffness, headache | Early acute phase |

Pain can be **severe and difficult to manage** even with multiple analgesic treatments (Weiss et al., 2025). In mechanically ventilated patients, pain assessment is particularly challenging and may require the use of behavioral pain scales (BPS or CPOT).

### 3.2 Pharmacological Treatment

#### First-Line: Gabapentinoids

**Gabapentin** is the best-studied agent for GBS pain:

- **Dosing:** 15 mg/kg/day in three divided doses (typically 300-900 mg TID)
- In a double-blinded, placebo-controlled crossover RCT of 18 ICU ventilated GBS patients:
  - Mean pain score reduction from 7.22 +/- 0.83 to 2.06 +/- 0.63 (MD -3.61, 95% CI -4.12 to -3.10)
  - Significant fentanyl consumption reduction: -251 mcg (95% CI -265 to -238 mcg)
  - Reduced sedation scores: -1.19 (95% CI -1.52 to -0.86)
  - Minimal side effects compared to placebo

(Pandey et al., 2002)

In a subsequent comparative trial, gabapentin produced significantly lower median pain scores across all 7 treatment days versus both placebo and carbamazepine (P <0.05) (Pandey et al., 2005).

**Pregabalin** is used as an alternative gabapentinoid, though with less specific trial evidence in GBS. Dosing typically starts at 75 mg BID, titrated to 150-300 mg BID.

#### Second-Line: Carbamazepine

- **Dosing:** 100 mg three times daily
- Median pain scores significantly lower than placebo from day 4 onward (P <0.05)
- Fentanyl consumption reduction: -176 mcg (95% CI -202 to -150 mcg)
- **Limitations:** Significant drug interactions, liver enzyme induction, potential bone marrow suppression
- Not preferred first-line due to drug interaction profile

(Pandey et al., 2005)

#### Tricyclic Antidepressants (Amitriptyline, Nortriptyline)

- Can be effective for neuropathic pain
- **Use with caution in dysautonomic patients** -- can worsen autonomic instability, cause urinary retention, and exacerbate ileus
- Anticholinergic effects may be problematic in ventilated patients
- May have additional benefit for concurrent depression

(Weiss et al., 2025)

#### Opioids

- Acceptable but require caution in GBS patients due to:
  - **Ileus risk** (already high from autonomic dysfunction and immobility)
  - Respiratory depression concerns (relevant during weaning)
  - Potential to mask clinical deterioration
- Use **scheduled acetaminophen** (1 g every 6 hours, with monitoring for liver enzyme elevation as GBS may cause mild hepatic cytolysis) as a baseline
- If opioids are necessary, monitor bowel function closely and consider prophylactic bowel regimen

(Farkas, EMCrit IBCC; Weiss et al., 2025)

#### Adjunctive Agents

- **Lidocaine 5% patches:** Low risk of adverse effects, may be useful for localized neuropathic pain
- **IV lidocaine infusions:** May be considered in refractory cases
- **NSAIDs:** Useful for musculoskeletal component, but limited role for neuropathic pain
- **Corticosteroids for pain:** Methylprednisolone 500 mg IV daily x 5 days + IVIg was studied but showed no significant benefit for pain (RR 0.89, 95% CI 0.68-1.16) (Ruts et al., 2007)

#### Overall Evidence Quality

A Cochrane review of three RCTs (277 patients) rated the evidence as **"very low" to "low" quality** but noted that gabapentin and carbamazepine did reduce pain severity versus placebo with few side effects. Larger, well-designed studies are needed (Hughes et al., 2016).

### 3.3 Non-Pharmacological Approaches

- Positioning and gentle range-of-motion exercises
- Warm or cool compresses
- TENS (transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation)
- Psychological support and relaxation techniques
- Sleep hygiene optimization
- Minimizing unnecessary stimulation

## 4. Complications in ICU-Bound GBS Patients

Two-thirds of GBS patients in the ICU suffer at least one complication during their stay (Weiss et al., 2025).

### 4.1 Infections

#### Ventilator-Associated Pneumonia (VAP)

- Prevalence: **30-78%** of ventilated GBS patients (highest complication rate)
- Delayed tracheostomy is associated with increased VAP risk (OR 8.2, p=0.029)
- **Prevention strategies:**
  - Standard VAP bundle (head of bed elevation 30-45 degrees, oral care with chlorhexidine, daily sedation interruption, daily assessment of readiness to wean)
  - Chest physiotherapy and assisted cough techniques
  - Air-stacking maneuvers
  - Early tracheostomy may reduce VAP in prolonged ventilation cases
  - Consider antibiotic prophylaxis for nosocomial respiratory infections in mechanically ventilated patients

(Weiss et al., 2025; Cheng et al., 2021)

#### Other Infections

- **Septic shock:** 22-24%
- **UTI:** Common, especially with Foley catheter use; minimize duration of catheterization
- **Central line infections:** Standard line care bundles; remove lines as soon as feasible

### 4.2 DVT/PE Prophylaxis

GBS patients are at **high risk for venous thromboembolism** due to prolonged immobility, paralysis, and potential dehydration. DVT occurs in **5-20%** of GBS patients, and pulmonary embolism affects approximately **7%** of ICU-admitted patients (Weiss et al., 2025).

**Prophylaxis strategies:**

| Modality | Details |
|---|---|
| **Pharmacological (first-line)** | LMWH (e.g., enoxaparin 40 mg SC daily) preferred over UFH. LMWH is probably more effective than UFH in reducing DVT incidence |
| **Alternative pharmacological** | Unfractionated heparin 5000 U SC every 8-12 hours if LMWH contraindicated (renal failure) |
| **Mechanical** | Intermittent pneumatic compression (IPC) devices; graduated compression stockings |
| **Combination** | Pharmacological + mechanical in very high-risk patients |
| **When coagulopathy present** | IPC devices alone until coagulation normalizes; reassess daily for pharmacological prophylaxis |

(Cheng et al., 2021; CHEST VTE prophylaxis guidelines)

**Special consideration for GBS patients receiving plasma exchange:** Plasma exchange can reduce fibrinogen and coagulation factors. If fibrinogen <2 g/L, fresh frozen plasma should be used as replacement fluid. DVT prophylaxis may need temporary interruption during severe coagulopathy, with mechanical prophylaxis maintained (Weiss et al., 2025).

### 4.3 Pressure Injuries

Immobilized, paralyzed GBS patients are at extreme risk for pressure injuries.

**Prevention strategies:**
- **Repositioning every 2 hours** (or more frequently)
- Specialized pressure-relieving surfaces (foam, air, water, or gel mattresses)
- Meticulous skin assessment at each turn
- Maintain adequate hydration and nutrition
- Passive ROM exercises at least twice daily
- Support and properly position paralyzed extremities
- Minimize shear forces during transfers

(GBS-CIDP Foundation, Acute Care Guide; Cheng et al., 2021)

### 4.4 Nutritional Support

GBS patients are at **high risk for malnutrition** throughout their illness due to:
- Dysphagia from bulbar weakness (swallowing difficulties)
- Gastroparesis from autonomic dysfunction
- Increased metabolic demands
- Prolonged ICU stay

**Nutritional management principles:**
- Initiate enteral nutrition early (within 24-48 hours of ICU admission)
- **Post-pyloric feeding** (nasojejunal tube) if gastroparesis prevents gastric feeding
- Parenteral nutrition may be necessary if enteral feeding fails
- **Monitor blood glucose closely:** Hyperglycemia is correlated with poorer outcomes upon ICU discharge in ventilated GBS patients; use insulin protocol as needed
- Assess swallowing function before oral feeding (speech-language pathology evaluation)
- Aggressive caloric support to prevent catabolism

(Weiss et al., 2025; Cheng et al., 2021)

### 4.5 ICU-Acquired Weakness (ICUAW) and Contractures

- GBS patients are doubly vulnerable: disease-related weakness plus ICU-acquired weakness from disuse, critical illness polyneuropathy/myopathy, and corticosteroid exposure
- **Early mobilization and physiotherapy** are essential to prevent contractures
- Proper positioning prevents **equinus varus deformity** (foot drop), which if severe may require tenotomy
- Prevention of **osteoma** (heterotopic ossification) through early mobilization

(Weiss et al., 2025)

### 4.6 Psychological Impact and ICU Delirium

#### Vivid Dreams and Altered Mental States

GBS patients can experience **vivid dreams with depersonalization symptoms** that may be misdiagnosed as ICU delirium. These occur even in patients not yet on mechanical ventilation and may signal impending dysautonomia and more severe illness. If present in non-ventilated patients, consider ICU transfer (Weiss et al., 2025).

#### True Psychiatric Complications

In one ICU-based study of 139 GBS patients (Cochen et al., 2005):
- **60%** experienced hallucinations
- **70%** experienced delusions
- **30%** experienced illusions
- **19%** reported vivid dreams
- Visual hallucinations and paranoid delusions peaked on **day 9** of ICU admission

Additional data:
- **82%** of severely disabled GBS patients experience moderate-to-severe anxiety
- **67%** experience depression
- GBS patients have a **4.8-fold** increased risk of depression compared to the general population
- 10.35% of GBS patients develop psychiatric disorders vs 7.5% in matched controls (HR 4.281, p<0.001)

(Peric et al., 2020; Cochen et al., 2005)

#### PTSD

Post-traumatic stress disorder is particularly prevalent in mechanically ventilated GBS patients requiring prolonged ICU stays and is associated with GBS independently of anxiety and depression (Peric et al., 2020).

### 4.7 Communication Strategies for Locked-In-Like States

GBS patients with severe motor impairment, even those with complete paralysis, **usually have fully intact consciousness, vision, and hearing.** This creates a locked-in-like state that is psychologically devastating.

**Communication strategies:**
- **Communication cards** (designed by the GBS-CIDP Foundation) for basic needs
- **Alphabet boards** and eye-pointing systems
- **Advanced communication interfaces:** Visual tracking devices, subtle movement detectors, neurophysiological signal-based computer control systems
- **Speaking valves** (Passy-Muir) once tracheostomy is in place and appropriate
- **Consistent staff assignment** to improve communication familiarity
- **Always address the patient directly** -- explain all procedures, assume full cognitive awareness
- **Early detection of complications** is enabled when communication tools allow patients to express symptoms

(Weiss et al., 2025; GBS-CIDP Foundation)

### 4.8 Other Complications

| Complication | Prevalence |
|---|---|
| ARDS | 26% |
| Ileus and/or bowel perforation | 17% |
| Gastrointestinal hemorrhage | 7% |
| Acute renal failure | 4% |
| Tracheostomy complications | 4% |
| PRES | 7% (of dysautonomic patients) |
| Hyponatremia | Common |

(Weiss et al., 2025)

## 5. Prognosis in Ventilator-Dependent GBS

### 5.1 Mortality

| Population | Mortality Rate |
|---|---|
| Overall GBS | 3-7% |
| ICU-admitted GBS | ~5% |
| Mechanically ventilated GBS | ~20% (older estimates; Thille 2025: 9.4%, n=513 GBS, 47 ICUs) |
| GBS with dysautonomia | ~6% (3x higher than without) |

Death occurs most commonly from cardiovascular and respiratory complications, which can arise in both the acute and recovery phases (van den Berg et al., 2014; Weiss et al., 2025).

### 5.2 Recovery and Walking

| Outcome Measure | Result |
|---|---|
| Independent ambulation at 6 months (all GBS) | ~80% |
| Independent ambulation at 6 months (ventilated) | ~57% |
| Independent ambulation at 6 months (prolonged MV) | ~18% |
| Eventual independent ambulation (ventilated survivors) | ~79% |
| Unable to walk independently at 1 year (severe ICU cases) | ~16% |
| Improvement beyond 1 year | 19% improve at least one functional grade |

(Fletcher et al., 2000; Walgaard et al., 2017; Weiss et al., 2025)

**Key message:** Even patients with the most severe GBS -- those who are tetraplegic at nadir or require prolonged mechanical ventilation -- show extensive recovery, **especially in the first year** after disease onset. Recovery can continue for 2-5 years (van den Berg et al., 2014).

### 5.3 Prognostic Scoring: mEGOS (Modified Erasmus GBS Outcome Score)

The mEGOS predicts the probability of being unable to walk independently at 4 weeks, 3 months, and 6 months after weakness onset (van Koningsveld et al., 2007; Walgaard et al., 2011).

#### mEGOS Components

**At Hospital Admission (Total Score 0-9):**

| Component | Category | Points |
|---|---|---|
| **Age** | <=40 years | 0 |
| | 41-60 years | 1 |
| | >60 years | 2 |
| **Preceding diarrhea (4 weeks before onset)** | No | 0 |
| | Yes | 1 |
| **MRC sum score** | 51-60 | 0 |
| | 41-50 | 2 |
| | 31-40 | 4 |
| | <=30 | 6 |

**At Day 7 of Admission (Total Score 0-12):**

| Component | Category | Points |
|---|---|---|
| **Age** | <=40 years | 0 |
| | 41-60 years | 1 |
| | >60 years | 2 |
| **Preceding diarrhea** | No | 0 |
| | Yes | 1 |
| **MRC sum score** | 51-60 | 0 |
| | 41-50 | 3 |
| | 31-40 | 6 |
| | <=30 | 9 |

Higher scores indicate worse prognosis (inability to walk independently).

#### Predictive Accuracy (AUC)

| Time Point | At Admission | At Day 7 |
|---|---|---|
| 4 weeks | 0.73 | 0.87 |
| 3 months | 0.73 | 0.84 |
| 6 months | 0.77 | 0.84 |

The day-7 assessment provides substantially better predictive accuracy than admission assessment (Walgaard et al., 2011).

#### Application in Ventilated Patients

Ventilated patients inherently have high mEGOS scores (typically >=6 at admission, >=9 at day 7) due to severe weakness. The mEGOS was validated internationally across 557 patients from 11 countries, confirming its utility across diverse populations (Hokkam et al., 2022).

### 5.4 Key Prognostic Factors

#### Factors Predicting Poor Recovery

- **Most important single predictor:** Abnormal mean amplitude of compound muscle action potential (CMAP) on ENMG (Weiss et al., 2025)
- Older age
- Rapid onset (symptom onset to hospitalization <=7 days)
- Need for mechanical ventilation
- Axonal subtypes (AMAN, AMSAN) or unexcitable nerves on NCS
- Preceding C. jejuni infection (associated with axonal subtypes)
- Severe weakness at nadir (low MRC sum score)
- Preceding diarrhea

#### Factors Predicting Better Recovery

- Younger age
- AIDP (demyelinating) subtype
- Preserved CMAP amplitudes
- Rapid improvement after immunotherapy
- Absence of preceding diarrhea

#### Emerging Biomarkers

Elevated plasma levels of **neurofilament light chains (NfL)** may serve as an early surrogate marker for long-term outcomes, though this requires further validation (Weiss et al., 2025).

### 5.5 NCS Neuroprognostication Guidelines (2023)

The Neurocritical Care Society published evidence-based neuroprognostication guidelines for GBS in 2023, assessing 45 articles across 8 clinical variables and 6 prediction models (Karmel et al., 2023):

**Key recommendations:**
- **Motor weakness at nadir** and **EGRIS** are moderately reliable predictors of mechanical ventilation need
- **EGOS and mEGOS** are moderately reliable predictors of independent ambulation at >=3 months
- **Few predictors** were considered moderately reliable; higher quality data are needed
- **Consider the complete clinical condition** during prognostication, not any single variable alone
- Three critical outcomes assessed: (1) MV need within 14 days, (2) independent ambulation at >=3 months, (3) functional outcome at >=3 months

(Karmel et al., 2023)

### 5.6 Long-Term Outcomes (1-5 Years)

#### Residual Deficits

- **Chronic pain:** ~30% of severe ICU cases at 1 year (Weiss et al., 2025)
- **Residual fatigue:** 38-86% of patients; 70% experience **severe** residual fatigue that persists for years and is independent of measured nerve dysfunction or physical fitness (Peric et al., 2020)
- **Paresthesia and sensory complaints:** Up to 40% long-term
- **Residual motor weakness:** About one-third of patients retain some permanent weakness
- **Psychological sequelae:** Anxiety, depression, and PTSD can persist years after discharge

#### Recovery Timeline

- **Recovery is greatest in the first year** but can continue for 2-5 or more years
- Mean time to clinical recovery: approximately **200 days**
- **19%** of patients improve at least one functional grade beyond 1 year
- Some studies report continued strength improvement beyond 2 years
- Lung function shows steady improvement with near-complete recovery within 2 years in most patients

(van den Berg et al., 2014; Patel & Gwathmey, 2017; Peric et al., 2020)

## 6. Rehabilitation in Severe GBS

### 6.1 When to Start

- **Rehabilitation should begin in the ICU** as soon as the patient is medically stable
- **Early mobilization and appropriate positioning by a physiotherapist** are crucial to prevent contractures and heterotopic ossification
- Early involvement of allied health staff (physiotherapy, occupational therapy, speech-language pathology) is recommended
- About **40%** of hospitalized GBS patients require inpatient rehabilitation

(Weiss et al., 2025; Khan et al., 2011)

### 6.2 ICU-Based Physiotherapy

#### Goals During Acute Phase

- **Prevent contractures** through passive range-of-motion exercises (at least twice daily)
- **Prevent pressure injuries** through positioning
- **Prevent DVT** through passive movements and positioning
- **Respiratory physiotherapy:** Chest percussion, secretion mobilization, assisted cough techniques
- **Proper positioning** to prevent equinus varus deformity (foot drop) and shoulder subluxation

#### Early Mobilization Protocol

- Active, assisted, or passive limb mobilization
- Cycle ergometry when feasible
- Progressive transfers to sitting and standing
- Sessions: typically 30 minutes twice daily (1 hour total per day)
- May begin within 24 hours of ICU admission for appropriate patients

(Weiss et al., 2025; Cheng et al., 2021)

### 6.3 Respiratory Rehabilitation

#### Inspiratory Muscle Training (IMT)

- Can commence once mechanically ventilated for at least **7 days**
- **Protocol:** Removable threshold device, 5 sets of 6 breaths at a minimum of 50% of maximum inspiratory pressure (MIP), once per day supervised by physiotherapist
- Intensity increased daily based on measured MIP
- IMT combined with comprehensive rehabilitation reduces MV duration by approximately **3.2 days**

(Bissett et al., 2019; Elkins & Dentice, 2015)

#### Additional Respiratory Techniques

- Incentive spirometry (once patient can cooperate)
- Air-stacking maneuvers
- Assisted cough techniques
- Chest physiotherapy and postural drainage
- Progressive ventilator-free breathing trials
- NIV as a weaning bridge (59% of successfully weaned patients used NIV)

### 6.4 Recovery Phases and Rehabilitation Approach

#### Acute Phase (Weeks 1-4)

- **Focus:** Prevention of complications, respiratory support, passive exercises
- Positioning, skin care, communication support
- Psychological support for patient and family
- Pain management

#### Plateau Phase (Variable: Days to Weeks)

- **Focus:** Maintaining function, gentle progressive mobilization
- Begin active-assisted exercises as strength returns
- Continue respiratory therapy
- Assess and manage swallowing function

#### Recovery/Descending Phase (Weeks to Months)

- **Focus:** Progressive strengthening, functional retraining
- Increased upright posturing and weight-bearing
- High-intensity rehabilitation utilizing active resistance training
- Neuromuscular facilitation techniques
- Transfer training, gait retraining
- Activities of daily living (ADL) retraining

#### Long-Term Recovery (Months to Years)

- Multidisciplinary ambulatory rehabilitation beneficial even up to 12 months from onset
- Tailored exercise programs reduce fatigue by approximately **20%** (independent of fitness gains)
- Continued physiotherapy, occupational therapy, psychological support
- Vocational rehabilitation and return-to-work planning

(Khan et al., 2011; Peric et al., 2020; Weiss et al., 2025)

### 6.5 Occupational Therapy

- **ADL retraining:** Dressing, eating, grooming, toileting
- **Assistive device prescription:** Splints, adaptive equipment
- **Upper extremity strengthening** and fine motor retraining
- **Energy conservation techniques** (particularly important given GBS fatigue)
- **Home modification assessment** for discharge planning
- **Cognitive and psychological support**

### 6.6 Recovery Milestones (Typical Timeline for Severe GBS)

| Approximate Timeline | Milestone |
|---|---|
| Weeks 2-4 | Nadir of weakness reached |
| Weeks 4-8 | Plateau phase; may begin to see flickers of movement |
| Months 2-4 | Weaning from ventilator (if applicable); beginning active exercises |
| Months 3-6 | Transfer to rehabilitation facility; progressive strengthening |
| Months 6-12 | Major functional gains; many achieve assisted/independent ambulation |
| Year 1-2 | Continued improvement; most recovery occurs in this window |
| Years 2-5 | Slower incremental gains possible; fatigue management; long-term adaptation |

**Note:** These are general estimates. Individual recovery varies enormously based on subtype, severity, age, and comorbidities.

### 6.7 Patient and Family Support Resources

- **GBS-CIDP Foundation International** (www.gbs-cidp.org): The primary patient advocacy organization
  - Local chapters worldwide for peer support
  - Communication cards for ventilated patients
  - Educational booklets for patients, families, and caregivers
  - Survivor visits to hospitalized patients
  - Caregiver handbook (comprehensive 70-page guide)
  - Telephone and online support networks
- **National Organization for Rare Disorders (NORD)**
- **Christopher Reeve Foundation** (paralysis-related resources)
- **Local rehabilitation support groups**

## 7. Communication with Families of Severely Ill GBS Patients

### 7.1 What Prognostic Information to Share

Communication with families of severely ill GBS patients requires a careful balance of honesty and hope. The following framework is recommended:

#### Share Early and Clearly

- GBS is an **immune-mediated, potentially reversible** condition -- fundamentally different from degenerative neurological diseases
- The disease follows a predictable pattern: **progression -> plateau -> recovery**
- Even the most severely affected patients -- those who are tetraplegic, ventilator-dependent, or unable to communicate -- **typically have intact consciousness and cognition**
- Treatment with IVIg or plasma exchange can shorten the course and improve outcomes

#### Provide Realistic Timelines

- Be clear that recovery from severe GBS is measured in **months to years**, not days to weeks
- The median duration of mechanical ventilation is approximately **4 weeks**, though some patients require months
- Most recovery occurs in the **first 12 months** but can continue for up to 5 years
- The road to recovery is rarely linear -- expect setbacks and plateaus

### 7.2 Balancing Hope and Realistic Expectations

#### Grounds for Hope

- **79% of ventilated survivors** eventually regain independent ambulation
- Even patients with prolonged ventilation (months) can achieve meaningful recovery
- **GBS is generally recoverable** -- this distinguishes it from many other causes of ICU-level paralysis
- **Only 50%** achieve independent walking at 6 months with current standard of care, but recovery continues well beyond this point
- The 2025 review by Weiss et al. emphasizes that "most patients with GBS, even those who were tetraplegic at nadir or required mechanical ventilation for a long period of time, show extensive recovery"

#### Realistic Challenges to Acknowledge

- **16% of severe ICU cases** remain unable to walk independently at 1 year
- **~30%** experience chronic pain long-term
- **70%** experience severe residual fatigue
- **Mortality in ventilated patients is approximately 20%**
- Psychological sequelae (anxiety, depression, PTSD) are common and can persist
- About one-third of patients retain some permanent weakness

### 7.3 Key Messages for Family Communication

1. **"GBS is a recoverable disease."** The immune system attacks the nerves, but once the attack is stopped and the body begins to heal, nerves can regenerate. This is fundamentally different from conditions where nerve or brain tissue is permanently destroyed.

2. **"Your loved one is aware."** Unless there is a separate neurological issue, GBS patients retain full consciousness, hearing, and vision. Speak to them, explain what is happening, provide reassurance. They understand everything.

3. **"Recovery takes time."** Nerves regenerate at approximately 1 mm per day (about 1 inch per month). For severely affected patients, this means months to years of recovery. Patience is essential.

4. **"There will be setbacks."** Recovery is not linear. Bad days do not mean the disease is worsening. Treatment-related fluctuations occur in about 10% of patients.

5. **"Rehabilitation is crucial."** Early and sustained rehabilitation makes a significant difference in outcomes. The family's role in encouraging and supporting rehabilitation is invaluable.

6. **"Take care of yourselves."** 72% of close relatives experience social dysfunction, psychological distress, and problems with daily activities. Family caregiver well-being directly affects patient outcomes.

### 7.4 Supporting Families

- **Early and ongoing psychiatric screening** for both patients and family members
- **Psychoeducation** about the disease course, expected recovery, and psychological impacts
- **Connect with the GBS-CIDP Foundation** for peer support and survivor visits
- **Involve social work** for financial counseling, leave-of-absence assistance, and care coordination
- **Family meetings** with the multidisciplinary team at regular intervals (at least weekly in ICU)
- **Allow and encourage family presence** at the bedside when possible
- **Facilitate communication** between patient and family using appropriate tools
- **Prepare families for the long road ahead** -- discharge planning should begin early, addressing rehabilitation placement, home modifications, and long-term support needs

### 7.5 Special Considerations in Prognostication

- The NCS 2023 guidelines recommend considering **the complete clinical condition** rather than any single variable during prognostication
- **Avoid premature prognostic statements** in the first 1-2 weeks, when the disease may still be progressing
- **Day 7 mEGOS** provides better predictive accuracy than admission scores
- **Electrophysiology results** (NCS/EMG) at 2+ weeks provide important prognostic information about subtype and axonal involvement
- **Share prognostic information in stages** as more clinical data becomes available
- **Never communicate hopelessness** -- even patients with the worst prognostic profiles can achieve meaningful recovery

## Summary of Key Points for the Intensivist

1. **Ventilation:** Standard modes apply; no GBS-specific mode is superior. Monitor FVC, MIP, MEP closely. Avoid succinylcholine. Median MV duration is ~28 days.

2. **Weaning:** Begin after immunotherapy completion. Target FVC >15 mL/kg, MIP >-20 cmH2O. Diaphragm may recover before limbs. Extubate to BiPAP/HFNC.

3. **Tracheostomy:** Consider after 2 weeks of failed weaning. Early tracheostomy (within 7 days) shows no mortality benefit. Benefits include comfort, communication, and mobilization.

4. **Dysautonomia:** Present in 75% of ventilated patients. Conservative management preferred. Keep atropine at bedside. Avoid beta-blockers. Do not over-treat labile blood pressure.

5. **Pain:** Affects up to 89% of patients. Gabapentin 15 mg/kg/day is first-line. Use opioids cautiously (ileus risk). Address neuropathic, musculoskeletal, and visceral components.

6. **Complications:** Anticipate VAP (30-78%), DVT (5-20%), pressure injuries, ileus, and psychological distress. Prophylax aggressively.

7. **Prognosis:** 79% of ventilated survivors walk again. Use mEGOS for prognostication (day 7 is more accurate). Recovery continues for years.

8. **Rehabilitation:** Start in the ICU. Early mobilization, respiratory physiotherapy, and IMT improve outcomes. Continue rehabilitation up to 12 months or longer.

9. **Families:** GBS is recoverable. Communicate hope balanced with realism. Connect families with support resources. Screen for caregiver psychological distress.

10. **Psychological care:** 60-70% of ICU patients experience hallucinations or delusions. Distinguish vivid dreams (GBS-related) from delirium. Screen for PTSD, anxiety, and depression.
