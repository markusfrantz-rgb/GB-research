## 1. Pathophysiology

### 1.1 Overview

Selective IgA deficiency (SIgAD) is a heterogeneous disorder characterized by a maturation defect in B lymphocytes that prevents their terminal differentiation into IgA-secreting plasma cells. Although B cells in SIgAD patients may express surface IgA markers, they retain an immature phenotype with co-expression of IgM and IgD and fail to undergo the final maturation step required for IgA secretion [1, 2, 3].

### 1.2 B-Cell Differentiation Defects

The central pathogenic mechanism involves a block in B-cell class-switch recombination (CSR) and/or terminal differentiation to IgA-producing plasma cells. Key findings include:

- **IgA memory B-cell deficiency**: Both CD27+ (T-cell-dependent) and CD27- (T-cell-independent) IgA memory B cells are significantly reduced or undetectable in symptomatic patients. In a study of 30 SIgAD patients, IgA memory B cells were undetectable in 11 of 14 adult patients [4].

- **B-cell extrinsic defects**: The impairment in IgA memory formation is not due to an intrinsic B-cell activation defect but rather reflects B-cell extrinsic immune dysfunction involving altered T-helper cell subsets and aberrant cytokine signaling [4].

- **T-cell abnormalities**: Adult SIgAD patients exhibit decreased Th1 and Th17 cell numbers, along with elevated CD8+ central memory T cells, suggesting broader immune dysregulation beyond B cells [4].

- **Cytokine dysregulation**: Deficiencies or dysregulation of IL-4, IL-6, IL-7, IL-10, IL-21, and TGF-beta have been documented. Paradoxically, adult SIgAD patients show *increased* TGF-beta1, BAFF, and APRIL blood levels, suggesting disturbed immune regulation rather than simple class-switch deficiency [2, 4].

- **IL-21 as a potential therapeutic target**: IL-21 stimulation has been shown to induce class-switch recombination to IgA and differentiation of IgA- and IgG-secreting plasma cells with restoration of immunoglobulin production ex vivo [2].

- **Increased apoptosis**: Enhanced apoptosis during immune responses contributes to reduced survival, growth, and differentiation of B cells and their inability to produce normal IgA levels [3].

### 1.3 Genetic Basis

SIgAD demonstrates complex, polygenic inheritance with autosomal recessive, autosomal dominant, and sporadic transmission patterns, suggesting multiple genetic abnormalities rather than a single primary defect [2].

#### 1.3.1 Major Histocompatibility Complex (MHC) Associations

The strongest genetic association is with the MHC region on chromosome 6p21:

- **The 8.1 ancestral haplotype (HLA-A1, B8, DR3, DQ2)**: Present in approximately 45% of SIgAD patients versus 16% of the general population. This haplotype confers risk in a multiplicative manner--homozygosity substantially increases disease risk [2, 5, 6].

- **HLA-DQ beta chain position 57**: An amino acid substitution at this position has been associated with susceptibility to IgA deficiency. This position is also critical for celiac disease and type 1 diabetes susceptibility [2, 7].

- **Protective haplotypes**: The HLA-DR15, DQ6 haplotype confers almost complete protection against IgAD. The DRB1*1501 allele protects against both IgAD and type 1 diabetes [5, 6].

- **Fine mapping at IGAD1**: Genome-wide genetic linkage analysis implicates HLA-DQ/DR as a major susceptibility locus in both SIgAD and CVID [7].

#### 1.3.2 Non-MHC Genetic Loci (GWAS Findings)

A landmark GWAS meta-analysis of 1,635 SIgAD patients and 4,852 controls identified four novel genome-wide significant loci [8]:

| Locus | Peak Variant | P-value | OR | Associated Autoimmune Diseases |
|-------|-------------|---------|-----|-------------------------------|
| **PVT1** (8q24) | rs11299600 | 4.3 x 10^-11 | 0.73 (protective) | Rheumatoid arthritis |
| **ATG13-AMBRA1** (11p11.2) | rs4565870 | 6.7 x 10^-10 | 1.38 | Autophagy pathway |
| **AHI1** (6q23.3) | rs7773987 | 8.4 x 10^-10 | 1.30 | Multiple sclerosis |
| **CLEC16A** (16p13.13) | rs34069391 | 1.4 x 10^-9 | 0.71 (protective) | Type 1 diabetes, primary biliary cirrhosis |

Additional confirmed associations:
- **IFIH1** (encoding MDA5, a cytosolic dsRNA sensor): Thr946Ala variant, P = 3.7 x 10^-15, OR = 0.70; rare loss-of-function variant Ile923Val, P = 2.6 x 10^-8 [8, 9].

**Genome-wide heritability** was estimated at 0.49 (SE = 0.047), decreasing to 0.39 when conditioning on the five peak non-MHC variants and MHC variants [8].

**Pathway analysis** revealed striking association with the KEGG intestinal immune network for IgA production pathway (empirical P < 0.0001), with 22 of 30 annotated pathway genes containing at least one variant with P <= 0.05. Key genes included IL-2, IL-4, IL-6, IL-10, IL-15, APRIL, BAFF, CD40, CD28, ICOS, ICOSLG, and AICDA [8].

#### 1.3.3 TACI (TNFRSF13B) Mutations

TACI mutations represent the most well-characterized monogenic contributor to both SIgAD and CVID:

- Mutations in TNFRSF13B have been found in approximately 5% of SIgAD patients and 8-10% of CVID patients [10, 11].
- B cells from individuals with TACI mutations express TACI but fail to produce IgG and IgA in response to the TACI ligand APRIL, reflecting impaired isotype switching [10].
- TACI mutations induce disease *susceptibility* rather than directly causing disease, suggesting that additional genetic or environmental cofactors determine whether SIgAD or CVID develops [10, 12].
- One family investigation identified the heterozygous C104R TNFRSF13B mutation in children with CVID and their mother who had selective IgA deficiency, demonstrating the spectrum nature of these conditions [10].

#### 1.3.4 Other Genetic Associations

- Chromosome 18p deletions have been found in some patients [1].
- Mutations in JAK3, RAG1, RAG2, CXCR4, and STAT1 can cause abnormal cytokine signaling affecting immunoglobulin switching [1].

### 1.4 Relationship to the CVID Spectrum

SIgAD and common variable immunodeficiency (CVID) represent a continuum of humoral immunodeficiency:

- **Shared genetic susceptibility**: Both conditions share the IGAD1 locus at HLA-DQ/DR, TACI mutations, and other genetic risk factors [2, 7, 10].
- **Familial clustering**: Approximately 20% of CVID patients have a first-degree relative with SIgAD [10].
- **Shared cytotoxic T lymphocyte-associated protein-4 (CTLA-4) and inducible costimulator (ICOS) risk loci** have been identified in both conditions [2].
- **B-cell immunophenotyping**: SIgAD patients with reduced switched memory B cells (<0.4%, classified as Group I) demonstrate higher rates of pneumonia, autoimmunity, and hepatosplenomegaly -- features reminiscent of CVID [1].
- **Disease progression**: SIgAD may progress to CVID (see Section 2.3) [2, 13].

### 1.5 Compensatory Mechanisms

- **Secretory IgM upregulation**: Most IgA-deficient patients demonstrate increased production of secretory IgM as a partial compensatory mechanism at mucosal surfaces, though some patients lack this compensation [2].
- **Compensatory IgE elevation**: IgE levels may increase, contributing to the elevated prevalence of atopic disease observed in SIgAD [1, 14].
- **Gut microbiome disruption**: The absence of secretory IgA leads to distorted gut microflora; the compensatory IgM response only partially restores microbial populations [1].

## 2. Epidemiology

### 2.1 Prevalence by Ethnicity

SIgAD is the most common primary immunodeficiency disease worldwide. Prevalence varies dramatically by ethnic background:

| Population | Prevalence | Reference |
|-----------|-----------|-----------|
| **Arabian Peninsula** | 1:142 - 1:143 | [2, 3] |
| **Spain** | 1:155 - 1:163 | [5, 6] |
| **Caucasian (general)** | 1:142 - 1:600 | [1, 5, 6] |
| **Brazil** | 1:965 | [15] |
| **England** | 1:875 | [2] |
| **United States** | 1:223 - 1:1000 | [1, 3] |
| **China (Han)** | 1:2,600 | [16] |
| **China (Mongolia ethnic)** | 1:2,700 | [16] |
| **China (overall)** | 1:2,600 - 1:5,300 | [16] |
| **China (Zhuang ethnic)** | 1:5,300 | [16] |
| **Japan** | 1:14,840 - 1:18,550 | [17] |

- Gender distribution is equal [1, 3].
- Familial clustering is significant: 38-fold higher prevalence in first-degree relatives, with approximately 20% inheritance risk [1].

### 2.2 Natural History

The natural history of SIgAD has been characterized in a longitudinal pediatric cohort study (n=51, mean follow-up 10 years) [15]:

- **Disease is usually lifelong**, though spontaneous normalization can occur.
- **Partial IgA recovery**: 9 patients (17.6%) progressed to partial IgA deficiency at an average age of 10.6 years (range 6-14 years).
- **Full normalization**: 4 of these 9 patients eventually normalized IgA levels by an average age of 13 years (range 9-15 years).
- **Clinical manifestations timeline**:
  - Infections: earliest onset (median 1 year of age)
  - Allergies: secondary onset (median 5 years)
  - Autoimmunity: latest onset (median 8 years)
- **Asymptomatic cases**: 85-90% of IgA-deficient individuals are asymptomatic and remain undetected except through coincidental screening [2].

### 2.3 Progression to CVID

SIgAD may evolve into CVID, typically during adolescence or young adulthood:

- In a large pediatric cohort, 2 patients (3.9%) progressed to CVID at ages 12 and 18 years [15].
- **Risk factors for progression** include:
  - Associated IgG subclass deficiency
  - Autoimmune manifestations
  - Reduced switched memory B cells
- All symptomatic SIgAD patients should be monitored for evolution to CVID [13].
- Regular follow-up every 4-6 months is recommended [1].

**Key reference for progression**: Aghamohammadi A, Mohammadi J, Parvaneh N, et al. Progression of selective IgA deficiency to common variable immunodeficiency. *Int Arch Allergy Immunol*. 2008;147(2):87-92. DOI: 10.1159/000135694 [13].

## 3. Diagnosis

### 3.1 ESID Diagnostic Criteria

The European Society for Immunodeficiencies (ESID) recognizes two diagnostic categories [1, 3, 18]:

#### Definitive SIgAD
All of the following criteria must be met:
1. Age > 4 years at diagnosis
2. Serum IgA < 0.07 g/L (measured by nephelometry, confirmed on at least two occasions)
3. Normal serum IgG and IgM levels
4. Normal IgG antibody response to vaccinations
5. Exclusion of secondary causes of hypogammaglobulinemia
6. Exclusion of T-cell defects
7. Increased susceptibility to infection, autoimmune manifestations, and/or affected family member

#### Probable (Partial) IgA Deficiency
- Male or female patient > 4 years of age
- Serum IgA at least 2 standard deviations below normal for age but above 0.07 g/L
- Normal serum IgG and IgM
- Other causes of hypogammaglobulinemia excluded

### 3.2 Age Considerations

- **The 4-year age threshold** is critical: IgA is the last immunoglobulin to reach adult levels, and the IgA system may not fully mature until age 4 or later. Premature diagnosis before this age risks labeling transient physiological hypogammaglobulinemia as a permanent deficiency [1, 3, 18].
- In children younger than 4, consider transient developmental delay of IgA maturation.
- Serum IgA reaches adult levels by approximately age 6-10 years in most children.

### 3.3 Diagnostic Evaluation

**Laboratory testing**:
- Quantitative immunoglobulins: IgG, IgA, IgM (repeat at least once for confirmation)
- IgG subclasses (to identify concomitant IgG subclass deficiency)
- Specific antibody responses to protein and polysaccharide vaccines
- Anti-IgA antibodies (baseline and periodic monitoring)
- Complete blood count with differential
- Lymphocyte subsets (to exclude T-cell defects)

**Specialized immunophenotyping**:
- Switched memory B cells (class-switched CD27+ B cells): Values < 0.4% identify Group I patients at higher risk for complications [1].

**Additional evaluations**:
- Chest X-ray in patients > 45 years (to exclude thymoma)
- Pulmonary function tests for respiratory monitoring
- Jejunal biopsy for recurrent diarrhea (may show villous blunting, absence of IgA-secreting cells, increased IgM-secretory cells)
- Total IgA should always be checked when ordering celiac disease serology (see Section 3.5) [1]

### 3.4 Differential Diagnosis

| Condition | Key Distinguishing Features |
|-----------|----------------------------|
| **Common Variable Immunodeficiency (CVID)** | Low IgG AND IgA (IgM reduced in ~50%); impaired vaccine responses |
| **X-Linked Hyper-IgM Syndrome** | Only IgM elevated; absent IgG, IgA, IgE |
| **Ataxia-Telangiectasia** | Low IgA, IgG, IgE; cerebellar ataxia; telangiectasias; elevated AFP |
| **Thymoma/Good Syndrome** | Low/absent B cells; CD4+ T-cell decrease; thymoma on imaging |
| **IgG Subclass Deficiency** | Isolated IgG subclass reduction with normal total IgG and IgA |
| **Drug-Induced Hypogammaglobulinemia** | Temporal relationship with offending medication (see Section 3.5) |
| **Chronic Lymphocytic Leukemia/Lymphoma** | Abnormal lymphocyte counts; secondary hypogammaglobulinemia |
| **Transient Hypogammaglobulinemia of Infancy** | Age < 4 years; spontaneous resolution |

### 3.5 Secondary (Drug-Induced and Acquired) Causes

Secondary IgA deficiency may be reversible upon removal of the cause [1, 19, 20]:

**Medications**:
- **Antiepileptics**: Phenytoin (IgA deficiency in 29% of treated epileptic patients), valproic acid (IgA deficiency in 50% of users vs. 16% of non-users), carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine [19, 20]
- **Immunosuppressants**: Cyclosporine
- **Antirheumatic agents**: Gold, penicillamine, sulfasalazine
- **Antihypertensives**: ACE inhibitors

**Viral infections**: Hepatitis C virus, Epstein-Barr virus, congenital rubella (may cause persistent effects) [1].

**Reversibility**: Drug-induced IgA deficiency is typically reversible. Mean serum IgA concentrations increase significantly after drug discontinuation or switching to alternative agents [20].

### 3.6 Celiac Disease Screening Considerations

IgA deficiency is 10-15 times more common in celiac disease patients than in the general population. Standard celiac serology (IgA anti-tissue transglutaminase [tTG], IgA endomysial antibodies) will produce **false-negative results** in IgA-deficient patients [21, 22].

**Recommended approach**:
1. Always measure total serum IgA when ordering celiac screening
2. If serum IgA < 0.2 g/L, use IgG-based testing:
   - IgG anti-deamidated gliadin peptide (DGP) antibodies (recommended)
   - IgG anti-tissue transglutaminase antibodies
3. IgG-tTG with recombinant human tTG is a good alternative to IgG endomysial antibody (IgG-EmA) detection [21, 22]

## 4. Clinical Manifestations

### 4.1 Asymptomatic Cases

The majority of SIgAD patients (85-90%) remain asymptomatic throughout life and are detected only through incidental laboratory screening [2]. Compensatory mechanisms, particularly increased secretory IgM production at mucosal surfaces, likely explain the benign course in most patients [2].

### 4.2 Recurrent Infections

Infections are the most common clinical manifestation when symptoms occur [2, 3]:

#### 4.2.1 Sinopulmonary Infections
- **Most common clinical presentation** in symptomatic patients
- **Principal pathogens**: *Streptococcus pneumoniae* and *Haemophilus influenzae* (encapsulated bacteria)
- Manifests as recurrent sinusitis, otitis media, bronchitis, and pneumonia
- **Pneumonia** was documented in 94% of a symptomatic cohort [15]
- Severity increases with concomitant IgG2 and/or IgG3 subclass deficiency [1, 2]
- Chronic or recurrent infections may lead to **bronchiectasis** and **obliterative bronchiolitis** ("popcorn lung") -- irreversible complications [1]

#### 4.2.2 Gastrointestinal Infections
- **Giardia lamblia** infection with chronic diarrhea is characteristic [1, 2]
- Other GI manifestations include:
  - Malabsorption and lactose intolerance
  - Nodular lymphoid hyperplasia
  - Increased susceptibility to enteric infections due to absent mucosal IgA barrier

### 4.3 Autoimmune Associations

Autoimmune diseases represent the second most common clinical association after infections, affecting **20-30% of symptomatic patients** [2, 3]. Autoimmunity tends to appear later than infections (median onset age 8 years vs. 1 year for infections) [15]. See Section 7 for comprehensive mapping.

### 4.4 Allergic Diseases

Allergic manifestations are reported in **25-50% (or up to 84% in some series) of SIgAD patients** [2, 14]:

- **Asthma**: Most frequently described allergic manifestation
- **Allergic rhinoconjunctivitis**
- **Atopic dermatitis/eczema**
- **Urticaria**
- **Food allergies**: Increased risk due to enhanced intestinal mucosal permeability and allergen absorption
- **Drug allergies**

**Allergies as presenting feature**: Allergic disease is the presenting complaint in up to 40% of SIgAD cases [14].

#### Mechanism of Increased Allergy Risk

1. **Compensatory IgE elevation**: IgE concentration increases to compensate for low IgA, directly driving atopic responses [1, 14]
2. **Increased allergen penetration**: Defective mucosal IgA barrier permits greater allergen contact with the mucosal immune system [14]
3. **Loss of IgA-mediated tolerance**: Secretory IgA normally binds allergens and clears them without activating complement or inflammatory responses. Its absence removes this anti-inflammatory mechanism [5, 14]
4. **Enhanced food sensitization**: Decreased secretory IgA in the gut predisposes to increased intestinal permeability and absorption of ingested antigens [14]

### 4.5 Malignancy Risk

The relationship between SIgAD and malignancy is modest but clinically relevant:

- **Overall cancer risk**: In a combined Danish-Swedish study of 386 IgA-deficient patients, overall cancer incidence was not significantly increased (SIR = 1.0, 95% CI 0.5-1.7) [23].
- **Gastric adenocarcinoma**: Elevated risk (SIR = 5.4, 95% CI 0.7-19.5 -- nonsignificant but notable trend). The proposed mechanism involves compromised mucosal defense against *Helicobacter pylori* due to absent secretory IgA [1, 23].
- **Lymphoma**: Slightly increased but nonsignificant risk (SIR = 2.1 for lymphomas; SIR = 2.6 for non-Hodgkin lymphoma) [23].
- **Nodular lymphoid hyperplasia (NLH)**: Associated with SIgAD and may be a precursor to lymphoma [1].
- **Gastrointestinal malignancies**: An overall increased risk for GI cancers was found in IgA-deficient relatives [23].
- **Neoplasms** were documented in 5.8% of a symptomatic pediatric cohort followed longitudinally [15].

**Key citation**: Mellemkjaer L, et al. Cancer risk among patients with IgA deficiency or common variable immunodeficiency and their relatives: a combined Danish and Swedish study. *Clin Exp Immunol*. 2002;130(3):495-500. DOI: 10.1046/j.1365-2249.2002.02004.x [23].

## 5. Treatment and Management

### 5.1 General Principles

There is no cure or specific corrective therapy for SIgAD. Management is individualized and comprises [1, 2, 3]:

1. Patient education and counseling
2. Periodic monitoring (every 4-6 months)
3. Prevention and treatment of infections
4. Management of associated autoimmune conditions
5. Management of allergic diseases
6. Cautious use of blood products
7. Immunoglobulin replacement therapy (rare, specific indications only)

### 5.2 Management of Infections

#### 5.2.1 Antibiotic Therapy
- **Empiric antibiotic therapy** directed toward *Streptococcus pneumoniae* and *Haemophilus influenzae* for acute infections [1, 2]
- **Prophylactic antibiotics**: Daily prophylaxis on a continuous or seasonal intermittent basis may be beneficial in patients with recurrent infections [2]

#### 5.2.2 Vaccination
- **Polyvalent pneumococcal vaccine** and **influenza vaccine**: Recommended for all patients [1]
- **Contraindicated vaccines**: Live/attenuated vaccines including oral polio (OPV), BCG, and yellow fever should be avoided [1]
- Vaccine responses should be assessed to rule out functional antibody deficiency

#### 5.2.3 Giardiasis
- Metronidazole is the treatment of choice for *Giardia lamblia* infection [2]

### 5.3 Autoimmune Complications Management

- Autoimmune conditions are treated using the same standard-of-care protocols as in immunocompetent patients [2]
- Conditions may include:
  - Immune thrombocytopenic purpura (ITP): Corticosteroids, IVIg (with caution), rituximab
  - Autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA): Corticosteroids, rituximab
  - Celiac disease: Strict gluten-free diet
  - Thyroid disorders: Standard endocrine management
  - Systemic lupus erythematosus: Immunosuppressive therapy as indicated
- **Monitoring for autoantibodies**: Autoantibodies without clinical disease were detected in 60.7% of a pediatric cohort (median detection age 9 years), warranting vigilance for future autoimmune manifestations [15]

### 5.4 Asymptomatic Patients

- No treatment required for patients diagnosed coincidentally without symptoms [2]
- Regular monitoring is still recommended due to potential for:
  - Development of symptoms over time
  - Progression to CVID
  - Development of autoimmune disease
  - Emergence of anti-IgA antibodies [1]

### 5.5 IgG Replacement Therapy Considerations

#### 5.5.1 Indications
Immunoglobulin replacement therapy is **NOT indicated** for isolated SIgAD unless there is:
- Concomitant IgG subclass deficiency (particularly IgG2)
- Demonstrable defect in specific antibody production (functional antibody deficiency)
- Recurrent severe infections despite prophylactic antibiotics [1, 2, 24]

#### 5.5.2 Dosing
When indicated:
- **IV route**: 400-600 mg/kg every 3-4 weeks
- **SC route**: Equivalent dose divided into daily, weekly, or biweekly administrations (using appropriate correction factor)
- **Target**: Trough IgG level of approximately 6-8 g/L [24]

#### 5.5.3 Safety Precautions (see also Section 6)
- Use IgA-depleted or low-IgA products
- Screen for anti-IgA antibodies before initiating therapy
- Subcutaneous immunoglobulin (SCIG) is generally safer than IVIG in IgA-deficient patients [24, 25]
- Have epinephrine and resuscitation equipment immediately available

### 5.6 Dietary and Lifestyle Modifications

- Dietary modifications for food allergies and malabsorption [1]
- Gluten-free diet for patients with concomitant celiac disease
- Medical alert identification (bracelet/card) documenting IgA deficiency and anti-IgA antibody status [1, 26]

## 6. IgA Deficiency and Immunoglobulin Therapy Complications

### 6.1 Anti-IgA Antibodies: Prevalence and Types

#### 6.1.1 Prevalence
- **Overall**: Anti-IgA antibodies are found in approximately **20-40%** of IgA-deficient patients [1, 2, 25]
- Among IgA-deficient patients with **history of anaphylactic transfusion reactions**: 76.3% test positive for anti-IgA antibodies [26]
- Among **asymptomatic** IgA-deficient patients: only 21.7% have anti-IgA antibodies [26]
- In CVID patients: 9-25% have anti-IgA antibodies [25]
- Population-based estimate: Approximately 1 in 1,200-1,600 IgA-deficient patients develop clinically significant anti-IgA antibodies [26]

#### 6.1.2 Types of Anti-IgA Antibodies

| Type | Specificity | Clinical Significance |
|------|-----------|----------------------|
| **IgG anti-IgA (class-specific)** | Binds both IgA1 and IgA2 | Most common; associated with non-IgE mediated anaphylaxis |
| **IgG anti-IgA (subclass-specific)** | Reacts with IgA1, IgA2m(1), or IgA2m(2) only | Less well characterized clinically |
| **IgE anti-IgA** | IgE isotype targeting IgA | Rare but most dangerous; 3 of 4 documented cases experienced anaphylaxis [25] |

#### 6.1.3 Detection Methods
- **ELISA**: Standard method; measures IgG anti-IgA1 and anti-IgA2 at 1:100 serum dilution
- **Luminex-based assay**: Reports results in arbitrary units
- **Passive hemagglutination**: Qualitative detection
- **ImmunoCAP**: For IgE anti-IgA measurement
- **Surface plasmon resonance sensors**: Newer, portable technology allowing rapid identification in approximately one hour [25, 26]

### 6.2 Anaphylaxis Risk with Blood Products Containing IgA

#### 6.2.1 Incidence
- Anaphylactic transfusion reactions due to anti-IgA antibodies: estimated at **1:20,000 to 1:47,000 transfusions** [1]
- Actual incidence of reactions caused by anti-IgA antibodies: approximately **1.3 per million units** of transfused blood products [26]

#### 6.2.2 Mechanism

**IgE-mediated pathway** (classic anaphylaxis):
1. Patients develop IgE anti-IgA antibodies
2. IgE antibodies bind to and sensitize mast cells and basophils
3. Upon exposure to IgA-containing products, cross-linking occurs
4. Degranulation releases histamine and other mediators
5. Results in vasodilation, smooth muscle contraction, and systemic anaphylaxis [26]

**IgG-mediated pathway** (complement-independent):
1. IgG anti-IgA binds to IgA in blood products
2. Immune complexes interact with Fc-gamma receptors on macrophages and basophils
3. Release of platelet-activating factor (PAF) rather than histamine
4. Causes platelet aggregation and vasoconstriction [26]

#### 6.2.3 Clinical Presentation
- **Onset**: Typically within minutes of beginning transfusion
- **Mild reactions**: Fever, chills, urticaria, nausea, flushing
- **Severe reactions**: Pruritus, urticaria, flushing, and diarrhea rapidly followed by shock, hypotension, angioedema, and respiratory distress [26]
- **Biphasic reactions**: Occur in approximately 5% of patients [26]

#### 6.2.4 Implicated Blood Products
All of the following contain IgA and can trigger reactions:
- Whole blood
- Packed red blood cells
- Platelets
- Fresh frozen plasma (FFP)
- Cryoprecipitate
- Granulocytes
- IgA-containing intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) [1, 26]

### 6.3 Protocols for Safe Blood Product Administration

#### 6.3.1 Screening Recommendations
- Universal screening of all patients is NOT necessary [26]
- **Screening IS recommended for**:
  - Patients with known severe SIgAD (IgA < 0.07 g/L)
  - Patients with history of transfusion reactions
  - Before initiating immunoglobulin replacement therapy

#### 6.3.2 Safe Blood Products

| Product | Approach |
|---------|----------|
| **Red blood cells** | Washed RBCs (reduces IgA content); or products from IgA-deficient donors |
| **Platelets** | Washed platelets; or products from IgA-deficient donors |
| **Plasma/FFP** | CANNOT be washed -- must use plasma from IgA-deficient donors |
| **Coagulation support** | Recombinant activated factor VII (rFVIIa) as alternative to FFP/platelets in bleeding |
| **Immunoglobulin** | IgA-depleted IVIG; low-IgA products; or SCIG (see below) |

#### 6.3.3 Patient Precautions
- Inform all healthcare providers of IgA deficiency before any transfusion
- Wear medical alert identification
- Ensure medical records explicitly document the condition
- Request IgA-deficient or washed products when transfusion is needed [1, 26]

#### 6.3.4 Emergency Management of Anaphylaxis
1. **Stop the transfusion immediately**
2. **Epinephrine**: First-line treatment; administer IM; repeat every 5-15 minutes as needed
3. High-flow oxygen (15 L via non-rebreather mask)
4. IV fluid resuscitation (20 mL/kg boluses for children; 1-2 L for adults)
5. Short-acting beta-agonists for bronchospasm
6. Antihistamines (after epinephrine)
7. Vasopressors if unresponsive to initial interventions
8. **Minimum 4 hours of observation** for uncomplicated cases (due to biphasic reaction risk) [26]

### 6.4 IgA-Depleted IVIG Products

#### 6.4.1 Available Products
Low-IgA IVIG products (IgA content < 10 mcg/mL or specified low levels) include:
- **Gammagard S/D** (lyophilized): IgA < 1 mcg/mL
- **Gammaplex**
- **Vigam**
- **Iveegam**
- **Polygam**
- **Nanogam** (IgA < 10 mg/mL)

Note: Product availability and IgA content may vary by manufacturer and region [2, 24].

#### 6.4.2 Evidence for Safety of IgA-Depleted Products
- Long-term infusions of IgA-depleted IVIG, within the range of IgA concentrations investigated, into patients with even very high-titered anti-IgA antibodies have been demonstrated to be safe [27].
- **Gammagard S/D** preparation with premedication (prednisone, diphenhydramine, and acetaminophen orally prior to infusion) was successfully tolerated in a patient with anti-IgA antibodies [28].

#### 6.4.3 Subcutaneous Immunoglobulin (SCIG) as a Safer Alternative
- **SCIG appears safer than IVIG** for IgA-deficient patients with anti-IgA antibodies [24, 25]
- All three patients with positive IgG anti-IgA in one study tolerated SCIG without adverse effects [25]
- One patient who had experienced IVIG anaphylaxis has received SCIG successfully for over 2.5 years [25]
- Mechanism: SCIG enters the bloodstream slowly through lymphatic absorption, reducing the risk of sudden IgA exposure that triggers anaphylaxis

#### 6.4.4 Desensitization Protocols
- For patients who require immunoglobulin therapy and have anti-IgA antibodies, desensitization may be attempted
- Involves exposing patients to a continuous infusion of gradually increasing concentrations of IgA-containing immunoglobulin preparations [26]
- Induction of unresponsiveness against IgA has been documented in IgA-deficient patients on SCIG therapy [29]

## 7. Autoimmune Comorbidities -- Comprehensive Mapping

### 7.1 Overview

Autoimmune diseases are the second most common clinical association with SIgAD, affecting **20-30% of symptomatic patients** [2, 3]. The association is bidirectional: SIgAD is enriched among patients with specific autoimmune diseases, and autoimmune conditions occur more frequently in SIgAD patients than in the general population.

**Hypergammaglobulinemia** (elevated IgG/IgM) is present in approximately 49% of SIgAD patients and is significantly associated with autoimmune diseases and autoantibodies [15].

Autoantibodies without clinical autoimmune disease are detectable in approximately **60.7%** of SIgAD patients, highlighting the need for ongoing surveillance [15].

### 7.2 Comprehensive Disease Mapping

#### 7.2.1 Endocrine Autoimmune Diseases

**Graves Disease (Autoimmune Hyperthyroidism)**
- Prevalence of SIgAD among Graves disease patients: approximately **1:60 to 1:61** (vs. 1:600 in general population) [5, 6]
- Shared HLA associations: 8.1 ancestral haplotype (HLA-B8, DR3, DQ2) [5]
- Shared non-MHC genes: IFIH1, CLEC16A [5, 9]

**Hashimoto Thyroiditis (Autoimmune Hypothyroidism)**
- Elevated prevalence in SIgAD patients [2, 3]
- Thyroiditis was among the most commonly reported autoimmune conditions [2]

**Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus**
- Prevalence of SIgAD among T1D patients: approximately **1:100 to 1:122** (Caucasians); approximately fivefold increase compared to general population [5, 6]
- Swedish cohort: 1:114; Italian cohort: 1:122 [5]
- Shared genetics: 8.1 ancestral haplotype, IFIH1, CLEC16A, PTPN22 [5, 9]

#### 7.2.2 Gastrointestinal Autoimmune Diseases

**Celiac Disease**
- Prevalence of SIgAD among celiac patients: approximately **1:39** (5-15 fold elevation over general population) [5, 6]
- SIgAD is 10-15x more common in celiac patients than in healthy controls [21]
- Shared HLA: HLA-DQ2 (the major celiac risk allele) is part of the 8.1 haplotype that also confers SIgAD risk [5, 30]
- **Clinical implication**: Standard IgA-based celiac serology produces false negatives; use IgG-based assays (see Section 3.6) [21, 22]

**Inflammatory Bowel Disease**
- IBD prevalence in SIgAD: **3.9%** (vs. 0.81% in controls) [31]
  - Crohn's disease: 2.4% (vs. 0.42%) [31]
  - Ulcerative colitis: 1.7% (vs. 0.46%) [31]

**Autoimmune Hepatitis**
- Associated with SIgAD, though precise prevalence is not well defined [31]
- Reported as part of the spectrum of GI autoimmune conditions in SIgAD

**Pernicious Anemia**
- Recognized association, likely due to autoimmune gastritis [31]

#### 7.2.3 Hematologic Autoimmune Diseases

**Immune Thrombocytopenic Purpura (ITP)**
- Most common autoimmune cytopenia in SIgAD patients [2]
- Among the earliest autoimmune conditions to manifest

**Autoimmune Hemolytic Anemia (AIHA)**
- Second most common autoimmune cytopenia after ITP [2]
- May present as warm or cold type

**Evans Syndrome**
- Concurrent ITP and AIHA may occur

#### 7.2.4 Rheumatologic Autoimmune Diseases

**Rheumatoid Arthritis (RA)**
- Prevalence of SIgAD among RA patients: approximately **1:194** [5]

**Juvenile Idiopathic Arthritis (JIA) / Juvenile Rheumatoid Arthritis**
- Notably higher SIgAD prevalence than adult RA: approximately **1:37** [5]
- Among the most commonly reported autoimmune conditions in pediatric SIgAD cohorts [2]

**Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE)**
- Prevalence of SIgAD among SLE patients: **1:67** (Caucasians); **1:121** (Chinese populations) [5, 6]
- Over 30-fold elevation in Chinese SLE patients versus general Chinese population [5]
- Shared genetics: 8.1 haplotype, IFIH1, IRF5, STAT4, PTPN22 [5, 9]

**Ankylosing Spondylitis**
- Elevated prevalence of SIgAD reported [31]

**Scleroderma/Systemic Sclerosis**
- Weaker association reported [31]

#### 7.2.5 Dermatologic Autoimmune Diseases

**Vitiligo**
- Elevated prevalence in SIgAD patients [31]

#### 7.2.6 Neurologic Autoimmune Diseases

**Myasthenia Gravis**
- Prevalence of SIgAD: approximately **1:221** among MG patients (slightly elevated but not markedly increased) [5]

#### 7.2.7 Other Autoimmune Associations

**Autoimmune Cytopenias**: As described above (ITP, AIHA)

**Alopecia Areata**: Reported association

**Sjögren Syndrome**: Reported in some series

### 7.4 Proposed Mechanisms for Autoimmune Associations

#### 7.4.1 Shared Genetic Susceptibility

**MHC-linked mechanisms**:
- The 8.1 ancestral haplotype (HLA-A1, B8, DR3, DQ2) is the strongest shared risk factor, present in 45% of SIgAD patients and strongly associated with Graves disease, SLE, T1D, and celiac disease [5, 6].
- Gene-gene interactions within the MHC amplify risk: e.g., interaction between HLA-DRB1*03 and thyroglobulin variants increases Graves disease risk [5].

**Non-MHC shared genes**:
- **IFIH1** (rs1990760): Associated with SIgAD, T1D, SLE, and Graves disease [5, 9]
- **CLEC16A**: Associated with SIgAD, T1D, SLE, and celiac disease; widely expressed on immune cells [5, 8]
- **PTPN22**, **STAT4**, **CTLA4**, **IRF5**, **TNFAIP3**: Implicated across multiple autoimmune conditions; cluster in pathways for T-cell differentiation, cell activation/signaling, and innate immunity [5]

#### 7.4.2 Immunological Mechanisms

1. **Loss of IgA-mediated immune tolerance**: Serum IgA normally binds antigens and clears them without activating complement, limiting inflammatory responses. IgA deficiency removes this anti-inflammatory mechanism, potentially allowing autoantigen sensitization [5, 6].

2. **Mucosal barrier disruption and molecular mimicry**: Absent mucosal IgA exposes the adaptive immune system to pathogenic and commensal microorganisms, promoting cellular and humoral responses that may cross-react with self-antigens through molecular mimicry [5, 6].

3. **Break of tolerance against IgA itself**: Approximately 30% of SIgAD patients have demonstrable titers of IgG antibodies against IgA, suggesting that SIgAD may involve autoimmune-like loss of tolerance to self-immunoglobulin [5].

4. **Defects in B-cell switching factors (APRIL, BAFF)**: Dysregulation of these TNF superfamily members may simultaneously impair IgA production and promote autoimmune B-cell activation [4, 5].

5. **Regulatory T-cell dysfunction**: GWAS pathway analysis showed significant enrichment of FOXP3+ regulatory T-cell gene signatures among SIgAD risk loci, suggesting that impaired Treg function may contribute to both IgA deficiency and autoimmune susceptibility [8].

6. **Microbiome-immune axis disruption**: Absent secretory IgA leads to altered gut microbiome composition, which may trigger or perpetuate autoimmune responses through altered antigen exposure and modified innate immune signaling [1].

#### 7.4.3 Is SIgAD Itself an Autoimmune Disease?

Some investigators have proposed that SIgAD may itself represent an autoimmune condition rather than purely a primary immunodeficiency, given:
- The high prevalence of anti-IgA autoantibodies (30%)
- The strong HLA associations shared with classic autoimmune diseases
- The autoimmune clustering in patients and families [5]
