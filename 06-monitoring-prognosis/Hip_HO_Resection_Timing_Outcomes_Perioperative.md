## 2. HO AFTER ICU CARE AND GBS — DOES NEUROGENIC HO EVIDENCE APPLY?

Almost all surgical evidence comes from **neurogenic HO (NHO)** after traumatic brain injury (TBI), spinal cord injury (SCI) and stroke. Its applicability here rests on three observations:

| Finding | Source | Level |
|---|---|---|
| HO developed in **4/65 GBS patients (6%)**, all at the hip, all with lasting ROM loss | Zeilig 2006 | 3 |
| In the Garches pre-surgical CT series (101 patients), **21.7% of HO followed "prolonged resuscitation"** (ICU stay without brain/spinal injury) | Law-Ye 2016 | 3 |
| HO in **19% of ventilated COVID-ARDS patients**; risk rose with ventilation time (**OR 2.64 per week**); 9/10 had received neuromuscular blockade | Stoira 2021 | 3 |
| COVID-ICU HO (10 consecutive cases): mostly at the hip, located **away from main arteries**, limited sciatic contact, no severe osteopenia | Mezghani 2022 | 4 |
| Garches team: HO is more frequent with ICU-acquired neuromuscular damage, peripheral nerve damage contributes; manage as NHO; **early surgery feasible** | de l'Escalopier 2021 | 5 |
| HO after ARDS with neuromuscular blockade was self-limited over 1–2 years | Goodman 1997 (n=6) | 4 |
| GBS case reports (Nalbantoglu 2020, Aldhilan 2024, Lachhab 2025, Kerdoncuff 2002 incl. nerve compression; Çelik 2024 bilateral excision + radiotherapy) | — | 4 |
| Among 357 Garches surgical patients, **only one had GBS** | Genêt 2011 | 3 |

> **Interpretation:** ICU-acquired HO and HO after peripheral neuropathy appear to behave like neurogenic HO and are managed as such by the reference centre. The patient's BBE overlap (CNS involvement) brings her somewhat closer to the brain-injury populations. Extrapolation remains inference. *[Level 3–5]*

## 3. WHEN TO OPERATE: THE "MATURITY" RULE REVISITED

### 3.1 Where the rule comes from

The traditional rule — wait until HO is "mature": ~6 months after trauma, 12 months after SCI, 18 months after TBI, confirmed by normalised ALP and a quiescent bone scan — was formulated in older series (Garland 1991). Its rationale was fear of recurrence when excising immature bone.

### 3.2 What the larger modern data show

| Study | Design / n | Finding on timing |
|---|---|---|
| **Chalidis 2007** | Systematic review, 16 studies, n=255 (TBI) | Recurrence 19.8%; **time to excision had no effect** (OR 1.00/month); coma length and neurological deficit predicted recurrence |
| **Genêt 2009** | 183 hips (70 ankylosed) | **All 25 intra-operative femoral neck fractures occurred in ankylosed hips**; loss of ROM matters more than maturity; early surgery limits joint damage without increasing recurrence |
| **Genêt 2011** | 539 surgeries, 357 patients | Recurrence 5.8%, **not associated with delay** (p=0.7); **181 surgeries in the first year — no recurrence** |
| **Genêt 2011b** | Case-control 19 vs 76 | Recurrence not related to delay (p=0.51) or Brooker extent |
| **Genêt 2012** | Case-control 16 vs 64 (TBI) | Delay not related to recurrence (p=0.54) |
| **Almangour 2016** | Systematic review + BANKHO registry | Surgery **< 6 months** not associated with recurrence; operate "as soon as comorbid factors are controlled and the NHO is sufficiently constituted" |
| **Genêt 2015** | SCI | Same conclusion |
| **Law-Ye 2016** | CT, n=101 | Recurrence associated with capsule contact/disruption, joint-space narrowing and **bone demineralisation** (p<0.001) — features of long-standing HO |
| **de l'Escalopier 2019** | Review | Long delay (esp. >2 years with ankylosis) → cartilage loss, femoral-head osteoporosis, fracture risk |

*[Level 3]*

### 3.3 How "maturity" is assessed today

- **Contrast CT** (with 3D reconstruction) is the work-up of choice at the reference centre; "**scintigraphy is non-contributive**" (Denormandie 2018).
- **No validated cut-offs** exist for ALP, bone-specific ALP or osteocalcin (Yuan 2026).
- The practical criterion is that the HO is **"sufficiently constituted"** (corticated, well-demarcated on CT), not a fixed number of months.

### 3.4 Indications

Surgery is indicated when HO is **troublesome**: pain not controllable without surgery, **nerve or vessel compression**, loss of function (sitting, transfers, hygiene), or impending ankylosis (Genêt 2011; Denormandie 2018; de l'Escalopier 2019).

> **Synthesis for this patient (inference):** The HO will be ~7–8 months old by January 2027 and CT already shows bone. Femoral nerve involvement and refractory neuropathic pain are recognised indications; sitting tolerance and rehabilitation goals are at stake. The modern evidence does not support waiting a fixed ~12 months *for recurrence reasons*. Whether the HO is "sufficiently constituted" and whether surgery is worth its risks are questions for an experienced neuro-orthopaedic team after contrast CT and nerve imaging. *[Level 5]*

## 4. PRE-OPERATIVE ASSESSMENT

| Investigation | Purpose | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Contrast CT, arterial + venous phase, 3D** | Extent, location (Mavrogenis type), corticalisation, joint space, capsule, vessel relations | CT–surgery agreement κ 0.82 for vessels (Law-Ye 2016); predicted all intra-articular lesions (Genêt 2009) |
| **MRI** (± high-resolution ultrasound) of the femoral nerve | Nerve course and compression | **Femoral nerve visualised more often on MRI than CT**; CT–MRI agreement only κ 0.47 for femoral nerve (Amar 2024); MRI equivalent to CT for bone (Amar 2021) |
| **NCS/EMG**, femoral (quadriceps, iliopsoas, saphenous) **with side-to-side comparison** | Focal femoral lesion on top of GBS; baseline for recovery | ENMG alone **misses** compression (abnormal in only 10/18 entrapped sciatic nerves — Salga 2015) |
| **Bone density** of the femur | Fracture risk during mobilisation of an ankylosed/osteopenic hip | Genêt 2009; Law-Ye 2016 |
| **Clinical function** — sitting tolerance, transfers, hip flexion | Defines the **"contract"** (agreed functional goal) | Denormandie 2018 |
| **3D-printed model** (complex cases) | Planning around femoral vessels | Solyom 2026 (case) |

*[Level 3–4]*

## 5. WHAT RESECTION ACHIEVES

| Study | Population | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| **Mavrogenis 2012** | 24 pts / 33 hips (TBI/SCI) | Mean flexion **25° → 79°**; **all patients could sit** |
| **Beom 2023** | Stroke, 9 hips, Smith-Petersen approach | Flexion gain mean **59°** |
| **Melamed 2002** | 8 hips | Arc 92.5° at 1 year; walking improved in 4/8 |
| **Moore 1993** | 13 ankylosed hips | Arc 85° post-op; 17/20 joints kept functional motion |
| **Sarafis 1999** | 22 ankylosed hips | 15 reached functional range; 6 of 7 failures due to uncontrolled neurological condition |
| **Garland & Orwin 1989** | SCI, 24 hips | Arc 11.5° pre → 82.7° intra-op → **35.2° at 6 years**; 74% could sit |

**Pattern:** resection reliably restores a functional arc and sitting in selected patients; some intra-operative gain is lost; walking depends mainly on neurological recovery and motor control (Garland 1991). Resection at Garches is deliberately **functional, not complete** — complete removal adds complications (Denormandie 2018). No series reports patient satisfaction or pain as a formal outcome. *[Level 3–4]*

## 6. WHAT RESECTION COSTS: BLEEDING, INFECTION, FRACTURE, VESSELS

### 6.1 Bleeding

| Source | Blood loss / transfusion |
|---|---|
| de l'Escalopier 2019 | 300–500 mL intra-operative **+ up to 1 L post-operative oozing**; drains 48 h |
| **Le Tat 2026** (18 anteromedial/circumferential) | **Mean ~1,789 mL; 33% transfused**; embolisation did not reduce loss but shortened stay (6.2 vs 8 days); one puncture-site arterial thrombosis |
| Papalexis 2023 (16) | 1,350 → 875 mL with pre-op embolisation; transfusion −75% |
| Mavrogenis 2012 | Transfusion in 14/20 hip operations; **more with anteromedial/circumferential types** |
| Beom 2023 | Hb drop 3.1 g/dL; 3.9 RBC units |

### 6.2 Infection

- **Gatin 2017** (411 hip NHO excisions): infection **10%**; risk factors ASA III (OR 2.84), age <30, SCI (OR 4.89); **~5% when the aetiology was not SCI**; ICU-type organisms predominated.
- **de l'Escalopier 2019:** 10.3% overall, **17.4% for anterior NHO**.
- No data on immunodeficiency/IgA deficiency.

### 6.3 Other

- **Femoral neck fracture** — in ankylosed and osteopenic hips (Genêt 2009).
- **Vascular:** vessel contact in 42% on CT, 8 bony tunnels around vessels, femoral vein injury and circumflex artery injury reported (Law-Ye 2016); deep femoral artery sacrificed in a giant iliofemoral HO (Solyom 2026).
- Haematoma; instability (1/9 → arthroplasty, Beom 2023); joint damage may require arthroplasty or head–neck resection, planned in advance (de l'Escalopier 2019).
- Hospital stay 6–11.5 days (Le Tat 2026; Papalexis 2023).

*[Level 3–4]*

## 7. ANTERIOR HO AND THE FEMORAL NERVE

- Anterior HO is common (48–60% of hip NHO; Law-Ye 2016; Genêt 2011) and approached anteriorly (Smith-Petersen or extended Hueter) (Garland 1991; Mavrogenis 2012; Le Tat 2026). It is the **highest-bleeding and highest-infection location**.
- The **femoral vessels** are the structures most at risk; the femoral nerve lies immediately lateral.
- **No study reports the incidence of femoral nerve entrapment in hip HO, nor outcomes of femoral neurolysis.** The large series analyse the sciatic nerve only.
- **Case-level evidence on nerve release from HO:**
  - **Polfer 2013** (5 combat-related lower-limb entrapments): no intra-operative nerve/vessel injury; **all patients with neuropathic pain improved; sensory deficits recovered before motor**.
  - **Brooke 1991** (2 cases): nerve function improved; early surgery recommended.
  - **Keister 2026:** myositis ossificans compressing **femoral nerve branches** → excision + neurolysis → knee extension M4 at 3 weeks, partial sensory recovery.
  - **Salga 2015:** sciatic release needed in **47%** of posterior resections; clinical signs in only 22% — compression is under-diagnosed.
- **Pain relief is not guaranteed:** after HO excision post-arthroplasty, ROM improved but **pain relief was inconsistent** (Lachiewicz 2024).

> **Inference:** Release is most likely to help if the nerve is **still compressed**; if the injury occurred at onset (during the lift) and compression is no longer active, decompression may relieve less. MRI/ultrasound of the nerve is the key discriminator before surgery. *[Level 4–5]*

## 8. RECURRENCE PROPHYLAXIS — AND THE CURRENT NAPROXEN

### 8.1 In neurogenic HO specifically

| Source | Finding |
|---|---|
| **Azhideh 2026** (meta-analysis, 18 studies, n=937 NHO) | Recurrence 12.4%: drugs 9.3%, radiotherapy 15.7%, **none 13.6%** — differences not significant |
| **Honore 2020** (BANKHO case-control) | Radiotherapy **did not reduce recurrence** (OR 0.63) and **increased sepsis requiring revision** (p<0.05); authors advise against RT with hip NHO excision |
| **Genêt 2011** | 5.8% recurrence **without** radiotherapy or indomethacin |
| **Denormandie 2018** | "No adjuvant treatments have demonstrated efficacy against recurrence" |
| **Seegenschmiedt 1997** (n=410) | Pre-op single 7 Gy **failed in 39%** when excising Brooker III–IV HO vs 5% with post-op fractionated RT |
| **Haran 2004** (Cochrane, etidronate) | May only delay mineralisation, resumes after stopping; insufficient evidence |

### 8.2 In hip arthroplasty / acetabular fracture (primary prevention)

- Radiotherapy (single 7–8 Gy, <4 h pre-op or <72 h post-op) ≈ NSAIDs (Vavken 2009, 9 RCTs, RR 0.96; Pakos 2004: RT better for Brooker 3–4 but absolute difference **1.18%**; Gregoritch 1994; Blokhuis 2009; Wen 2025).
- **Naproxen 750 mg/day** ≈ indomethacin, celecoxib, meloxicam (Xie 2025, network meta-analysis, 17 RCTs). Naproxen 500 mg ×2 for 3 weeks: HO 4% vs 46% placebo (Beckmann 2015, RCT n=108); 8 days to 6 weeks equally effective (Ma 2018).
- **HIPAID** (Fransen 2006, n=902): ibuprofen reduced HO but gave **no gain in pain or function** and **doubled major bleeding**.
- Long-term safety of RT prophylaxis: no early loosening or carcinogenesis at 10 years in 97 patients (Pakos 2020); a single case of sarcoma after 7 Gy has been reported (Rosenberg 2019, PMID 31815809).

### 8.3 The current naproxen

- NSAIDs **prevent** HO when started early — peri-operatively, or ~3 weeks after SCI (Banovac 2001, 2004).
- **No study shows that an NSAID alters established HO at ~8 months.**
- Long-term NSAID risks: GI bleeding, renal function (especially with fluid shifts), and possible interaction with neurogenic bowel. Naproxen also provides analgesia.

> **Fair question for the team (not a recommendation):** what is the current purpose of continued naproxen — HO prophylaxis, analgesia, or both — and is it to be continued, paused around surgery (bleeding), or restarted as a short post-operative course? *[Level 5]*

## 9. PERIOPERATIVE ISSUES SPECIFIC TO THIS PATIENT

### 9.1 Transfusion with anti-IgA antibodies

- Expected blood loss for anterior/anteromedial HO: **~0.9–1.8 L**, transfusion in **33–70%** (§6.1).
- Anti-IgA antibodies carry a risk of anaphylactic transfusion reaction; standard measures are **washed red cells/platelets**, **IgA-deficient donor plasma** when plasma is needed, and albumin rather than FFP for volume. These are covered in [Selective_IgA_Deficiency_Comprehensive_Review.md](../02-IgA-deficiency/Selective_IgA_Deficiency_Comprehensive_Review.md) and [IgA_Deficiency_SCIG_IVIg_Safety.md](../02-IgA-deficiency/IgA_Deficiency_SCIG_IVIg_Safety.md).
- The evidence base is weak and international practice varies: a critical review concluded that management of IgA-related reactions "is not evidence based" (Sandler 2015); surveys show no harmonised definitions or product criteria (Bazin 2026; Keller 2026).
- **Blood-sparing options:** **intra-operative cell salvage** reduces allogeneic transfusion in orthopaedics without significant adverse events (Uchimura 2026; Walton 2023: OR 0.33 in revision hip arthroplasty). **Tranexamic acid** reduces transfusion in hip surgery without increased VTE (Lewis 2023, Cochrane overview; Fillingham 2019 guideline) — no HO-specific data. **Pre-operative embolisation** reduced blood loss in one series (Papalexis 2023) but not in another (Le Tat 2026).

> **Inference:** Salvaged blood is washed and largely plasma-free, which makes cell salvage logically attractive with anti-IgA antibodies — not studied in IgA deficiency. Early involvement of the transfusion service is the practical step. *[Level 5]*

### 9.2 Surgery as a GBS trigger — relevant with recurrent GBS

| Source | Finding |
|---|---|
| **Rudant 2018** (French nationwide case-crossover, 8,364 GBS cases) | Surgery within 60 days OR 1.53; **bone surgery OR 2.78 (1.68–4.60)** |
| **Gensicke 2012** (n=63) | RR 13.1 within 6 weeks after surgery; ~4.1 per 100,000 surgeries |
| **Huang 2020** (systematic review, 136 cases) | Median 9 days from trigger to onset |
| **Yang 2026** | 91% of post-spinal-surgery GBS within 14 days |
| **Olivier 2010** | **Two patients with recurrent GBS after hip prosthesis surgery**, one after two separate operations |
| **Abwini 2024** | GBS after one knee arthroplasty and again 11 years later after the second (onset day 11) |
| **Staff 2010** (*Brain*) | Post-surgical inflammatory neuropathy (33 cases, 14 orthopaedic), within 30 days, immunotherapy-responsive |

> **Implication (inference):** The absolute risk is low, but this patient has **recurrent** GBS. Structured neurological surveillance for ~6 weeks (highest risk days 5–14) and a pre-agreed plan for early treatment are reasonable topics to raise. There is no guideline for elective surgery in recurrent GBS. *[Level 3–5]*

### 9.3 Anaesthesia

- **Succinylcholine should be avoided** — hyperkalaemic cardiac arrest has occurred within a month of clinical recovery from GBS (Feldman 1990; Raja 2014); acetylcholine-receptor up-regulation occurs with denervation **and immobilisation** (Martyn 2006). **No safe interval after GBS has been established.**
- Non-depolarising blockers: risk of prolonged block; TOF monitoring from the start; sugammadex for reversal; consider post-operative ICU/HDU (Radkowski 2024).
- Regional anaesthesia: 0.4% new/worse deficits in 567 patients with pre-existing neuropathy given neuraxial anaesthesia (Hebl 2006); ASRA advisory on pre-existing neurological disease (Kopp 2015); GBS after spinal anaesthesia reported (Kapadia 2025). No GBS-specific series; the existing femoral catheter is a practical data point, not evidence.
- **Autonomic instability:** prepare for hypotension at induction and labile BP with bleeding (see [Severe_GBS_ICU_Management.md](../01-GBS/Severe_GBS_ICU_Management.md)).
- **Respiratory reserve:** recently decannulated, FVC ~1.3 L (day 184) — plan for post-operative monitoring, cough assistance, and cautious opioid escalation.

### 9.4 Venous thromboembolism

- GBS patients in ICU: DVT 52%, PE 15% despite LMWH (Ryabinkina 2016); clinical DVT 7% with one fatal PE on enoxaparin (Gaber 2002). Risk declines after months of immobility (Gaber 2005) — hip surgery re-introduces it. Femoral vein thromboses were found pre-operatively in 3/101 HO patients (Law-Ye 2016). See [PE_Coagulopathy_and_Thromboprophylaxis_in_GBS.md](../05-treatment-resistance/PE_Coagulopathy_and_Thromboprophylaxis_in_GBS.md).

### 9.5 Infection

~5–10% (Gatin 2017); 17% for anterior HO (de l'Escalopier 2019). A long ICU stay with possible colonisation by ICU organisms is a more concrete concern than IgA deficiency, for which there are no data. *[Level 3–5]*

## 10. SEQUENCING: HO SURGERY, NEUROMODULATION AND REHABILITATION

### 10.1 Post-operative course

Gentle mobilisation from day 2 (Genêt) or after week 1 (de l'Escalopier); rehabilitation in a dedicated centre, intensified after suture removal ~day 15; weight-bearing graded by bone density; hospital stay ~6–11 days. Goals agreed before surgery ("contract") — sitting, transfers, hip flexion (Denormandie 2018; Genêt 2019).

### 10.2 Options (not recommendations)

| Sequence | For | Against |
|---|---|---|
| **A. Implant (DRG/SCS) now, HO surgery later** | Pain control now; implant outside the surgical field | Implant may treat a pain source that decompression could remove; SSEP/root questions unresolved; second procedure later |
| **B. HO resection + femoral neurolysis first, reassess pain, implant only if pain persists** | Treats the cause if compression is active; restores flexion/sitting; may avoid permanent hardware | Bleeding, infection, GBS-trigger risk; pain relief not guaranteed; timing vs rehab |
| **C. Bridge (catheter / TENS / temporary 60-day PNS) until decision on B** | Reversible; buys time for CT/MRI/NCS and specialist review | Catheter duration limited; temporary PNS durability poor in independent data (Sherwood 2026) |

### 10.3 Timing relative to Högsbo (2027-01-11)

- **Before admission:** the ~6-week post-operative GBS-surveillance window and early post-op period would overlap the start of rehabilitation.
- **During the stay:** interrupts intensive rehabilitation ~2 weeks; requires coordination with the rehab unit.
- **After the stay:** the original "~1 year" plan; carries the costs of waiting (ankylosis risk, osteopenia, prolonged pain, restricted sitting, possible ongoing nerve compression).

*[Level 5 — synthesis]*

## 11. RANGE OF MOTION WHILE HO IS PRESENT

- **Forcible stretching** of immobilised limbs consistently produced HO in rabbits; gentle passive motion did not (Michelsson 1980, 1983). In SCI, HO occurred when passive movements started after contractures had begun (Daud 1993). *[Level 5 — animal/observational]*
- Systematic review recommends early, controlled, **pain-free** passive ROM (Vasileiadis 2023; very low quality).
- The temporal sequence in this patient (HO after vigorous ICU mobilisation) is biologically plausible but causality cannot be established in an individual.
- **With good analgesia (catheter, TENS, possibly neuromodulation), pain no longer limits range** — the 50–60° flexion restriction must be maintained by protocol. *[Level 5]*

## 13. EVIDENCE GAPS AND HONEST LIMITATIONS

- No surgical series of HO after **GBS** (one GBS patient among 357 Garches patients; case reports only).
- No data on **femoral nerve** entrapment incidence or neurolysis outcomes in hip HO.
- Timing evidence is Level 3 observational, mainly from **TBI/SCI**; early-surgery follow-up in the largest series was short.
- **Prophylaxis** evidence for NHO is weak; RCT evidence comes from arthroplasty and acetabular fractures.
- No data on **IgA deficiency** and surgical infection, nor on **cell salvage** in anti-IgA-positive patients.
- No guideline on elective surgery in **recurrent GBS**.
- Pain and patient satisfaction are rarely formal outcomes in HO surgery series.
