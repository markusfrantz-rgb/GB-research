## 1. SCOPE AND THE CLINICAL QUESTION

**Clinical context (as of 2026-11-25, day ~249):** A 52-year-old woman in the recovery phase of severe recurrent GBS (anti-GQ1b spectrum with Bickerstaff brainstem encephalitis overlap) has **heterotopic ossification (HO) / myositis ossificans at the right hip/groin**. Resection has been planned for when the bone has matured (~1 year).

**The pain (neurosurgical note 2026-11-18):** acute onset in early summer **during a lift transfer** — intense right groin pain radiating to the anterior thigh — interpreted as **neuropathic pain from injury to the right femoral nerve at groin level by the local HO** (ICD G58.8, mononeuropathy). Examination: **hypoaesthesia to touch below the groin on the anterior right thigh**, normal sensation above the groin. A **femoral nerve block** gave very good relief; she now has a **continuous peripheral nerve catheter along the femoral nerve** (effective, but not a long-term solution). The neurosurgeon is considering **spinal cord stimulation (SCS) or dorsal root ganglion (DRG) stimulation** and required a **local TENS trial** (groin/anterior thigh) first. **The TENS trial (week 48) gave a good result.**

> **Correction note (2026-11-25):** The first version of this document assumed the blocked nerve was probably the obturator nerve and framed the pain as hip-joint (capsular) pain. The neurosurgical note establishes that it is the **femoral nerve** and that the pain is **neuropathic**. Sections 2–3 (hip capsule innervation, obturator anatomy) are retained as background; the decision-relevant material is in §4–6 (TENS), §9 (DRG vs SCS) and §10 (HO compression of the femoral nerve).

**The femoral nerve at the groin.** The femoral nerve (L2–L4) descends within iliopsoas and passes **beneath the inguinal ligament** in the lacuna musculorum, immediately lateral to the femoral artery and vein, then fans out in the femoral triangle into anterior cutaneous branches (anterior thigh — the hypoaesthetic area), the saphenous nerve, and motor branches to **quadriceps** and hip flexors. *[Level 5 — standard anatomy]*

**Questions addressed:**

1. How is surface TENS used and placed for maximal effect — particularly when the painful territory is hypoaesthetic?
2. Does a positive TENS response (or nerve block) predict that an implanted system will work?
3. DRG vs SCS vs peripheral nerve stimulation (PNS) for focal femoral/groin neuropathic pain — effect, durability, complications.
4. What is specific to a patient whose roots and peripheral nerves are recovering after GBS?
5. Could the femoral nerve be compressed by HO — and does that change the timing and choice of treatment?

**Intended use.** Background for the treating neurologist, neurosurgeon, pain/anaesthesia team and physiotherapist. It is written by an analyst compiling evidence, not by a specialist in neuromodulation; it describes what the literature shows and where it stops.

## 2. WHICH NERVES CARRY PAIN FROM THE HIP

### 2.1 The hip capsule has several nerve supplies, not one

Three systematic/anatomical reviews agree on the broad picture: the hip joint capsule is innervated by **several nerves**, and the supply is **highly variable between individuals**.

| Capsule region | Main nerves | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| **Anterior (superior/lateral)** | Femoral nerve (articular branches), accessory obturator nerve when present | Highest density of nociceptors (with the superior labrum) |
| **Anteromedial / inferomedial** | **Obturator nerve** (articular branches, usually from the anterior branch or common trunk) | Primary target for obturator-based techniques |
| **Posteromedial** | Nerve to quadratus femoris, sciatic nerve branches | Not reached by anterior blocks |
| **Posterolateral** | Superior gluteal nerve | Not reached by anterior blocks |

*Sources: Birnbaum 1997; Laumonerie 2021; Tomlinson 2021.* *[Level 3 — anatomical studies and systematic reviews]*

**Key findings:**

- **Laumonerie 2021** (systematic review, 21 studies): the **superior anterior capsule and the labrum** have the densest nociceptive innervation; the **anterior capsule, supplied primarily by the femoral and obturator nerves**, is the main pain generator of the hip joint.
- **Tomlinson 2021** (meta-analysis of gross-anatomy studies): innervation is "highly variable"; the **obturator nerve and the nerve to quadratus femoris** appear to be the most consistent suppliers; many articular branches arise from **muscular branches**. Recommended primary target for denervation: the **anterior inferomedial capsule**.
- **Birnbaum 1997** started from a clinical observation: **obturator nerve block sometimes has only a minor effect on hip pain.** Dissection showed why — the femoral, sciatic, superior gluteal nerves and the nerve to quadratus femoris also contribute. Their conclusion: effective neural blockade of the hip requires more than one nerve.

### 2.2 Clinical consequence

> **A single-nerve approach covers part of the hip, not all of it.** If the temporary block — and now TENS — has given good relief, that is evidence that the *dominant* pain pathway in this particular patient runs through the targeted nerve. It does not exclude residual pain from other territories (e.g., posterior capsule, lateral hip), which may become noticeable once the dominant component is controlled.

This is consistent with the only published case of implanted PNS for complex post-traumatic hip/pelvic pain (Elsharkawy 2026, §8), where **three** nerves (obturator, femoral, middle cluneal) were targeted after diagnostic blocks of each.

### 2.3 PENG — a block designed around this anatomy

The **PENG block** (PEricapsular Nerve Group; Girón-Arango 2018) was developed specifically from this anatomical knowledge: a single ultrasound-guided injection between the psoas tendon and the iliopubic eminence that reaches the **articular branches of the femoral and accessory obturator nerves** (and, by spread, sometimes the obturator nerve) **while largely sparing motor function**. A 2025 five-year review (Girón-Arango & Peng) summarises its mechanism, spread patterns and the occasional quadriceps weakness when spread reaches the femoral nerve trunk. *[Level 2–3 — RCTs in hip fracture/arthroplasty; no data in HO]*

If the "temporary nerve block" given so far was a PENG block rather than a pure obturator block, the target structures for a PNS lead would differ. This is worth clarifying (§11).

## 3. THE OBTURATOR NERVE: CANAL, VESSELS AND BRANCHES

### 3.1 Course

The obturator nerve (roots **L2–L4**) descends within the psoas, runs along the lateral pelvic wall and leaves the pelvis through the **obturator canal** — a fibro-osseous tunnel at the upper edge of the obturator foramen, beneath the superior pubic ramus — **together with the obturator artery and vein**. This is the "tunnel with a blood vessel" in the clinical description. *[Level 5 — standard anatomy]*

### 3.2 Where it divides

**Prabhu 2025** (50 cadaveric limbs) measured where the nerve divides into its anterior and posterior branches:

| Division site | Proportion |
|---|---|
| Inside the pelvis (before the canal) | 56% |
| Inside the obturator canal | 24% |
| No division observed | 20% |

Trunk width ≈ 2.8 mm, thickness ≈ 1.1 mm — a small target. *[Level 3 — cadaveric]*

**Branch functions (simplified):**

- **Anterior branch:** adductor longus, brevis, gracilis (± pectineus); **articular branch to the hip**; cutaneous branch to the **medial thigh**.
- **Posterior branch:** obturator externus, adductor magnus (part); articular branch to the **knee**.

The **articular hip branch** most often comes from the anterior branch or from the trunk close to the canal (Tomlinson 2021). This is why a block or stimulation **at the canal exit**, before the branches spread, can capture the hip component.

### 3.3 Blocking (and stimulating) at the canal

**Labandeyra 2026** (cadaver study, 20 hemipelves) described an ultrasound-guided approach **at the exit of the obturator canal**, using the iliopubic ramus as landmark: dye reached the **common trunk and anterior branch in 100%** and the **posterior branch in 80%**. Their conclusion: a sagittal approach at the canal exit achieves "the most complete obturator nerve block." *[Level 3 — cadaveric; clinical safety and efficacy not yet confirmed]*

The same location is relevant for **lead placement** in PNS, because one lead near the trunk can influence both branches — but the trunk also carries **motor fibres to the adductors** (§6.3).

### 3.4 Clinical evidence for obturator-targeted treatment of hip pain

| Study | Design | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| **Yavuz 2013** | Retrospective, n=20, hip OA | Block of articular branches of obturator + femoral nerves: significant pain reduction at 1 and 3 months; NSAID use ↓ ~70% |
| **Crema 2023** | Double-blind RCT, n=44, severe hip OA | Obturator anterior-branch block with **phenol vs lidocaine**: both improved pain and function at 1 month, effect **fading by 4 months**; **no difference** between phenol and lidocaine |
| **Tolba 2020** | Case report | Severe obturator neuropathy after pubic ramus fracture: dorsal root ganglion stimulation → 90% relief sustained 1 year, after spinal cord stimulation failed |

*[Level 2–4]*

## 4. HOW TENS WORKS — AND WHAT DETERMINES WHETHER IT WORKS

### 4.1 Mechanisms

TENS delivers pulsed current through skin electrodes. Analgesia arises through **segmental** and **descending** mechanisms (Sluka & Walsh 2003; Vance 2022):

- **Segmental ("gate") inhibition:** stimulation of large-diameter, myelinated **Aβ afferents** inhibits nociceptive transmission in the dorsal horn.
- **Descending inhibition:** activation of the periaqueductal grey–rostral ventromedial medulla system.
- **Endogenous opioids — frequency-dependent:** in animal models, **high-frequency TENS (≈80–100 Hz)** acts via **δ-opioid** receptors and **low-frequency TENS (≈2–4 Hz)** via **μ-opioid** receptors. This has a clinical implication: patients on **high-dose μ-opioids** may be relatively cross-tolerant to low-frequency TENS, and high-frequency TENS may be the better starting point (Sluka & Walsh 2003; DeSantana 2008). *[Level 5 — animal mechanism, limited human confirmation]*

### 4.2 How large is the effect?

**Johnson 2022 (meta-TENS; 381 RCTs, 24,532 participants)** — the largest synthesis to date:

- Pain intensity was lower **during or immediately after** TENS than placebo: **SMD −0.96** (95% CI −1.14 to −0.78), **moderate-certainty evidence** (91 RCTs, n=4,841).
- The effect was **not modified** by diagnosis or acute vs chronic pain.
- Adverse events were poorly reported, generally mild, not different from comparators.
- Inclusion criterion worth noting: **"strong non-painful TENS at or close to the site of pain."**

*[Level 1 — systematic review/meta-analysis, moderate certainty]*

**Important qualifier:** the robust effect is **during and immediately after** stimulation. Evidence for lasting effect after the device is switched off is weaker. TENS should be thought of as an **on-demand tool used while it is running**, not a treatment that "heals" pain.

### 4.3 Neuropathic vs nociceptive pain

- **Gibson 2017 (Cochrane, TENS for neuropathic pain):** 15 RCTs, 724 participants; pooled effect favoured TENS over sham (−1.58 on VAS), but **very low quality** evidence — no confident conclusion.
- **Oosterhof 2008 (RCT, n=163, chronic pain):** patients with **post-surgical and bone/soft-tissue injury pain** were most satisfied with high-frequency TENS; patients with **osteoarthritis or peripheral neuropathic pain were less satisfied**.

**Relevance:** HO pain is primarily **nociceptive/inflammatory tissue pain** around the joint — the patient herself distinguished it from her GBS nerve pain. That is the pain type in which TENS appears to perform comparatively well. *[Level 2–3]*

### 4.4 Why so many TENS studies are negative: underdosing

Both Johnson's group and Sluka's group have argued that much of the historically mixed evidence reflects **inadequate dose** — intensity too low, application too short, outcome measured too late (Vance 2022). **Intensity is the single parameter with the clearest dose–response:**

- **Moran 2011** (RCT, n=130 healthy volunteers): hypoalgesia was greatest with **"strong but non-painful"** TENS; below-sensory-threshold TENS was no better than placebo; the effect **correlated with current amplitude**.
- **Vance 2022:** "stimulation intensity has been documented as critical to therapeutic success."

*[Level 2 — RCT; consistent across reviews]*

## 5. OPTIMISING TENS IN PRACTICE: DOSE, PLACEMENT, TIMING, TOLERANCE

The following table summarises what the literature supports. It is meant as a discussion basis for the physiotherapist/pain team, not as an instruction.

| Parameter | What the evidence supports | Source / level |
|---|---|---|
| **Intensity** | **"Strong but comfortable"** — clearly strong, not painful. Below-threshold is ineffective. | Moran 2011 *[L2]*; Vance 2022 |
| **Finding the right intensity** | The **SIT method**: raise to sensory threshold → strong-comfortable → briefly to noxious → back to strong-comfortable. Final intensity ends up higher (median +1.7 mA) than without the noxious step. | Vance 2018 (n=143) *[L2]* |
| **Re-titration during session** | Perceived intensity fades within minutes (habituation); **turn up** when the sensation weakens to keep the dose. Few clinical studies did this. | Gibson 2017; Vance 2022 *[L5]* |
| **Frequency** | **High frequency (80–100 Hz) in opioid users**: low-frequency TENS had no effect in opioid users whereas conventional TENS did (Léonard 2011, crossover n=23). Mixed/alternating frequency delays tolerance in animals. | Léonard 2011 *[L2]*; DeSantana 2008 *[L5, animal]* |
| **Placement** | At or close to the pain site, over nerve bundles proximal to it, or — **when the painful area is hypoaesthetic — on skin with intact sensation** (bracketing the numb area, proximal, paravertebral at the same segments, or contralateral). For femoral pain with anterior-thigh hypoaesthesia: around the groin/lateral and medial thigh (L2–L4 territories with intact sensation) or paravertebral L2–L4. Placement matters less than intensity (Amer-Cuenca 2026). Nerve-dense skin beat the exact pain spot in knee OA (Xu 2025, RCT n=80); lumbar-plexus placement ≥ wound placement after hip fracture (Oksar 2024, RCT n=120). | Johnson 2021 *[L5]*; Xu 2025, Oksar 2024 *[L2]*; Amer-Cuenca 2026 *[L1–2]* |
| **Timing** | Use **during the painful activity** — transfers, sitting, training — not only at rest. In the FAST trial (n=301), TENS used **during activity** 2 h/day for 4 weeks reduced **movement-evoked pain** vs placebo TENS (−1.0) and vs no TENS (−1.8); global improvement 70% vs 31%. | Dailey 2020 *[L1–2 — RCT]* |
| **Predicting responders** | The response to the **first 30-minute session** predicted response after one month (NNT 3.3–5.3). | Vance 2021 *[L2]* |
| **Tolerance** | Repeated daily TENS at the **same** intensity and frequency produced analgesic tolerance by **day ~5** in rats. **Increasing intensity ~10%/day** prevented it; **mixed frequency** delayed it ~5 days. | Sato 2012; DeSantana 2008 *[L5, animal]* |

### 5.1 Implications for the coming weeks

```
IF TENS gives good relief now:
  → Use it during the moments that are painful (lift, wheelchair sitting, gym),
    not only at rest.
  → Strong-but-comfortable intensity, re-titrated during the session.
  → Watch for fading effect after ~1 week of daily use:
      - if effect wanes: raise intensity, switch to mixed/alternating frequency.
  → Document effect systematically (pain score before/during/after,
    sitting tolerance in minutes, opioid doses) — this becomes the objective
    basis for the implant decision.
```

### 5.2 Safety in this specific patient

- **Skin:** the anterior right thigh is **hypoaesthetic** (neurosurgical note) — electrodes should not sit only on numb skin, which cannot report heat or irritation; impaired sensation is a listed precaution (Physiotherapy Canada 2010). Electrode sites should be inspected. Skin irritation is the commonly reported adverse event (Gibson 2017).
- **Autonomic dysfunction:** no specific signal in the TENS literature; TENS over the groin/thigh is far from the sites of concern (anterior neck, chest).
- **Standard contraindications** (cardiac pacemaker/ICD, over the anterior neck, broken skin) apply. *[Level 5]*

## 6. THE GBS-SPECIFIC QUESTION: TENS AND PNS ON A RECOVERING NERVE

No study has examined TENS or PNS for musculoskeletal/HO pain in patients after GBS. The following is **mechanistic reasoning**, labelled as such.

### 6.1 TENS depends on functioning large-fibre afferents

Segmental TENS analgesia is mediated by **Aβ fibres** — exactly the large, myelinated fibres that GBS damages. **Ruts 2012** showed that both **unmyelinated and myelinated skin nerves** are diffusely affected in GBS (including pure motor variants), and that intraepidermal nerve fibre density **remains low at 6 months**.

> **Interpretation (hypothesis, not established fact):** A clear analgesic response to TENS suggests that there is **enough functioning large-fibre afferent input** in the stimulated territory for segmental inhibition to work. It does not by itself say anything about motor recovery. *[Level 5 — mechanistic inference]*

### 6.2 Pain in GBS lasts — and is under-treated

**Ruts 2010** (prospective, n=156): pain was reported by **66%** in the acute phase and by **38% after one year**, mostly moderate–severe. **Liu 2015 (Cochrane):** there is **insufficient evidence** for any pharmacological treatment of GBS pain, and **no trials at all** in the convalescent phase. Non-pharmacological options therefore carry relatively more weight — and HO pain comes **on top of** any GBS-related pain.

### 6.3 Mixed nerves: sensory relief vs motor effect

Both the obturator and femoral nerves are **mixed nerves** (adductors and quadriceps respectively). Two published observations are relevant:

- **Pierson 2024** (case, femoral PNS after post-traumatic hip arthroplasty): 4 weeks at **100 Hz sensory-level** for pain, then switched to **12 Hz motor-level** stimulation → hip flexion strength rose from 10.4 to 23.0 kg in 3 weeks, functional score 35→54/80. *[Level 4 — single case]*
- **Gordon 2016** (review, animal + human data): **brief low-frequency (20 Hz) electrical stimulation** of the proximal nerve after injury **accelerates axon regeneration** and target reinnervation; demonstrated in human carpal tunnel and digital nerve studies. *[Level 3–5 — not studied in GBS]*

> **What this does and does not mean:** These findings make it *plausible* that stimulation of a mixed nerve is at least not harmful to a recovering nerve, and raise an interesting question about motor-level stimulation. They do **not** show that PNS improves GBS recovery, and no such claim should be made. The question to ask is the opposite one: **is there any reason, in a nerve still undergoing reinnervation, to prefer or avoid particular stimulation settings?** This is a neurophysiological question for the treating neurologist. *[Level 5]*

### 6.4 Baseline neurophysiology

NCS/EMG has not been performed during the current episode. If an obturator/femoral lead is implanted, a baseline study of the **adductor and quadriceps** territories would make any later change (improvement or deterioration) interpretable. This connects to the already-open question of NCS/EMG as a recovery baseline (see [Motor_Relearning…](Motor_Relearning_Neurophysiotherapy_GBS_Recovery_Phase.md)).

## 7. FROM TENS TO IMPLANTED PERIPHERAL NERVE STIMULATION (PNS)

### 7.1 What PNS is

A thin lead is placed (usually ultrasound-guided, percutaneously) **close to the target nerve**, and connected to a pulse generator — either **implanted** or **external/wireless**. Available systems include SPRINT (60-day temporary, external), StimRouter, Freedom/Curonix, Nalu (ASIPP 2024).

| Variant | Characteristics |
|---|---|
| **Temporary percutaneous PNS (60 days)** | Fine-wire coiled lead, external stimulator, removed after ~60 days; no permanent hardware. Relief has been reported to persist after removal in some populations. |
| **Permanent PNS** | Lead + implanted or wirelessly powered receiver; often preceded by a trial. |

### 7.2 Does a positive TENS response predict PNS response?

**Direct evidence: none found.** Guidelines require patient selection by "**positive diagnostic nerve block or stimulation trial**" (Strand 2022, ASPN). In most published PNS series the trial is a **percutaneous lead trial** (a few days to weeks with a temporary lead near the nerve), not surface TENS.

The physiological logic is reasonable — both act through large-fibre activation in the same territory — but **TENS stimulates skin and superficial tissue, whereas PNS stimulates the nerve trunk directly.** A positive TENS trial and a positive diagnostic block together are **supportive**, but not a substitute for a lead trial. *[Level 5]*

> **Worth asking:** Will there be a **percutaneous lead trial** before a permanent implant, or is the TENS result considered sufficient?

### 7.3 Evidence for PNS overall

| Source | Conclusion |
|---|---|
| **Xu 2021** (systematic review, 227 studies) | Level I–II for chronic migraine; Level II for post-amputation, pelvic, low back/lower extremity pain; **Level IV for postsurgical and peripheral neuropathic pain** |
| **Strand 2022** (ASPN guideline) | Level I evidence for several indications incl. lower extremity neuropathic and post-amputation pain; PNS should follow **adequate screening and a positive block or stimulation trial** |
| **ASIPP 2024** (Manchikanti) | Implantable PNS after a trial: **Level III / fair, moderate certainty**; temporary 60-day PNS: same |
| **Ontario Health 2024** (HTA) | Permanent PNS for chronic neuropathic pain: **likely** reduces pain, improves function and QoL vs placebo (**GRADE moderate**); procedure "reasonably safe", most adverse events local and mild |
| **Ilfeld 2021** (RCT, n=66, sham-controlled) | Percutaneous PNS (incl. **femoral nerve**) after orthopaedic surgery: opioid use first week **5 vs 48 mg**; average pain **1.1 vs 3.1** |
| **Gilmore 2019** (RCT, n=28) | Percutaneous PNS (femoral + sciatic) for post-amputation pain: **58% vs 14%** achieved ≥50% relief |

*[Level 1–3]*

### 7.4 Complications

**Eldabe 2016** (review of SCS and PNS): 30–40% experience at least one complication over time; **lead migration is the most common hardware complication**; infection and pain over the implant are the common biological ones; serious neurological harm is rare. Lead migration occurred in the hip case report (Elsharkawy 2026) and required revision.

> **Specific to this patient:** transfers by lift with 2 persons, hip movement at the 50–60° flexion limit, and later HO surgery in the same region — all plausible stressors on a lead near the groin. Whether fixation technique or lead type is chosen with this in mind is a reasonable question. *[Level 5]*

### 7.5 MRI

**Shellock 2018** tested retained fragments of percutaneous PNS leads: MRI was safe at **1.5 T** under defined conditions; at **3 T**, excessive heating could occur. Each device has its own MR labelling. Since the patient has needed repeated brain and spinal MRI during this illness and may need more, **MR conditionality of the chosen system** is a practical question. *[Level 3]*

## 8. PNS FOR HIP AND GROIN PAIN: WHAT HAS BEEN PUBLISHED

The hip-specific PNS literature is **small and consists of case reports and case series**:

| Study | Patient | Target | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Elsharkawy 2026** | 68 F, chronic hip/pelvic pain after iliac wing fracture (non-union, not operable); on tramadol, gabapentin, baclofen | Diagnostic blocks of **obturator + femoral sensory branches** (8→2/10 for 2 days) and **middle cluneal nerve** → 1-week PNS trial of all three (wireless, externally powered system; no implanted battery) | Trial: **75% pain reduction**. Settings **1499 Hz, 30 µs** (high-frequency). The distal contacts over the **obturator branches** gave the best relief of anteromedial hip pain. Permanent tined leads: at 1 month **all leads had migrated** → revision at 2 months. After revision: anterior and posterior pain **3–4/10** at 6 months; pain returned within ~2 h if switched off; ~50% relief sustained at 12 months |
| **Pierson 2024** | 53 M, pain and weakness after post-traumatic hip arthroplasty | **Femoral nerve**, single percutaneous lead | 100 Hz sensory → 12 Hz motor-level; pain and **hip flexion strength** improved |
| **Yakovlev 2010** | 12 patients, chronic pain after hip arthroplasty/bursectomy | Peripheral nerve **field** stimulation (subcutaneous leads over lateral thigh) | Sustained relief at 12 months (retrospective) |
| **Tolba 2020** | 61 F, obturator neuropathy after pubic fracture | **Dorsal root ganglion** stimulation (not peripheral) | 90% relief at 1 year |

*[Level 4 — case reports/series]*

**Pattern across these cases:**
1. **Diagnostic block first**, then a stimulation trial, then permanent implant.
2. **More than one nerve** was often required for complex hip/pelvic pain (consistent with §2).
3. **Lead migration** is the recurring technical problem.

**Technical detail from Elsharkawy 2026 (full text):** the obturator lead was placed at the **articular (sensory) branches lateral to the obturator foramen, just below the acetabulum (the radiographic "teardrop")**, deep to the femoral vessels and iliopsoas tendon, under combined ultrasound and fluoroscopy — i.e., targeting the hip branches rather than the obturator trunk in the canal. The femoral lead targeted femoral articular branches at the upper outer hip capsule. The authors highlight lead migration and the need to keep the external transmitter aligned during movement as the main practical limitations. *[Level 4]*

**No published case of PNS for HO-related hip pain was found**, and none in a patient after GBS.

## 9. DRG STIMULATION VS SPINAL CORD STIMULATION FOR FOCAL FEMORAL/GROIN NEUROPATHIC PAIN

### 9.1 Efficacy

| Study | Design | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| **ACCURATE** (Deer 2017) | RCT, n=152, lower-limb CRPS-I and causalgia (CRPS-II, i.e. pain after peripheral nerve injury) | Treatment success DRG vs SCS **81.2% vs 55.7%** (3 mo), **74.2% vs 53.0%** (12 mo). Causalgia subgroup at 3 mo: **79.3% vs 53.3%**. Paraesthesia confined to the painful area 94.5% vs 61.2%. Industry-sponsored. *[Level 1–2]* |
| **Levy 2020** (ACCURATE) | Habituation analysis | DRG relief stable ~69–74%; SCS declined 66.9% → 57.9% over 12 months *[Level 2]* |

### 9.3 Do a positive block or TENS trial predict success?

- **No study shows that a positive TENS trial or nerve block predicts DRG or SCS success.**
- For PNS, the size of diagnostic-block relief predicted temporary-PNS relief at 3 months but not permanent-PNS outcome (Hoffmann 2024, n=173); relief during a 60-day temporary PNS trial predicted permanent-PNS relief weakly at 6 months but not at 12 months, and relief fell ~25% by 12 months (D'Souza 2025, n=47).
- The only TENS→implant link: TENS responders responded to **peripheral nerve field stimulation** (94%; Schwarm 2021, n=41) — a peripheral, not spinal, technique.
- Screening trials themselves discriminate poorly: in TRIAL-STIM (Eldabe 2020, RCT n=105) the SCS trial had sensitivity 100% but **specificity 8%**, and did not improve outcome.

*[Level 2–3]*

### 9.4 Dorsal-column and root integrity — the GBS question

- **Sindou 2003** (n=95, including 27 root lesions): SCS success **0%** when dorsal-column conduction on SSEP was markedly abnormal vs **75.4%** when normal. A 2026 systematic review (Odonkor) ranks pre-operative SSEP as the strongest single predictor, though unreplicated. *[Level 3]*
- GBS is a **polyradiculoneuropathy** and may involve dorsal roots and dorsal root ganglia. The femoral lesion itself is distal to the DRG, but GBS-related loss of DRG neurones or dorsal-column input could plausibly reduce DRG or tonic SCS efficacy. **No data exist.** *[Level 5]*
- Paraesthesia-free 10 kHz SCS (Petersen 2021) and burst SCS (one CIDP case, Iovenko 2025) may depend less on intact large fibres. *[Level 1 for diabetes; Level 4 for CIDP]*
- **Implication:** SSEP and femoral/saphenous NCS/EMG with side-to-side comparison would inform method choice. NCS/EMG has not been performed in this episode.

### 9.5 Immunodeficiency

No data on implant infection in IgA deficiency; two case reports of infected SCS systems in immunosuppressed patients (Kesayan 2026). *[Level 4–5]*

## 10. COULD THE FEMORAL NERVE BE COMPRESSED BY HO — AND DOES IT CHANGE THE TIMING?

| Finding | Source | Level |
|---|---|---|
| In 116 posterior hip NHO resections, **47% required sciatic nerve release**; the nerve was **entrapped within HO** in 26. Only 22% had clinical signs; ENMG abnormal in only 10/18. Nerve compression is under-diagnosed; CT and repeated examination recommended. | Salga 2015 | 3 |
| **MRI visualises the femoral nerve better than CT** in hip NHO; CT/MRI agreement for the femoral nerve only moderate (κ 0.47) | Amar 2024 (n=31 hips) | 2–3 |
| Femoral neuropathy after anterior THA: MRI showed mass effect in 4/17, one from HO | Thejeel 2024 | 4 |
| **Recurrence after NHO excision 5.8%, unrelated to delay to surgery**; 181 operations within the first year without recurrence. Indications include neurological involvement and pain difficult to manage medically. | Genêt 2011 (539 surgeries) | 3 |
| Loss of range of motion more relevant than HO "maturity" for timing; early surgery limits joint damage | Genêt 2009; Chalidis 2007 (systematic review) | 3 |
| Infection after hip NHO excision ~10% (5% when aetiology not spinal cord injury) | Gatin 2017 (n=411) | 3 |
| Compression-related femoral palsy can reverse after decompression | Kromoser 2025; Keister 2026 (myositis ossificans adjacent to femoral nerve) | 4 |
| Pain relief after HO excision is **inconsistent** (post-THA population) | Lachiewicz 2024 | 3 |
| HO occurs in ~6% of GBS patients | Zeilig 2006 (n=65) | 3 |

**Caveats:** no series addresses femoral entrapment by groin HO specifically; the large French series concern HO after brain/spinal cord injury — this patient has GBS with brainstem (BBE) involvement.

> **Why this may be the pivotal question.** If the neuropathic pain is driven by **ongoing compression**, the generator may be **removable**, and a permanent implant would treat a pain source that surgery could eliminate. The recurrence data no longer mandate waiting ~1 year for maturation, and nerve involvement is an accepted indication. Conversely, if the injury is complete and compression no longer active, decompression may not relieve pain — which is where neuromodulation fits. *[Level 5 — synthesis]*

> **Motor relevance.** The femoral nerve supplies **quadriceps** — the muscle needed for standing. Ongoing compression could plausibly superimpose a focal lesion on the GBS deficit and limit right quadriceps recovery. Not demonstrated in this patient; assessable by side-to-side strength, femoral NCS/EMG (quadriceps, iliopsoas, saphenous) and MRI/high-resolution ultrasound of the nerve. *[Level 5]*

## 11. ALTERNATIVES: BLOCKS, NEUROLYSIS AND RADIOFREQUENCY — AND WHY DESTRUCTIVE OPTIONS CARRY EXTRA COST HERE

| Option | Duration | Evidence | Consideration in this patient |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Repeated local anaesthetic block** (obturator, PENG, articular branches) | Hours–weeks (variable) | Yavuz 2013; Crema 2023 *[L2–4]* | Non-destructive; requires repeated procedures |
| **Chemical neurolysis (phenol)** of obturator anterior branch | Theoretically months | Crema 2023: **not better than lidocaine at 4 months** *[L2]* | Destroys axons, incl. **motor fibres to adductors** if trunk/branch is mixed |
| **Radiofrequency ablation** of articular branches | Months | Bhatia 2018: high reported success, **no RCTs**; uncertain anatomical targets *[L3–4]* | Targets sensory articular branches; anatomical variability (§2) limits precision |
| **Pulsed radiofrequency** | Weeks–months | Case series *[L4]* | Considered non-destructive |
| **PNS (temporary or permanent)** | Weeks (temporary) to years (permanent) | §7–8 | **Non-destructive and reversible** |

> **The key distinction for a patient in active reinnervation:** Destructive techniques (phenol, thermal RF) on a **mixed nerve** risk adding motor axon loss in the very muscles — hip adductors, quadriceps — that rehabilitation is trying to recover. Ablation of purely sensory articular branches carries less of this risk, but the branches are variable and small. **Non-destructive options (repeated blocks, TENS, PNS) preserve the recovering nerve.** This is likely one of the reasons a neuromodulation approach is being considered. *[Level 5 — mechanistic reasoning]*

## 12. INTERACTION WITH HETEROTOPIC OSSIFICATION AND FUTURE HO SURGERY

- **Pain mechanism changes over time.** HO pain in the early inflammatory phase differs from pain caused by mature bone mechanically limiting movement. Neuromodulation addresses pain signalling; it **does not** change the bone or relieve any ongoing nerve compression (§10) or the range-of-motion limitation. The orthopaedic flexion restriction (50–60° in lift) remains decisive.
- **Location matters for the surgical approach.** Mavrogenis 2012 classifies neurogenic hip HO by location — **anterior, posterior, anteromedial, circumferential** — because location determines the surgical approach (and anteromedial/circumferential types had more blood loss). HO described at the **hip/groin** is likely anterior or anteromedial, i.e., in the same region as the obturator/femoral nerve course and the PENG target; the exact type should come from the CT. *[Level 3–4]*
- **Practical questions** this raises (§13): lead path relative to the future surgical approach; whether a **temporary 60-day system** could bridge a defined period without leaving hardware in a future surgical field; infection risk management at HO surgery if a permanent system is present.
- **Pain-free movement and HO.** Better pain control may allow more sitting and active training. It also means pain no longer acts as a warning signal of over-stretching. Madeleine correctly identified HO-type pain as different from her nerve pain **before** imaging confirmed it. Under good analgesia the flexion restriction must be respected by protocol, not by pain. *[Level 5]*

## 13. QUESTIONS WORTH RAISING WITH THE TEAM

Framed as open questions to the treating team — the decisions rest with them.

**About the cause**
1. Is the femoral neuropathy driven by **ongoing compression** by HO, or is it a completed injury? Has CT/MRI shown the relation between the HO and the femoral nerve (MRI visualises the nerve better than CT — Amar 2024)?
2. Given that recurrence after NHO excision does not depend on timing (Genêt 2011) and nerve involvement is an accepted indication, has **earlier excision with femoral neurolysis** been discussed with orthopaedics?

**About the method**
3. **DRG or SCS** — which is favoured, and at which level (L3 ± L2/L4)? The only RCT favours DRG for focal nerve-injury pain (§9.1).
4. Has **femoral PNS** (permanent or 60-day temporary) been considered as a less invasive option or as a bridge until HO surgery?
5. Would **SSEP and femoral/saphenous NCS/EMG with side-to-side comparison** inform method choice, given root/DRG involvement in GBS (§9.4)?

**About the trial and durability**
6. Will there be a temporary-lead trial, for how long, and what counts as success (given the low specificity of screening trials — §9.3)?
7. Expected lead fracture/migration and revision risk with lift transfers, fall risk and later hip surgery?
8. Reversibility if efficacy wanes?

**About practicalities**
9. Which system, and its **MRI conditionality**?
10. **Infection risk** with selective IgA deficiency — any specific prophylaxis?
11. **Timing** relative to Högsbo admission 2027-01-11 and HO surgery.
12. Can TENS and the peripheral catheter continue meanwhile — for how long can the catheter stay?

**About TENS now (§5)**
13. Electrodes on skin with **intact sensation** (bracketing the hypoaesthetic anterior thigh, proximal, or paravertebral L2–L4)? High-frequency (opioid co-treatment), strong-but-comfortable, used during transfers and sitting?

## 14. EVIDENCE GAPS AND HONEST LIMITATIONS

- **No study** of TENS, PNS, DRG or SCS for **heterotopic ossification** pain, and **no series of femoral nerve entrapment by groin HO**.
- **No study** of TENS, PNS, DRG or SCS in patients **after GBS** or with any acquired demyelinating/axonal polyneuropathy in recovery.
- **No direct evidence** that a positive TENS trial or nerve block predicts DRG or SCS success; no study compares PNS with DRG or SCS.
- Most DRG efficacy data are **industry-sponsored**; independent durability data (lead fracture, explant, 3-year failure) are more sobering.
- **Hip-specific PNS data are case reports/series** (Level 4); the stronger PNS evidence comes from other indications (post-amputation, postoperative knee/shoulder/foot, migraine).
- Much of the TENS **parameter evidence** (tolerance, frequency-dependent opioid mechanisms) is from **animal models**.
- Hip capsule innervation is **highly variable** — individual anatomy may not match textbook descriptions, which is why diagnostic blocks matter.
- Several PNS guidelines have authors with **industry relationships** (declared in Strand 2022, Xu 2021); the independent Ontario HTA reached broadly similar conclusions on efficacy but found cost-effectiveness uncertain.
