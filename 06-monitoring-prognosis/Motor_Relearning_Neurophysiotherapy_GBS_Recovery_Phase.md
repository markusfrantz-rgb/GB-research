## 1. SCOPE AND THE CLINICAL QUESTION

A patient recovering from severe GBS with prolonged ICU care typically reaches a phase — commonly between month 4 and month 18 — in which they are medically stable enough to leave acute care but remain severely disabled, dependent for transfers, and in active neurological recovery. At this point a placement decision is made: specialist inpatient rehabilitation, a general ward, a municipal short-term facility, or home with community services.

The decision is frequently framed as a question of **nursing need** ("how much help does this patient require?"). This document argues that the more consequential question is **what is physiologically happening right now, and what does it require?** — because the recovery phase of severe GBS contains a time-limited window in which the quality of therapy determines the ceiling of eventual function.

**The central claim examined here:** during reinnervation, recovery of function is not automatic once axons regrow. The nervous system must re-establish cortical control over reinnervated muscle, and the joints must remain mobile long enough for that control to be usable. Both require active, specific intervention — not passive time.

## 2. THE PHYSIOLOGICAL SUBSTRATE: THREE PARALLEL PROCESSES

Three distinct processes run simultaneously during recovery from severe GBS, on different timescales, and each is addressed by a different therapeutic intervention. Conflating them is the source of most clinical misunderstanding about what physiotherapy is for.

### 2.1 Process A — Peripheral nerve recovery

**Remyelination** (AIDP) proceeds over weeks to months and can produce rapid functional gains once conduction is restored. **Axonal regeneration** (AMAN/AMSAN, or secondary axonal loss in severe AIDP) proceeds at roughly 1 mm/day, meaning reinnervation of distal hand and foot muscles from a proximal lesion takes many months — and explains the characteristic **proximal-to-distal, upper-before-lower** recovery gradient. *[Level 5 — established neurophysiology]*

**Clinical consequence:** the patient whose shoulders have recovered while the fingers remain weak is not plateauing. They are mid-process. The distal muscles have not yet been reached.

### 2.2 Process B — Cortical re-mapping

Reinnervation is not anatomically faithful. Regenerating axons do not reliably reconnect to their original targets; motor units are reorganised, and the cortical representation of the limb has changed during months of absent afferent input and absent movement. The result is that **a muscle may be reinnervated and still not be usable**, because the patient cannot selectively activate it.

This is the process that requires **motor relearning** — the deliberate, repeated attempt to produce a specific movement, with feedback, so that cortical control is re-established over the new peripheral arrangement. *[Level 3 — physiological and observational]*

### 2.3 Process C — Secondary musculoskeletal change

Independent of neurology, immobilisation produces progressive loss of joint range, muscle atrophy, connective tissue shortening and (in the neurologically injured) risk of heterotopic ossification. This process **runs on its own clock and does not wait for nerve recovery.**

### 2.4 The interaction that defines the clinical window

> **Processes A and B can succeed completely and still produce no functional benefit if Process C has rendered the joint immobile.**

A reinnervated wrist in a fixed-flexion contracture is a wrist the patient cannot use. Since Process A continues for 1–2 years in severe GBS, and Process C advances fastest in the early months of immobility, **the recovery phase is a race** — and therapy is what keeps the window open long enough for the neurology to arrive.

## 3. WHY PASSIVE WAITING IS INSUFFICIENT

The intuitive model — "the nerves will regrow, then function returns" — predicts that rehabilitation is useful mainly for conditioning and can be deferred until recovery is more advanced. Three observations contradict it:

1. **Reinnervation is misdirected.** The peripheral arrangement after regeneration differs from the original. Without retraining, the patient must discover new activation strategies unaided, and may instead develop compensatory patterns using stronger proximal muscles — which then persist and limit distal recovery.
2. **Disuse during the waiting period is not neutral.** Muscle that is reinnervated but never activated does not develop usable strength, and the joint it crosses loses range in parallel.
3. **In the only randomised trial of rehabilitation intensity in GBS, intensity mattered** (Section 4.1).

The clinical corollary: **the period of maximal reinnervation is the period of maximal therapeutic opportunity, not a period to wait out.**

## 4. WHAT THE EVIDENCE ACTUALLY SHOWS — INCLUDING WHERE IT IS WEAK

This section is deliberately explicit about the limits of the evidence base. Rehabilitation research in GBS is sparse, and overstating it undermines legitimate clinical arguments.

### 4.1 The only RCT: intensity matters — Khan 2011

A randomised controlled trial of **79 patients** in the chronic phase after GBS, comparing a high-intensity individualised multidisciplinary ambulatory rehabilitation programme (n=40) against a lower-intensity programme (n=39), over **12 months**. *[Level 1b — RCT]*

**The actual intensities matter and are frequently misquoted.** Verified against the full text:

| Arm | What was delivered |
|---|---|
| **"High intensity"** (n=40) | Up to **3 × 1-hour sessions per week**, outpatient, for up to 12 weeks. Half-hour blocks across disciplines (physiotherapy, occupational therapy, psychology, speech, social work) 2–3 times weekly. Goal-oriented, individualised, bi-weekly goal review. Compliance threshold >80 % attendance. |
| **"Low intensity"** (n=39) | Home-based maintenance programme: **30 minutes of physical activity (walking, stretching) twice weekly** plus self-management education and bi-monthly telephone calls. |

So the comparison is **~3 hours/week of multidisciplinary outpatient therapy versus ~1 hour/week of home exercise** — not inpatient versus outpatient, and not high-dose versus moderate-dose.

For orientation, a severely affected inpatient in active rehabilitation may receive on the order of **10–12 hours/week** (e.g. ~2 h on weekdays plus a shorter weekend session), i.e. several times the trial's intervention arm. The trial therefore cannot speak to escalation above ~3 h/week, but it speaks directly to the consequence of falling toward its control arm.

**Two implications follow, and they run in opposite directions:**

1. **This trial does not support escalating therapy above ~3 h/week.** A patient already receiving daily inpatient physiotherapy receives several times the "high-intensity" arm. Citing this trial as evidence that such a patient needs *more* misrepresents it.
2. **It is direct evidence against de-escalation to a maintenance model.** The control arm — twice-weekly walking and stretching at home with telephone follow-up — is essentially the service model of community or municipal maintenance rehabilitation. That arm achieved **half the rate of functional improvement**. Where a placement decision would move a patient from daily multidisciplinary therapy toward a maintenance programme, this trial describes the expected consequence.

| Outcome | Result |
|---|---|
| **Improvement in FIM (function)** | **68 % (treatment) vs 32 % (control)** |
| FIM domains improved | Mobility, transfers, sphincter control, locomotion (all **p < 0.005**) |
| Participation (PIPP relationships) | p = 0.011 |
| Effect sizes | Moderate to small (r = 0.36–0.23) |

**Conclusion of the authors:** higher-intensity rehabilitation reduces disability in people with GBS *in later stages of recovery* compared with less intense intervention.

This is the single most directly relevant trial in the literature, and it supports the proposition that **the dose and content of rehabilitation change the outcome** — not merely the presence of rehabilitation. Note also that the inferior arm's content was *walking and stretching*, consistent with the contracture evidence in Section 5: maintenance-style passive and generic exercise is the weaker model on two independent lines of evidence.

> **Citation:** Khan F, Pallant JF, Amatya B, Ng L, Gorelik A, Brand C. Outcomes of high- and low-intensity rehabilitation programme for persons in chronic phase after Guillain-Barré syndrome: a randomized controlled trial. *J Rehabil Med*. 2011;43(7):638-646. PMID: 21667009

### 4.2 The Cochrane review: low-quality evidence pointing the same way

The Cochrane review of multidisciplinary care in GBS (search to May 2010) **found no randomised or controlled clinical trials**. Best available evidence came from **three observational studies rated "very low quality"**, which provided some support for improved disability in the short term (< 6 months) with **high-intensity inpatient multidisciplinary rehabilitation**, and for improved quality of life. The authors described their conclusions as tentative. *[Level 2a — systematic review of very low quality evidence]*

**Interpretation:** the direction of evidence is consistent (higher intensity, inpatient, multidisciplinary → better function), but the certainty is low. Khan 2011 subsequently supplied the missing RCT.

> **Citation:** Khan F, Ng L, Amatya B, Brand C, Turner-Stokes L. Multidisciplinary care for Guillain-Barré syndrome. *Cochrane Database Syst Rev*. 2010;(10):CD008505. PMID: 20927774

### 4.3 Exercise reduces fatigue — and not via fitness

A 12-week bicycle exercise programme in 20 severely fatigued patients (16 GBS with relatively good recovery, 4 stable CIDP) produced a **20 % reduction in fatigue severity**, with improvements in fitness, functional outcome and quality of life, and was well tolerated. *[Level 4 — intervention case series]*

A subsequent analysis of the same cohort found that **changes in fatigue, actual mobility and perceived functioning were largely not explained by changes in physical fitness** — a small proportion of relationships between the fitness domain and the other domains reached significance (2/30, 7 %). The authors concluded that physical training has important additional effects not mediated by fitness gains. *[Level 4]*

**Clinical reading:** the benefit of structured therapy in this population is not reducible to conditioning. This argues against substituting generic exercise for targeted neurological rehabilitation.

> **Citation:** Garssen MPJ, Bussmann JBJ, Schmitz PIM, et al. Physical training and fatigue, fitness, and quality of life in Guillain-Barré syndrome and CIDP. *Neurology*. 2004;63(12):2393-2395. PMID: 15623709

> **Citation:** Bussmann JBJ, Garssen MPJ, van Doorn PA, Stam HJ. Analysing the favourable effects of physical exercise: relationships between physical fitness, fatigue and functioning in Guillain-Barré syndrome and chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy. *J Rehabil Med*. 2007;39(2):121-125. PMID: 17351693

### 4.4 Systematic reviews: consistent direction, insufficient certainty

A systematic review of RCTs of rehabilitation in GBS concluded that **definite conclusions on effectiveness cannot be extrapolated** and that high-quality studies are needed. *[Level 2a]*

A 2025 scoping review (16 papers from 1021 screened) concluded that physical exercise **may** enhance strength, reduce fatigue and promote functional independence in GBS, with recommended interventions including muscle strengthening, functional training and endurance exercise — again calling for larger studies. *[Level 2a]*

> **Citation:** Galeoto G, et al. The efficacy of rehabilitation in people with Guillain-Barré syndrome: a systematic review of randomized controlled trials. *Expert Rev Neurother*. 2021;21(4):455-461. PMID: 33567916

> **Citation:** Physical Exercise in Guillain-Barré Syndrome: A Scoping Review. *J Clin Med*. 2025;14(8):2655. PMID: 40283485

### 4.5 Documented recovery trajectory in BBE-GBS overlap

A published case of BBE-GBS overlap (24-year-old woman, MRI with bilateral posterior thalamic and periaqueductal T2 hyperintensity) admitted to rehabilitation **on day 43 with FIM 19/126** — the FIM floor is 18, i.e. total dependence in every domain: *[Level 4]*

| Timepoint | MRC upper limb | MRC lower limb | FIM (/126) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Day 43 (rehabilitation admission) | 18/30 | 9/30 | **19** |
| 9 months | 24/30 | 14/30 | 107 |
| 4.5 years | 30/30 | 28/30 | **123** |

Recovery pattern **proximal → distal, upper → lower**; ankle dorsiflexion last. Persistent cognitive deficits (attention, processing speed, dual-tasking) at 4.5 years despite near-complete motor recovery.

**Two clinical points follow.** First, total dependence and high nursing need did not preclude admission to specialist rehabilitation — the patient was admitted *in that state*, and the rehabilitation is what produced the change. Second, the recovery gradient is predictable, which means a patient with recovered shoulders and weak hands is at an expected intermediate stage rather than at a plateau.

> **Citation:** Lombet C, et al. BBE-GBS overlap rehabilitation. *Rev Med Liege*. 2021;76(7-8):608-613. PMID: 34357713

## 5. CONTRACTURE PREVENTION: THE EVIDENCE IS NOT WHAT MOST CLINICIANS ASSUME

This section matters because the common argument — *"the patient needs physiotherapy to stretch the joints and prevent contractures"* — is, as stated, **not supported by high-quality evidence**, and a well-informed clinician will say so. The defensible argument is different and stronger.

### 5.1 What the Cochrane evidence shows

**Stretch (Harvey 2017):** 49 studies, 2135 participants. No study applied stretch for more than seven months. The review found **high-quality evidence that stretch does not have clinically important short-term effects on joint mobility** in people with or at risk of contractures. *[Level 1a — Cochrane systematic review, high-quality evidence of absence of effect]*

**Passive movements (2013):** two trials, 122 participants with neurological conditions, both at low risk of bias. One within-participant study (20 participants, 40 limbs) found a mean between-group difference in ankle mobility of **four degrees (95 % CI 2–6)** — statistically detectable, clinically marginal. *[Level 1a]*

> **Citation:** Harvey LA, Katalinic OM, Herbert RD, Moseley AM, Lannin NA, Schurr K. Stretch for the treatment and prevention of contractures. *Cochrane Database Syst Rev*. 2017;1:CD007455. PMID: 28146605

> **Citation:** Prabhu RKR, Swaminathan N, Harvey LA. Passive movements for the treatment and prevention of contractures. *Cochrane Database Syst Rev*. 2013;(12):CD009331. PMID: 24374605

### 5.2 What follows from this — and what does not

**What does not follow:** that immobilisation is harmless, or that joint range can be ignored. The natural history of immobilisation-induced range loss is well described, and contractures in long-stay ICU populations are common and functionally consequential.

**What does follow:** that **passive stretching delivered by an attendant is not an effective substitute for active movement.** A service model in which "a physiotherapist comes by and moves the joints" is precisely the model Cochrane found wanting.

### 5.3 The defensible formulation

> *Passive stretch alone has high-quality evidence against clinically meaningful benefit. What preserves usable range in a recovering neurological patient is active and assisted-active movement through range, functional use of the limb, positioning, and orthotic management — i.e. the content of specialist neurological physiotherapy, not the passive component of it.*

This is a stronger argument for specialist input than the contracture-prevention claim it replaces, because it identifies what general or intermittent therapy cannot deliver.

## 6. OVERWORK WEAKNESS: THE COUNTERWEIGHT

A document arguing for intensity must address the opposing concern. **Overwork weakness** — prolonged reduction in strength and endurance following exertion, potentially becoming permanent — is a long-standing concern in partially denervated muscle, developed chiefly in the post-polio literature. The proposed mechanism is that surviving motor neurons supporting enlarged motor units through collateral sprouting cannot meet the metabolic demand, leading to terminal sprout dieback. *[Level 4–5 — observational and mechanistic, largely extrapolated from post-polio]*

**Honest appraisal:** the evidence specific to GBS is thin, and the post-polio model (chronic, decades-long overuse of a stable denervated population) differs substantially from acute GBS (active reinnervation with recovering nerve supply). The GBS exercise literature (Section 4.3) reports training as well tolerated.

**Practical implication:** this is an argument for *supervised, graded, individually titrated* training — which is what a specialist service provides — and not an argument against intensity per se. Markers to monitor: strength that is lower the day after a session than before it, prolonged post-exertional fatigue, new muscle pain or cramping.

> This is precisely the judgement that cannot be delegated to unsupervised activity or to generic exercise. It requires a therapist who can distinguish therapeutic fatigue from overwork in a reinnervating muscle.

## 7. THE THREE FUNCTIONS OF NEUROLOGICAL PHYSIOTHERAPY

| # | Function | Target process | What it looks like | Evidence base |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Range of movement** | Process C (musculoskeletal) | Active and assisted-active movement through range; positioning; orthoses; functional use. **Not passive stretch alone.** | Cochrane negative for passive stretch alone; natural-history data for immobilisation |
| **2** | **Strength and endurance** | Process A output | Graded strengthening of reinnervating muscle, titrated against overwork; endurance work | Garssen 2004; scoping review 2025 |
| **3** | **Motor relearning** | Process B (cortical) | High-repetition low-load activation of specific muscles; task-specific practice; feedback (visual, tactile, EMG-biofeedback); progression from assisted to independent activation | Mechanistic + rehabilitation-medicine practice; weakest formal evidence, highest specialist dependence |

**Function 3 is the one that is unavailable outside specialist services and the one most often omitted from placement discussions.** It is also the function with the least robust trial evidence — which should be stated plainly rather than overstated. The argument for it is mechanistic and practice-based: a reinnervated muscle that the patient cannot selectively activate confers no function, and relearning selective activation is a trainable skill.

## 8. WHAT DISTINGUISHES SPECIALIST NEUROPHYSIOTHERAPY FROM GENERAL PHYSIOTHERAPY

For placement decisions, the operative question is not "is there a physiotherapist?" but "what will that physiotherapist do, how often, and with what competence?"

| | General / municipal service | Specialist neurological rehabilitation |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary purpose** | Assessment, aids, maintenance, safe transfers | Change in function over a defined period against goals |
| **Frequency** | Episodic; assessment-driven | Daily, often multiple sessions |
| **Motor relearning** | Not typically within scope | Core activity |
| **Titration against overwork** | Not routinely monitored | Session-by-session judgement |
| **Team** | Physiotherapist, occupational therapist | Rehabilitation physician, nurse, PT, OT, speech therapist, neuropsychologist, social worker |
| **Goal structure** | Function maintained, aids provided | Written rehabilitation plan with measurable goals and review |
| **Response to complications** | Refer onward | Managed within the team |

**The decision-relevant question to put to a placement meeting:**

> *"How many hours of therapy per week, delivered by which professions, will the patient receive at the proposed destination — and who will deliver motor relearning as the wrists and hands reinnervate?"*

If the answer represents a reduction from the patient's current provision, the transfer is a **de-escalation of active treatment during the phase of maximal reinnervation**, and should be documented as such.

## 9. IMPLICATIONS FOR PLACEMENT DECISIONS

### 9.1 "High nursing need" is not a contraindication to rehabilitation

The use of hoist transfers, two-person handling, or full assistance with personal care describes the patient's **current dependency**, which is the problem rehabilitation addresses — not a reason to withhold it. The reference case (Section 4.5) was admitted to rehabilitation at FIM 19, requiring assistance in every domain, and reached FIM 123.

Where dependency is cited as grounds for declining admission, the clinically coherent question is: *what level of dependency would qualify, and what is the mechanism by which dependency is expected to reduce without specialist input?*

### 9.2 "Not yet ready" requires a defined threshold

A readiness criterion that is not stated in advance, in full and in writing, cannot be worked toward. Where successive declines cite different criteria that the patient then meets, the clinically appropriate response is to request **the complete criterion set at once**, together with the planned date of re-assessment.

International practice for inpatient rehabilitation admission commonly references the ability to tolerate on the order of **3 hours of therapy per day** and willingness to participate. A patient already completing ~2 hours of daily therapy (gym work included), who requests more, is close to that threshold without having reached it — and tolerance is itself trainable, which argues for admission rather than against it.

### 9.3 Timing

Process A continues for 1–2 years. Process C advances fastest early. The window in which therapy changes the functional ceiling is therefore **during**, not after, reinnervation. Deferring specialist rehabilitation until the patient "is stronger" inverts the physiology.

## 10. EVIDENCE GAPS AND HONEST LIMITATIONS

Stated plainly, because a clinical argument built on overstated evidence collapses on contact with a well-read specialist:

| Claim | Strength | Caveat |
|---|---|---|
| ~3 h/week multidisciplinary outpatient rehabilitation outperforms ~1 h/week home maintenance exercise in chronic-phase GBS | **Moderate** | One RCT (n=79); effect sizes small-to-moderate; **does not address inpatient or higher doses** |
| Daily inpatient therapy outperforms ~3 h/week outpatient therapy | **Not studied** | No trial compares these; inference only |
| Exercise reduces fatigue in GBS | **Low–moderate** | Case series, n=20, selected for severe fatigue and good recovery |
| Passive stretch prevents contractures | **Evidence against** | Cochrane: high-quality evidence of no clinically important effect |
| Active movement and functional use preserve range | **Low** | Inferred; little direct trial evidence in this population |
| Motor relearning improves outcome after reinnervation | **Low (mechanistic)** | No RCT in GBS; strongest support is physiological reasoning and rehabilitation-medicine practice |
| Higher dose of task-specific training improves outcome | **Insufficient** | Stroke meta-analysis (26 RCTs, 1431 participants) found insufficient evidence on dose; only hand function >20 sessions (SMD 0.57, p=0.02) and arm function >50 repetitions (p=0.001, very low quality) |
| Overwork weakness limits safe intensity in GBS | **Low** | Extrapolated from post-polio; GBS exercise studies report good tolerance |

> **Citation:** Intensity of task-specific training for functional ability post-stroke: systematic review and meta-analysis. *Clin Rehabil*. 2025. PMID: 40576954

**The overall position this evidence supports:** the direction of benefit is consistent across every study type, the single relevant RCT favours higher intensity, and the physiological rationale for motor relearning during reinnervation is sound — but the certainty is moderate at best, and the optimal dose is not established. Absence of precise dose evidence is not evidence that dose is irrelevant.
