## 1. Clinical Significance

### 1.1 Why This Combination Matters

The co-occurrence of Guillain-Barre syndrome (GBS) and selective IgA deficiency (SIgAD) creates a clinically significant treatment dilemma:

- **First-line GBS treatment is IVIg** (intravenous immunoglobulin, 0.4 g/kg/day x 5 days)
- **IgA-deficient patients may have anti-IgA antibodies** (20-40% prevalence) that cause anaphylaxis to IVIg
- This creates a scenario where the standard treatment for a potentially life-threatening neurological emergency may itself be life-threatening

### 1.2 Epidemiological Context

- **SIgAD prevalence**: ~1:300-1:700 in Caucasians (most common primary immunodeficiency)
- **GBS incidence**: ~1-2 per 100,000 per year
- **Expected co-occurrence**: Approximately 1 case per 25-50 million person-years
- This rarity explains the absence of large case series or randomized trials for this specific combination

### 1.3 Literature Overview

**There are no major society guidelines that specifically address the GBS + IgA deficiency combination as a distinct clinical entity.** Management is extrapolated from two separate bodies of evidence:
1. GBS treatment evidence (Level 1: multiple RCTs, Cochrane reviews)
2. IgA deficiency and IVIg reaction evidence (Level 3-4: case series, systematic reviews)

## 2. The Core Treatment Dilemma

### 2.1 Standard GBS Treatment Options

Both IVIg and plasma exchange (PE) are established first-line treatments for GBS with equivalent efficacy *[Level 1 — Cochrane systematiska reviews]*:

- **IVIg**: 0.4 g/kg/day for 5 consecutive days (total 2 g/kg)
- **PE**: 5 exchanges over 7-14 days, 200-250 mL/kg total plasma volume

This equivalence is crucial because it means PE is NOT a "second-line fallback" -- it is a legitimate and equally effective first-line choice.

> **Key references:**
> - Hughes RAC, Swan AV, van Doorn PA. "Intravenous immunoglobulin for Guillain-Barre syndrome." *Cochrane Database Syst Rev.* 2014;(9):CD002063. DOI: 10.1002/14651858.CD002063.pub6
> - Raphael JC, Chevret S, Hughes RAC, Annane D. "Plasma exchange for Guillain-Barre syndrome." *Cochrane Database Syst Rev.* 2012;(7):CD001798.
> - van der Meche FGA, Schmitz PIM; Dutch Guillain-Barre Study Group. "A randomized trial comparing intravenous immune globulin and plasma exchange in Guillain-Barre syndrome." *N Engl J Med.* 1992;326(17):1123-1129.

### 2.2 The Problem with IVIg in IgA Deficiency

Standard IVIg preparations contain variable amounts of IgA:
- **Octagam**: ~200 mcg/mL
- **Gamunex-C**: ~46 mcg/mL
- **Privigen**: ~25 mcg/mL
- **Flebogamma DIF 5%**: <50 mcg/mL
- **Gammagard S/D** (lyophilized): <1 mcg/mL (IgA-depleted)
- **GAMMAGARD LIQUID ERC** (new, 2025): <=2 mcg/mL (low-IgA liquid)

Even small amounts of IgA can trigger severe reactions in sensitized patients.

### 2.3 IVIg Contraindication Criteria

IVIg is contraindicated in patients who:
1. Have selective IgA deficiency WITH IgE-class anti-IgA antibodies
2. Have a previous history of severe systemic or anaphylactic responses to IVIg
3. Have documented hypersensitivity to immunoglobulin products

> **Citation:** Orange JS, Hossny EM, Weiler CR, et al. "Use of intravenous immunoglobulin in human disease: A review of evidence by members of the Primary Immunodeficiency Committee of the American Academy of Allergy, Asthma and Immunology." *J Allergy Clin Immunol.* 2006;117(4 Suppl):S525-S553.

## 3. Anti-IgA Antibodies and Anaphylaxis Risk

### 3.1 Prevalence of Anti-IgA Antibodies

| Patient Population | Anti-IgA Prevalence | Reference |
|---|---|---|
| Selective IgA deficiency | 20-40% (IgG class) | Ferreira et al., 1988; Horn et al., 2007 |
| Selective IgA deficiency (sensitive methods) | ~25% (any measurable level) | Rachid & Bonilla, 2012 (PMID: 21835445) |
| CVID patients | 9-25% | Horn et al., 2007 |
| Healthy controls | ~2% | Multiple studies |
| IgA-deficient patients (IgE class) | Rare (exact prevalence unknown) | Burks et al., 1986 |

**Note:** Prevalence figures vary substantially depending on measurement method and sensitivity threshold. Rachid & Bonilla (2012) emphasized that detection of anti-IgA antibodies does not equate to clinical risk -- the isotype, specificity, and concentration all determine whether a reaction will occur.

### 3.2 Types of Anti-IgA Antibodies and Clinical Significance

| Antibody Type | Mechanism | Risk Level | Clinical Significance |
|---|---|---|---|
| IgG anti-IgA (class-specific) | Immune complex formation | Moderate | Most common; may cause mild-moderate reactions |
| IgG anti-IgA (subclass-specific) | Immune complex formation | Lower | Directed against specific IgA subclasses |
| IgE anti-IgA | Type I hypersensitivity (mast cell degranulation) | HIGHEST | True anaphylaxis with cardiovascular collapse |

### 3.3 Challenging the Dogma: Rachid & Bonilla 2012

A landmark comprehensive review *[Level 3 — systematic review of case reports and case series]* challenged the traditional view that anti-IgA antibodies invariably cause anaphylaxis:

**Key findings:**
- The actual risk of severe anaphylaxis from anti-IgA antibodies is **much lower than traditionally believed**
- Many patients with documented anti-IgA antibodies tolerate IVIg without reaction
- The causal link between anti-IgA antibodies and anaphylactic reactions remains **controversial** -- no definitive consensus exists
- ~25% of IgA-deficient patients have measurable anti-IgA levels when sensitive detection methods are used, yet severe reactions remain rare
- Multiple factors determine risk: serum antibody concentration, isotype (IgG vs IgE), specificity (class-specific vs subclass-specific), measurement method sensitivity, IgA content of the product, and route of administration
- The review highlights that detection of anti-IgA antibodies alone is insufficient to predict clinical reactions -- the clinical context and antibody characteristics must be interpreted together

> **Citation:** Rachid R, Bonilla FA. "The role of anti-IgA antibodies in causing adverse reactions to gamma globulin infusion in immunodeficient patients: a comprehensive review of the literature." *J Allergy Clin Immunol.* 2012;129(3):628-634. PMID: 21835445. DOI: 10.1016/j.jaci.2011.06.047

### 3.4 Recent Evidence (2024)

A 2024 review in *Clinical Reviews in Allergy & Immunology* further examined non-allergic hypersensitivity reactions to immunoglobulin preparations:

- Confirmed that anti-IgA antibodies are significantly more prevalent in patients experiencing anaphylaxis compared to those without
- However, the association remains incompletely understood
- Multiple non-IgA-mediated mechanisms may also cause reactions (complement activation by IgG aggregates, rate-related reactions, anti-allotype antibodies)

> **Citation:** "Non-allergic Hypersensitivity Reactions to Immunoglobulin Preparations in Antibody Deficiencies: What Role for Anti-IgA IgG and Complement Activation?" *Clin Rev Allergy Immunol.* 2024. DOI: 10.1007/s12016-024-09007-0

### 3.5 Anaphylaxis Incidence

- Overall IVIg anaphylaxis: 1:20,000 to 1:47,000 transfusions
- Most anaphylactic reactions occurred with products containing >50 mcg/mL IgA, possibly suggesting a threshold phenomenon
- Some patients tolerated products with the same or higher IgA content after premedication

> **Citation:** "Anaphylaxis to IVIG." *Arch Immunol Ther Exp.* 2016;64:439-454. DOI: 10.1007/s00005-016-0410-1

## 4. IgA-Depleted Immunoglobulin Products

### 4.1 Available Products

#### Gammagard S/D (Lyophilized) -- Baxter/Takeda
- **IgA content**: <1 mcg/mL
- **Status**: Historically the gold-standard IgA-depleted product
- **Limitation**: Lyophilized formulation requires reconstitution; availability has been inconsistent in various markets
- **Indication**: Primary immunodeficiency, CIDP

#### GAMMAGARD LIQUID ERC -- Takeda (NEW)
- **IgA content**: <=2 mcg/mL
- **FDA approval**: June 30, 2025
- **US availability**: January 22, 2026
- **EU name**: DEQSIGA (approved, planned rollout 2027)
- **Key advantage**: Only ready-to-use LIQUID immunoglobulin therapy with low IgA content -- no reconstitution needed
- **Indication**: Replacement therapy for primary immunodeficiency (age >=2)
- **Administration**: IV or subcutaneous
- **Clinical significance for GBS+IgA-deficiency**: This product represents a major advance, providing a practical low-IgA IVIg option that doesn't require lyophilized preparation

> **Citation:** Takeda Pharmaceuticals. "Takeda Announces U.S. Availability of GAMMAGARD LIQUID ERC." Press release, January 22, 2026. Available at: https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260114621969/

### 4.3 Pre-Medication Protocols for IVIg in IgA-Deficient Patients

When IVIg must be administered to an IgA-deficient patient (e.g., PE unavailable):

1. **Use IgA-depleted product** (Gammagard S/D or GAMMAGARD LIQUID ERC)
2. **Pre-treatment**:
   - Hydrocortisone 100-200 mg IV
   - Diphenhydramine 50 mg IV (or equivalent antihistamine)
   - Acetaminophen 1000 mg PO
3. **Test dose**: Initial infusion at 0.5 mL/kg/hr for 30 minutes
4. **Slow titration**: Gradually increase rate if tolerated
5. **Emergency preparedness**: Epinephrine at bedside, crash cart available
6. **Monitoring**: Continuous monitoring for at least first 30 minutes

### 4.4 Subcutaneous Immunoglobulin (SCIG) as Safer Alternative

SCIG may be safer than IVIg for IgA-deficient patients because:
- Slower absorption from subcutaneous tissue reduces peak serum IgA levels
- Systemic reactions are less common
- Desensitization may occur over time with repeated small-volume exposures

> **Citation:** Sundin U, Nava S, Hammarstrom L. "Induction of unresponsiveness against IgA in IgA-deficient patients on subcutaneous immunoglobulin infusion." *Clin Exp Immunol.* 1998;112(2):341-346.

## 5. Plasmapheresis as Alternative First-Line

### 5.1 Rationale

In the GBS + IgA deficiency scenario, PE is the logical and evidence-supported first-line choice:
- Avoids the IgA-related anaphylaxis risk entirely
- Equivalent efficacy to IVIg per Cochrane evidence (Level 1)
- Directly removes pathogenic anti-ganglioside antibodies
- No immunoglobulin product exposure required

### 5.2 Standard PE Protocol for GBS

| Severity | Number of Exchanges | Schedule |
|---|---|---|
| Mild (able to walk) | 2 exchanges | Over 7-14 days |
| Moderate-Severe (unable to walk) | 4-5 exchanges | Over 7-14 days |
| Ventilator-dependent | 5-6 exchanges | Over 7-14 days |

**Volume**: 200-250 mL/kg total plasma volume exchanged
**Replacement fluid**: 5% albumin (avoid fresh frozen plasma in IgA-deficient patients as it contains IgA)

### 5.3 Evidence Base

- **French Cooperative Group on Plasma Exchange in GBS**: Established PE efficacy. "Efficiency of plasma exchange in Guillain-Barre syndrome: role of replacement fluids." *Ann Neurol.* 1987;22(6):753-761.
- **GBS Study Group**: "Plasmapheresis and acute Guillain-Barre syndrome." *Neurology.* 1985;35(8):1096-1104.
- **Cochrane Review**: Raphael JC et al. Confirmed PE efficacy equivalent to IVIg. *Cochrane Database Syst Rev.* 2012;(7):CD001798.

### 5.4 Practical Considerations

**Advantages of PE in this context:**
- No IgA exposure risk
- Rapid removal of pathogenic antibodies
- Well-established safety profile

**Disadvantages/limitations:**
- Requires specialized equipment and trained staff
- Not available in all hospitals, particularly smaller/rural centers
- Hemodynamic instability in severely ill patients
- Central venous access risks (thrombosis, infection)
- Coagulation factor depletion
- May be challenging in pediatric patients or elderly

## 6. Clinical Decision Framework

### 6.1 Algorithm for GBS + Known or Suspected IgA Deficiency

```
GBS diagnosed + IgA deficiency known/suspected
|
+-- Step 1: Is plasmapheresis available?
|   |
|   +-- YES --> Proceed with PE as first-line
|   |           (5 exchanges over 7-14 days)
|   |           This is equivalent to IVIg per Cochrane evidence
|   |           No IgA-related risk
|   |           Use ALBUMIN (not FFP) as replacement fluid
|   |
|   +-- NO --> Go to Step 2
|
+-- Step 2: Check anti-IgA antibody status
|   |
|   +-- Anti-IgA IgE POSITIVE (highest risk)
|   |   --> Transfer to center with PE capability
|   |   --> If impossible: IgA-depleted IVIg (Gammagard S/D or
|   |       GAMMAGARD LIQUID ERC) with full anaphylaxis precautions
|   |   --> Consider complement inhibitor if available (investigational)
|   |
|   +-- Anti-IgA IgG only, no IgE (moderate risk)
|   |   --> IgA-depleted IVIg preferred
|   |   --> Standard IVIg with premedication and slow infusion
|   |       may be considered if IgA-depleted product unavailable
|   |   --> Monitor closely for first 30 minutes
|   |
|   +-- Anti-IgA NEGATIVE (lower risk)
|   |   --> Standard IVIg likely safe
|   |   --> Premedicate and monitor as routine
|   |   --> Consider IgA-depleted product if available
|   |
|   +-- Anti-IgA status UNKNOWN (cannot test urgently)
|       --> PE preferred if available
|       --> If PE unavailable: IgA-depleted IVIg with full precautions
|       --> Do NOT delay treatment for anti-IgA testing
|
+-- Step 3: Treatment failure (no improvement by day 7-14)?
|   --> Do NOT repeat IVIg (per SID-GBS trial, Walgaard 2021)
|   --> Consider PE if IVIg was used first (or vice versa)
|   --> Consider tanruprubart/ANX005 (if available, investigational)
|   --> Consider efgartigimod (case-report level evidence)
|   --> Supportive care, ICU management
|
+-- Step 4: Screen for IgA deficiency BEFORE IVIg in ALL GBS patients?
    --> Current evidence does NOT support universal screening
    --> Screen if: history of transfusion reaction, known immunodeficiency,
        family history of IgA deficiency, associated autoimmune conditions
    --> Routine immunoglobulin panel (IgG, IgA, IgM) is recommended as
        part of GBS workup per EAN/PNS 2023 guideline
```

### 6.2 Important Considerations

- **Time is critical**: GBS can progress rapidly to respiratory failure. Treatment should NOT be delayed for anti-IgA testing
- **Replacement fluid for PE**: Use 5% albumin, NOT fresh frozen plasma (FFP contains IgA)
- **Blood products**: If transfusions needed, use washed red blood cells and washed platelets to reduce IgA exposure
- **Pre-IVIg screening**: The EAN/PNS 2023 guideline recommends routine blood tests including immunoglobulin levels as part of GBS workup, which would identify IgA deficiency

## 7. Treatment-Resistant Scenarios

### 7.1 When Standard Treatment Fails in GBS + IgA Deficiency

Treatment resistance is particularly challenging in this combination because:
1. If PE was used as first-line (due to IgA deficiency), the usual "switch to the other treatment" option (IVIg) may be contraindicated
2. Second IVIg course is NOT recommended regardless (SID-GBS trial)
3. Combined PE + IVIg is NOT recommended (no additional benefit)

### 7.2 Options for Refractory GBS in IgA-Deficient Patients

| Treatment | IgA-Safe? | Evidence Level | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Plasmapheresis (if not yet tried) | Yes | Level 1 | First choice if IVIg was used initially |
| Tanruprubart/ANX005 (anti-C1q) | Yes | Level 2 (Phase 3 positive) | Monoclonal antibody; no IgA content |
| Efgartigimod (anti-FcRn) | Yes | Level 4 (case reports) | Monoclonal antibody; no IgA content |
| Imlifidase (IgG-cleaving enzyme) | Yes | Level 2 (Phase 2) | Cleaves pathogenic IgG; no IgA content |
| Eculizumab (anti-C5) | Yes | Level 2 (Phase 3 negative) | Monoclonal antibody; failed Phase 3 |
| Rituximab (anti-CD20) | Yes | Level 4 (case reports) | Monoclonal antibody; especially for panneurofascin+ GBS |
| Cyclophosphamide | Yes | Level 4 (case reports) | May be useful in SLE-associated GBS |
| IgA-depleted IVIg | Conditional | Level 1 (for IVIg) | Use Gammagard S/D or GAMMAGARD LIQUID ERC |

### 7.3 Recommended Approach for Refractory Cases

1. **Confirm diagnosis**: Rule out acute-onset CIDP (>3 fluctuations or progression >8 weeks)
2. **If PE was first-line**: Consider IgA-depleted IVIg with precautions
3. **If IVIg was first-line**: Switch to PE
4. **If both failed**: Consider tanruprubart (if accessible through compassionate use or trial) or efgartigimod
5. **For panneurofascin-antibody positive cases**: Consider rituximab
6. **Intensive supportive care**: Ventilatory support, ICU management, rehabilitation

## 8. IgA-Safe Emerging Therapies

### 8.1 Tanruprubart (ANX005) -- Anti-C1q Complement Inhibitor

**The most promising new GBS therapy and first with positive Phase 3 results.**

- **Mechanism**: Monoclonal antibody blocking C1q, the initiating protein of the classical complement pathway
- **Phase 3 results (2025)**: Single 30 mg/kg dose showed OR 2.4 (95% CI 1.29-4.50; p=0.0058) for improved GBS Disability Scale at Week 8
- **Additional benefits**: Patients walked independently 31 days earlier (p=0.0211); 28 fewer days on mechanical ventilation (p=0.0356)
- **Safety**: Transient infusion-related reactions in 35%; no increased mortality or infection
- **IgA safety**: Monoclonal antibody -- contains NO IgA. Completely safe for IgA-deficient patients
- **Regulatory status**: EMA MAA submission Q1 2026; FDA BLA discussion ongoing
- **Clinical significance for GBS+IgA**: Could become the ideal treatment for this combination -- effective, targeted, and IgA-safe

> **Citations:**
> - Annexon Biosciences. "Annexon Announces Positive Topline Results from Pivotal Phase 3 Trial for First-in-Class C1q Blocking Antibody ANX005 in Guillain-Barre Syndrome." 2024.
> - "Efficacy and Safety of Targeted Immunotherapy with ANX005 in Treating Guillain-Barre Syndrome: A Phase 3 Multicenter Study (PL5.006)." *Neurology.* 2025. DOI: 10.1212/WNL.0000000000212033

### 8.2 Efgartigimod (Vyvgart) -- FcRn Inhibitor

- **Mechanism**: Blocks neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn), accelerating degradation of pathogenic IgG antibodies
- **Evidence in GBS**: Case reports showing rapid neurological recovery; Phase 2 clinical trial ongoing (NCT05701189)
- **Retrospective data (2026)**: 17-patient analysis showed significantly faster improvement (4 days vs comparator groups) in *Scientific Reports*
- **Case reports**: Effective in refractory GBS, MFS-GBS overlap with poor IVIg response, and as add-on treatment
- **IgA safety**: Monoclonal antibody -- NO IgA content. Safe for IgA-deficient patients
- **Dosing**: 10 mg/kg IV, 2-4 doses

> **Citations:**
> - "Efgartigimod for treating Guillain-Barre syndrome with poor response to intravenous immunoglobulin: a case report." *BMC Neurol.* 2025. DOI: 10.1186/s12883-025-04412-0
> - Ripellino P, et al. "Efgartigimod as Add-On Treatment in Refractory Guillain-Barre Syndrome: A Case Report." *Eur J Neurol.* 2025. DOI: 10.1111/ene.70308
> - "Rapid neurological recovery in Guillain-Barre syndrome treated with efgartigimod." *Sci Rep.* 2026. DOI: 10.1038/s41598-026-44163-7

### 8.3 Imlifidase -- IgG-Cleaving Enzyme

- **Mechanism**: Endopeptidase from Streptococcus pyogenes that rapidly cleaves all IgG molecules, neutralizing pathogenic antibodies within hours
- **Phase 2 results (2024)**: Patients returned to independent walking 6 weeks sooner than IGOS comparators; 37% walking independently after 1 week
- **IgA safety**: Specifically cleaves IgG only -- does NOT interact with IgA. Safe for IgA-deficient patients
- **Unique advantage**: Rapid onset (hours), combined with IVIg for sustained effect
- **Developer**: Hansa Biopharma

> **Citation:** Hansa Biopharma. "Positive full results from 15-HMedIdeS-09 Phase 2 study of imlifidase in patients with Guillain-Barre Syndrome." December 2024.

### 8.4 Summary: Emerging Therapy IgA Safety

All three emerging therapies (tanruprubart, efgartigimod, imlifidase) are IgA-safe because they are either monoclonal antibodies or enzymes -- NOT pooled human immunoglobulin products. This makes them particularly attractive for the GBS + IgA deficiency population.

## 9. Shared Immunological Mechanisms

### 9.1 Why Might IgA Deficiency and GBS Co-occur?

#### Shared Autoimmune Predisposition
- SIgAD is associated with increased susceptibility to autoimmune diseases generally (20-30% develop autoimmune conditions)
- GBS is an autoimmune-mediated neuropathy
- Both conditions share features of immune dysregulation

#### HLA Associations
- **SIgAD**: Strongly associated with HLA-A1, B8, DR3 (the "8.1 ancestral haplotype") -- present in ~45% of patients
- **GBS**: Weaker HLA associations; some studies show HLA-DR and HLA-DQ associations, particularly in post-Campylobacter GBS
- **Overlap**: The 8.1 ancestral haplotype is associated with multiple autoimmune conditions that are themselves associated with GBS

> **Citations:**
> - Hammarstrom L, Vorechovsky I, Webster D. "Selective IgA deficiency (SIgAD) and common variable immunodeficiency (CVID)." *Clin Exp Immunol.* 2000;120(2):225-231.
> - Geleijns K, et al. "HLA class II alleles are not a general susceptibility factor in Guillain-Barre syndrome." *Neurology.* 2005;64(1):44-49.

#### Mucosal Immunity and Molecular Mimicry
- IgA is the primary mucosal immunoglobulin; its deficiency impairs mucosal barrier function
- GBS is frequently triggered by mucosal infections (C. jejuni gastroenteritis, respiratory infections)
- **Hypothesis**: IgA deficiency could predispose to more severe or altered mucosal infections, potentially increasing molecular mimicry risk and GBS susceptibility
- **Caveat**: This has NOT been demonstrated epidemiologically

#### Immune Dysregulation Pathways
- IgA deficiency: Defects in B-cell class-switch recombination, T-cell regulation, cytokine dysregulation
- GBS: Aberrant B-cell responses (anti-ganglioside antibody production), complement activation
- Shared susceptibility via dysregulated B-cell tolerance checkpoints is plausible but unproven
- Both conditions show Th1/Th17 elevation

### 9.2 Assessment

The co-occurrence is likely coincidental in most cases given the relatively high prevalence of IgA deficiency. However, a shared autoimmune diathesis linked to the 8.1 ancestral haplotype (HLA-A1, B8, DR3) is biologically plausible but unproven.

## 10. Case Reports and Published Evidence

### 10.1 Direct Case Reports

The published literature on GBS specifically in patients with known IgA deficiency is **extremely sparse**. Most evidence comes from:
- IVIg adverse reaction case series that include IgA-deficient patients (not GBS-specific)
- GBS treatment reviews that mention IgA deficiency as a contraindication
- Individual unreported cases managed at single centers

### 10.2 Key Foundational Papers

| Paper | Type | Key Finding |
|---|---|---|
| Burks AW et al., NEJM 1986 | Case series | IgA-deficient patients can develop IgE anti-IgA causing IVIg anaphylaxis |
| Ferreira A et al., 1988 | Case series | Documented anti-IgA prevalence in IgA-deficient patients |
| Rachid R, Bonilla FA, JACI 2012 | Systematic review | Actual anaphylaxis risk from anti-IgA is lower than believed |
| Sundin U et al., 1998 | Case series | IgA-deficient patients can be desensitized via SCIG |
| "Anaphylaxis to IVIG," 2016 | Review | Most reactions with products >50 mcg/mL IgA |

### 10.3 Evidence Quality Summary

| Topic | Highest Evidence Available |
|---|---|
| IVIg efficacy in GBS | Level 1 (multiple RCTs, Cochrane review) |
| PE efficacy in GBS | Level 1 (multiple RCTs, Cochrane review) |
| IVIg = PE equivalence in GBS | Level 1 (RCT, Cochrane) |

## 11. Evidence Gaps and Research Priorities

### 11.1 Critical Knowledge Gaps

1. **No prospective study** has ever examined GBS outcomes specifically in IgA-deficient patients
2. **No registry data** tracks this combination
3. **No guideline** from AAN, EFNS/PNS, or any other major neurological society specifically addresses GBS + IgA deficiency
4. **The actual incidence** of this combination is unknown
5. **Pre-IVIg IgA screening** is not universally recommended, meaning some cases may be missed until a reaction occurs
6. **Complement inhibitor and FcRn inhibitor data** in IgA-deficient GBS patients specifically is absent

### 11.2 Recommended PubMed Search Strategies

For ongoing monitoring of new evidence:

```
("Guillain-Barre syndrome" OR "GBS") AND ("IgA deficiency" OR "immunoglobulin A deficiency")

("Guillain-Barre syndrome") AND ("intravenous immunoglobulin" OR "IVIg") AND ("anaphylaxis" OR "adverse reaction") AND "IgA"

("Guillain-Barre syndrome") AND ("plasmapheresis" OR "plasma exchange") AND ("immunodeficiency" OR "IgA")

("tanruprubart" OR "ANX005" OR "efgartigimod" OR "imlifidase") AND "Guillain-Barre syndrome"
```

### 11.3 Research Priorities

1. **Registry-based study**: Identify GBS cases in IgA-deficient patients through immunodeficiency registries
2. **Retrospective cohort**: Compare GBS outcomes in IgA-deficient vs IgA-sufficient patients
3. **Pre-IVIg screening**: Assess value of routine IgA measurement before GBS treatment
4. **Emerging therapies in immunodeficient patients**: Track tanruprubart, efgartigimod, and imlifidase outcomes in immunodeficient subpopulations
5. **Guideline development**: Advocate for inclusion of IgA deficiency management in future GBS treatment guidelines
