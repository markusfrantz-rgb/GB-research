## 1. Executive Summary

**Clinical bottom line:** The risk of IVIg-induced anaphylaxis in IgA-deficient patients has been historically overestimated. Anti-IgA antibodies are neither necessary nor sufficient to cause reactions, and complement activation (CARPA) may be the true driver in most cases. Plasma exchange (PE) remains the safest first-line option for GBS patients with known IgA deficiency, as it is equally effective and eliminates IgA exposure entirely. When IVIg is required, low-IgA products (Privigen <= 25 mcg/mL in Sweden; Gammagard S/D or GAMMAGARD LIQUID ERC <= 2 mcg/mL if obtainable) with premedication and slow infusion in a monitored setting carry an acceptably low risk. Three novel IgA-safe therapies are emerging for GBS: tanruprubart (anti-C1q; Phase 3 positive), imlifidase (IgG-cleaving enzyme; Phase 2 positive), and efgartigimod (FcRn inhibitor; case series and Phase 2 ongoing). For recurrent GBS, no prophylactic therapy has been validated by any RCT or guideline. Reclassification to acute-onset CIDP (A-CIDP) should be considered when criteria are met, as this changes long-term management fundamentally. Rehabilitation evidence supports structured exercise programs for strength, function, and fatigue, though persistent fatigue affects >50% of patients at 3 years.

## 2. The IgA Deficiency-IVIG Risk: Current Evidence

**Clinical bottom line:** The actual anaphylaxis risk with IVIg in IgA-deficient patients is much lower than traditionally feared. Modern IVIg products and improved manufacturing have reduced overall anaphylaxis rates by 68% over the past decade. The dogma that IgA deficiency is an absolute contraindication to IVIg is not supported by current evidence.

### 2.1 Historical Context and Evolution of Understanding

The association between IgA deficiency and IVIg anaphylaxis was first described in 1968 by Vyas et al., when anti-IgA antibodies were identified as the cause of anaphylactic transfusion reactions. This led to decades of practice where IgA deficiency was treated as an absolute contraindication to IVIg therapy. *[Level 3 -- historical case series]*

However, a paradigm shift has occurred over the past two decades:

- **Rachid & Bonilla (2012)** conducted a comprehensive review finding only 23 documented cases of anaphylaxis in IgA-deficient patients with anti-IgA antibodies in the entire literature from 1968 to 2011. They concluded that the risk is **"much lower than traditionally believed"** *[Level 3 -- systematic review]* (PMID: 21835445)

- **Martinez et al. (2021)** analyzed 24,919 patients receiving IVIg (175,535 administrations) and found an overall moderate anaphylaxis rate of 7.11/10,000 and severe anaphylaxis rate of 0.23/10,000 (1 in 43,400). No fatalities occurred. Crucially, the anaphylaxis rate declined **68% over the decade** (2009-2018), from 14.87 to 4.39 per 10,000 administrations *[Level 2 -- large retrospective cohort]* (PMID: 34562316)

- **Brennan et al. (2003)** prospectively audited 459 primary antibody-deficient patients receiving 13,508 IVIg infusions. The overall adverse reaction rate was only 0.8%, with **zero severe reactions**. Anti-IgA antibodies were documented in only 2 patients and associated with just 4 mild reactions *[Level 2 -- prospective cohort]* (PMID: 12869031)

### 2.2 Risk Factors for Anaphylaxis

From the Martinez 2021 cohort, the strongest predictors of anaphylaxis with IVIg were:

| Risk Factor | Adjusted IRR (95% CI) | Significance |
|-------------|----------------------|--------------|
| Age <18 years | 2.94 (1.91-4.52) | Strong predictor |
| First-ever IVIg dose | Reference category -- highest risk | 22.87/10,000 at first dose |
| Subsequent dose within 42 days | 0.27 (0.17-0.42) | 73% risk reduction |
| IVIg-naive (>42 day gap) | 0.76 (0.44-1.32) | Moderate risk reduction |
| Higher dose | No effect | Not a predictor |
| Clinical indication | No effect | Not a predictor |

**Key insight:** The first dose carries the highest risk, and regular exposure provides a protective effect. This has implications for patients transitioning between products or resuming therapy after interruption.

### 2.3 The CARPA Hypothesis

**Collet et al. (2024)** published a landmark analysis challenging the entire anti-IgA hypothesis and proposing complement activation-related pseudoallergy (CARPA) as the primary mechanism *[Level 3 -- systematic review with in vitro/in vivo analysis]* (PMID: 39436576):

Key findings:
- Anti-IgA IgG was detected in 6% of CVID patients, 30% of SIgAD patients, and 2% of healthy controls
- 38 patients with anti-IgA had hypersensitivity reactions, BUT 49 patients with anti-IgA had **no reactions** -- demonstrating that anti-IgA is neither necessary nor sufficient
- 10 patients (19%) **without** anti-IgA still experienced hypersensitivity -- reactions occur independently of anti-IgA status
- Complement activation (sC5b9 elevation, Bb increases, CH50 decreases) occurred consistently after IVIg infusion in **all** tested patients, regardless of anti-IgA status
- The authors conclude: **"Dispelling the myth of IgA allergy carries major implications for primary immunodeficiency patient care"**

This supports the position that IgA deficiency should be considered a **relative**, not absolute, contraindication to IVIg -- and that premedication, slow infusion rates, and monitoring are more relevant risk mitigation strategies than anti-IgA testing alone.

### 2.4 Current Guideline Positions

| Guideline/Authority | Position on IgA Deficiency + IVIg |
|---------------------|-----------------------------------|
| BSI/UKPIN 2022 | "Anti-IgA levels are NOT routinely required before commencing IgRT" *[Level 4]* (PMID: 35924867) |
| EAN/PNS 2023 GBS Guideline | Recommends routine Ig panel (which identifies IgA-D) but does not specifically address management when found (PMID: 37814552) |
| AAAAI 2017 | IgA deficiency should not preclude Ig therapy; use low-IgA products when available (PMID: 28041678) |
| Swedish (Internetmedicin.se) | "The risk of a serious reaction after transfusion with blood or plasma to a patient with IgA deficiency is very low. Routine anti-IgA measurement is NOT considered necessary" |
| German 2020 Guideline | Low-IgA products for patients with documented severe reactions (PMID: 32845010) |

## 3. Anti-IgA Antibodies: Clinical Significance Revisited

**Clinical bottom line:** Detection of anti-IgA antibodies alone does not predict clinical reactions. The antibody isotype (IgE vs IgG), concentration, specificity, product IgA content, and route of administration all determine whether a reaction will occur. IgE anti-IgA is the only type definitively linked to true anaphylaxis, and it is exceedingly rare.

### 3.1 Antibody Types and Their Clinical Relevance

| Antibody Type | Prevalence in SIgAD | Mechanism | Clinical Risk |
|---------------|---------------------|-----------|---------------|
| **IgG anti-IgA (class-specific)** | 20-30% | Immune complex formation, complement activation | Moderate; most patients tolerate IVIg despite positive test |
| **IgG anti-IgA (subclass-specific)** | Variable | Narrower immune complex formation | Lower; clinical significance uncertain |
| **IgE anti-IgA** | Very rare (prevalence unknown) | Type I hypersensitivity (mast cell degranulation) | **HIGHEST** -- true anaphylaxis with cardiovascular collapse |

**Critical evidence from Rachid et al. (2011):** In a study of 22 IgA-deficient patients receiving Ig therapy, only 3 had elevated IgG anti-IgA. **IgE anti-IgA was not detected in any patient.** All patients reported only mild symptoms (headache, fatigue, malaise). Notably, all 3 patients with anti-IgA tolerated SCIG without reactions *[Level 3 -- observational study]* (PMID: 21397310)

**Gharib et al. (2016)** documented a critical case demonstrating that anaphylaxis can occur even when IgG anti-IgA is **negative**: A CVID patient with undetectable IgA and no prior sensitization experienced anaphylaxis with the first IVIg dose. IgG anti-IgA was negative, but skin prick testing was strongly positive to all IgA-containing products and negative to IgA-depleted Gammagard S/D. This implicated IgE anti-IgA (which cannot be commercially measured) as the mechanism *[Level 4 -- case report]* (PMID: 27190527)

### 3.2 Testing Methods and Their Limitations

| Assay | Method | Sensitivity | Specificity | Turnaround | Availability |
|-------|--------|-------------|-------------|------------|-------------|
| **ID-PaGIA** (DiaMed gel card) | Particle agglutination | 89.3% | 100% | Minutes-hours | Swedish blood banks (validated at Sahlgrenska) |
| **FEIA** (Phadia ImmunoCAP 250) | Fluorescence enzyme immunoassay | Reference standard | Reference standard | 1-3 days | Karolinska, Uppsala, Linkoping |
| **ELISA** (Eurofins Viracor) | Quantitative ELISA | Standard | Standard | 5-8 business days | International reference labs |
| **IgE anti-IgA** | Not commercially available | N/A | N/A | N/A | Research labs only |

> **Citation:** Oltean S, Epure A, Lindstrom K, Pardi C. "Detection of anti-IgA antibodies using the particle gel immunoassay: a rapid test for increased patient safety." *Blood Transfus.* 2014;12(3):334-339. PMID: 24887222

**Practical limitation:** The most clinically dangerous form (IgE anti-IgA) cannot be commercially measured. As Gharib et al. (2016) demonstrated, skin prick testing with immunoglobulin preparations represents the only practical way to detect IgE-mediated sensitization. This could be considered pre-IVIg in high-risk patients if time permits.

### 3.3 Revised Risk Assessment Framework

Based on the synthesis of evidence, the presence of anti-IgA antibodies should be interpreted in clinical context:

```
Anti-IgA IgG detected
|
+-- Does the patient have a HISTORY of severe reaction to Ig/blood products?
|   |
|   +-- YES --> High risk. Use IgA-depleted product or PE.
|   |           Consider skin prick testing if time allows.
|   |
|   +-- NO --> Low-moderate risk. Proceed with low-IgA product,
|              premedication, slow infusion, monitored setting.
|
Anti-IgA IgG NOT detected
|
+-- Does the patient have a HISTORY of severe reaction?
    |
    +-- YES --> Consider IgE anti-IgA (cannot be tested commercially).
    |           Skin prick test. Use IgA-depleted product or PE.
    |
    +-- NO --> Standard IVIg with routine precautions likely safe.
```

### 4.2 Yimmugo: A Special Consideration

**Duellberg et al. (2023)** characterized Yimmugo, a novel 10% IVIg preparation produced by a dual-product manufacturing process that preserves IgA/IgM potential from human plasma. While the IgG product itself has residual IgA at ~0.1% (approximately 500 mcg/mL for a 10% solution), the manufacturing process also yields trimodulin (an IgA/IgM-enriched product). The IgA content is **higher than most IVIg products** and Yimmugo should therefore be avoided in IgA-deficient patients with suspected anti-IgA sensitization *[Level 3 -- product characterization study]* (PMID: 37466834)

### 4.3 Product Selection Hierarchy for IgA-Deficient Patients in Sweden

**In decreasing order of preference:**

1. **Gammagard S/D** (<= 1 mcg/mL) -- if obtainable via compassionate use
2. **DEQSIGA / GAMMAGARD LIQUID ERC** (<= 2 mcg/mL) -- if obtainable via early access program (contact Takeda)
3. **Privigen** (<= 25 mcg/mL) -- **BEST ROUTINELY AVAILABLE OPTION**
4. **Flebogamma DIF 5%** (< 50 mcg/mL) -- if available
5. **Kiovig** (<= 140 mcg/mL) -- acceptable if above unavailable
6. **Avoid:** Octagam, Panzyga, Yimmugo (IgA > 200 mcg/mL)

## 5. SCIG as Safe Alternative: Evidence Base

**Clinical bottom line:** SCIG has a fundamentally different safety profile from IVIg for IgA-deficient patients. No anaphylaxis has been reported with SCIG. The slow subcutaneous absorption avoids the rapid IgA bolus that triggers IV reactions. However, SCIG tolerance does NOT guarantee IVIg safety -- the pharmacokinetic differences mean a patient safe with SCIG can still react to IVIg. SCIG is impractical for GBS immunomodulatory doses (2 g/kg) but is the preferred route for Ig replacement in IgA-deficient patients requiring long-term therapy.

### 5.1 Safety Data

- **No anaphylaxis has ever been reported with SCIG** in the published literature, including in IgA-deficient patients with documented anti-IgA antibodies *[Level 2 -- multiple cohort studies]*

- **Rachid et al. (2011):** All 3 patients with detected anti-IgA antibodies in a cohort of 22 IgA-deficient patients tolerated SCIG without reactions. One patient (#23) had experienced previous IVIg anaphylaxis yet tolerated SCIG for 2.5 years with IgG anti-IgA titers of 3,946 ng/mL (PMID: 21397310)

- **Eijkhout et al. (2003):** 14 CVID patients with anti-IgA all tolerated SCIG. SCIG proved safe in every patient tested (PMID: 12948165)

- **Horn et al. (2007):** 4 of 5 CVID patients with anti-IgA and prior IVIg reactions tolerated SCIG (PMID: 17137841)

- The relative risk of moderate/systemic adverse effects is **28% lower** with SCIG compared to IVIg (meta-analytic data) *[Level 1 -- meta-analysis]*

### 5.2 Why SCIG Is Safer: Pharmacokinetic Explanation

| Factor | SCIG | IVIg |
|--------|------|------|
| Peak serum IgA exposure | Gradual rise over hours; steady-state kinetics | Immediate high peak (bolus) |
| Rate of complement activation | Minimal (slow antigen presentation) | Rapid (CARPA mechanism) |
| Systemic adverse event rate | <1% across >40,000 infusions | 2.5-87.5% depending on product/patient |
| Anaphylaxis | Never reported | 7.11/10,000 moderate; 0.23/10,000 severe |

### 5.3 SCIG Tolerance Does NOT Guarantee IVIg Safety

This is a critical point for clinical decision-making. Rachid et al. (2011) documented a patient (#23) who experienced IVIg anaphylaxis despite tolerating SCIG for 2.5 years. The subcutaneous route allows immune complex formation and clearance without systemic activation; the IV route bypasses this protective mechanism entirely.

### 5.4 SCIG for CIDP Maintenance

For patients reclassified as A-CIDP requiring long-term Ig therapy, SCIG is strongly preferred over IVIg:

- **PATH trial (van Schaik et al., 2018):** SCIG (Hizentra) significantly reduced relapse in CIDP patients previously dependent on IVIg *[Level 1 -- Phase 3 RCT]* (PMID: 29122523)

- **PATH extension (2019):** 81% completed 48-week extension; >80% preferred SCIG over IVIg *[Level 2 -- open-label extension]* (PMID: 31355323)

- **ADVANCE-CIDP 1 (Bril et al., 2023):** Hyaluronidase-facilitated SCIG (HyQvia) reduced relapse to 9.7% vs 31.4% placebo *[Level 1 -- Phase 3 RCT]* (PMID: 37314318)

- **ADVANCE-CIDP 3 (Hadden et al., 2024):** 85 patients, 220 patient-years of fSCIG with favorable long-term safety (PMID: 39523874)

For Madeleine's case specifically: if reclassified as A-CIDP, SCIG maintenance with Hizentra (IgA <= 50 mcg/mL) represents the optimal route, given her established SCIG tolerance with Gamanorm (IgA <= 82.5 mcg/mL) and selective IgA deficiency.

## 6. Tolerance Induction and Desensitization Protocols

**Clinical bottom line:** True immune tolerance to IgA can be induced through controlled, gradual IgA exposure -- first subcutaneously, then intravenously. This has been demonstrated in multiple studies over periods of days to months. Tolerance requires ongoing exposure; interruptions lead to relapse. This approach is primarily relevant for patients requiring chronic Ig therapy (e.g., CVID, CIDP) rather than acute GBS treatment.

### 6.1 Landmark Tolerance Induction Studies

**Sundin et al. (1998) -- Karolinska Institute, Sweden** *[Level 3 -- prospective case series]* (PMID: 9649200)

This Swedish study from Huddinge University Hospital/Karolinska Institute demonstrated two distinct mechanisms of anti-IgA disappearance:

- 4 patients with anti-IgA antibodies received SCIG (100 mg/kg/week)
- With HIGH-IgA preparation (4.8 mg/mL), anti-IgA reached **undetectable levels within 1 month**
- Serum fractionation showed anti-IgA shifted from monomeric to immune complex fractions (complexing mechanism)
- Patient 2 achieved **true tolerization**: anti-IgA remained absent for **5+ years** even after switching to low-IgA preparation
- No systemic side effects in any patient
- Mechanism: B-cell anergy through immune complex formation and Fc receptor cross-linking

**Ahrens et al. (2008)** *[Level 3 -- prospective case series]* (PMID: 18234056)

- 5 CVID patients with documented anti-IgA and prior anaphylactic reactions
- Tolerance induction achieved in **all 5 patients** through cautious IgA-depleted IVIg followed by transition to standard IgA-containing preparations
- Key observation: patients with **weak** anti-IgA titers achieved tolerance more easily than those with strong titers
- Tolerance mechanism: dual -- antibody blockage in circulation AND inhibited anti-IgA production
- After tolerance induction, **all available IVIg preparations** could be administered without reactions

**Salama et al. (2014) -- Charite University Medicine, Berlin** *[Level 3 -- prospective case series with long-term follow-up]* (PMID: 25538541)

- 10 patients with anti-IgA (1 isolated IgA-D with PNH, 9 CVID)
- Tolerance induction via controlled subcutaneous and/or IV infusions
- Anti-IgA titers significantly diminished or disappeared in all patients
- Long-term treatment tolerated for **up to 10 years** without adverse reactions
- **Critical finding:** treatment interruption and re-exposure caused adverse reactions -- tolerance requires continuous maintenance
- Patient 10 (PNH) tolerated not only IVIg but also **unwashed red blood cell transfusions** after desensitization
- No boostering of anti-IgA occurred in any patient

### 6.2 Practical Desensitization Protocol

Based on the evidence from the above studies, a composite desensitization protocol for patients with anti-IgA:

```
Phase 1: SUBCUTANEOUS initiation (Days 1-7)
|
+-- Begin with low-dose SCIG (IgA <= 50 mcg/mL preparation)
|   at 0.05-0.1 g/kg, infused slowly over 2-4 hours
|
+-- Monitor anti-IgA titers if testing available
|
+-- Increase dose gradually over 1-2 weeks to standard SCIG dose
|
Phase 2: INTRAVENOUS transition (Days 7-14)
|
+-- When anti-IgA titer decreases or patient tolerates SCIG well:
|   begin IgA-depleted IVIg at very slow rate (0.5 mL/kg/hr)
|
+-- Increase rate stepwise per tolerance protocol
|
+-- If tolerated: transition to low-IgA IVIg (e.g., Privigen)
|
Phase 3: Maintenance
|
+-- Continue regular IVIg at individually determined intervals
|
+-- Do NOT interrupt treatment beyond the interval at which
|   anti-IgA reactivation occurs (typically 2-8 weeks)
|
+-- If interruption unavoidable: restart cautiously at slow rate
```

### 6.3 Relevance to Acute GBS

Tolerance induction is **not practical for acute GBS treatment** due to the time required (days to weeks). It is most relevant for:

- Patients with CVID or SIgAD requiring chronic Ig replacement
- Patients reclassified as A-CIDP requiring ongoing maintenance therapy
- Planning for potential future GBS recurrences in known IgA-deficient patients

For acute GBS, PE or low-IgA IVIg with full precautions remain the immediate options.

## 7. IgA-Safe Emerging Therapies for GBS

**Clinical bottom line:** Three novel therapies are in clinical development for GBS, all IgA-safe because they are monoclonal antibodies or enzymes -- not pooled human immunoglobulin. Tanruprubart (anti-C1q) has **positive Phase 3 results** and may become the first new approved GBS therapy in 30 years. These are particularly relevant for the GBS + IgA deficiency population because they eliminate the IgA exposure problem entirely.

### 7.1 Tanruprubart (ANX005) -- Anti-C1q Complement Inhibitor

**The most promising new GBS therapy and the most IgA-relevant.**

- **Mechanism:** Fully humanized IgG4 monoclonal antibody blocking C1q, the initiating protein of the classical complement pathway
- **IgA safety:** Monoclonal antibody -- contains NO IgA. Completely safe for IgA-deficient patients
- **Phase 3 results (2025):** 241 patients randomized in Bangladesh and Philippines (supportive care only setting). Single 30 mg/kg IV dose showed:
  - OR 2.4 (95% CI 1.29-4.50; p=0.0058) for improved GBS-DS at Week 8
  - Patients walked independently **31 days earlier** (p=0.0211)
  - **28 fewer days on mechanical ventilation** (p=0.0356)
  - Both 30 mg/kg and 75 mg/kg inhibited complement; 30 mg/kg was superior clinically
- **Safety:** Transient infusion-related reactions in 35%; no increased mortality or infection
- **Regulatory status:** EMA MAA submission Q1 2026; FDA BLA discussion ongoing
- **Clinical significance for GBS+IgA:** If approved, tanruprubart would be the ideal first-line treatment for this combination -- effective, targeted, and IgA-safe

*[Level 2 -- Phase 3 RCT, positive]*

> **Citation:** Wiegers EJA, Jacobs BC. "New insights in the immune treatment of Guillain-Barre syndrome." *Curr Opin Neurol.* 2025;38(5):471-477. PMID: 40748027

### 7.2 Imlifidase -- IgG-Cleaving Enzyme

- **Mechanism:** Endopeptidase from Streptococcus pyogenes that rapidly cleaves all IgG molecules within hours, neutralizing pathogenic antibodies
- **IgA safety:** Specifically cleaves **IgG only** -- does NOT interact with IgA
- **Phase 2 results (2024):** 30 patients, single-arm
  - Mean MRC sum score improvement: 10.9 points by Week 1
  - Median time to 1-grade GBS-DS improvement: **6 days**
  - 67% achieved independent walking by 8 weeks; 85% by 6 months
  - Treatment combined with IVIg (started >=48h after imlifidase to allow IgG clearance)
- **Unique advantage:** Extremely rapid onset (hours); combined with IVIg for sustained effect
- **Developer:** Hansa Biopharma (Sweden -- potentially accessible through compassionate use)

*[Level 2 -- Phase 2 single-arm trial]*

### 7.3 Efgartigimod (Vyvgart) -- FcRn Inhibitor

- **Mechanism:** Human IgG Fc fragment blocking neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn), accelerating degradation of all circulating IgG including pathogenic autoantibodies
- **IgA safety:** Monoclonal antibody derivative -- NO IgA content. Importantly, **FcRn inhibitors target IgG specifically without affecting IgA or IgM levels**
- **Approved indication:** Generalized myasthenia gravis (gMG)
- **GBS evidence (case reports, 2024-2026):**

| Study | N | Dosing | Key Outcome | PMID |
|-------|---|--------|-------------|------|
| Hu et al. 2025 | 1 (MFS-GBS overlap) | 800 mg x4 doses | Extubated after 2nd dose; GBS-DS 5 -> 1 | 41029230 |
| Ripellino et al. 2025 | 1 (refractory GBS) | Add-on after failed PE + IVIg | Walking alone at 4 months | 40799081 |
| Chen et al. 2025 | 3 (various phenotypes) | Sequential after IVIg | Shortened disease course | 40012687 |
| Deng et al. 2025 | 4 (including monotherapy) | 10 mg/kg | Efficacy including as monotherapy | 40977730 |
| Cheng et al. 2026 | 17 (retrospective) | Various | 1-point GBS-DS improvement in **4 days** (vs comparators) | 41851318 |

- **Phase 2 RCT ongoing:** NCT05701189 (efgartigimod 20 mg/kg on days 1 and 5 vs IVIg)
- **CIDP:** Approved for CIDP (ADHERE trial: 61% relapse reduction vs placebo; PMID: 39304241)

*[Level 4 -- case series/reports for GBS; Level 1 for CIDP]*

### 7.4 Eculizumab (Anti-C5) -- Negative Phase 3

- Phase 2 was positive (74% eculizumab vs 18% placebo could run at 24 weeks in Japan) (PMID: 29685815)
- **Phase 3 was NEGATIVE:** No clinical benefit demonstrated (PMID: 38987228)
- EAN/PNS guideline does not recommend eculizumab for GBS
- Included for completeness but **NOT recommended**

### 7.5 Summary: Emerging Therapy IgA Safety Profile

## 8. IgA-Safe Emerging Therapies for CIDP Maintenance

**Clinical bottom line:** If Madeleine's disease course is reclassified as A-CIDP, long-term maintenance therapy will be needed. SCIG is the preferred standard approach for an IgA-deficient patient. For IVIg-dependent patients, efgartigimod is now FDA/EMA-approved for CIDP. Rituximab has mixed evidence -- positive for IgG4-antibody-positive cases, negative in unselected typical CIDP.

### 8.1 Efgartigimod for CIDP

- **ADHERE trial (Lewis et al., 2024):** 61% relapse reduction vs placebo. FDA approved for CIDP June 2024 *[Level 1 -- Phase 3 RCT]* (PMID: 39304241)
- **Advantage for IgA-deficient patients:** Eliminates need for IVIg/SCIG entirely during treatment
- **Caution:** 4/9 patients in one series experienced severe relapse within days of transitioning from IVIg to efgartigimod (PMID: 39578164) -- transition strategy is critical

### 8.2 Rozanolixizumab for CIDP -- NEGATIVE

- **Phase 2a trial (Querol et al., 2024):** Despite reducing IgG by >80%, rozanolixizumab showed **no efficacy** vs placebo in CIDP. 31% in each group required rescue therapy *[Level 2 -- Phase 2a RCT]* (PMID: 38729747)
- This raises important questions about whether CIDP is purely IgG-mediated in all patients
- **NOT recommended for CIDP**

### 8.3 Rituximab for CIDP

- **CIDPRIT trial (Campagnolo et al., 2025):** Rituximab was **NOT more effective than placebo** in typical CIDP *[Level 1 -- RCT, negative]* (PMID: 39658326)
- **However:** Rituximab appears effective in **IgG4-antibody-positive** CIDP/nodopathies:
  - RECIPE trial: 66.7% vs 20% placebo in anti-paranodal antibody-positive CIDP (PMID: 32234705)
  - Low-dose rituximab: 73 patients, superior to conventional therapy (PMID: 39660535)
- **For Madeleine's case:** Rituximab would only be relevant if anti-paranodal antibodies (anti-CNTN1, anti-NF155, anti-NF186) are identified
- **Krecak et al. (2024):** Low-dose rituximab (100 mg/m2 x4 weekly) rescued an 85-year-old with refractory GBS associated with myeloma after IVIg and PE both failed *[Level 4 -- case report]* (PMID: 38549594)

### 8.4 Other IgA-Safe Maintenance Options

| Agent | Evidence | IgA-Safe? | Notes |
|-------|----------|-----------|-------|
| Mycophenolate mofetil | Retrospective: 87.5% improved MRC at 12 months in refractory CIDP (PMID: 40357692) | Yes | Oral; immunosuppressive |
| Cyclophosphamide | Pooled response rate 68% in CIDP (PMID: 35872028) | Yes | Reserved for severe cases |
| Corticosteroids | Pulsed dexamethasone: 26% long-term remission (PMID: 22442436) | Yes | 1st-line for CIDP alongside IVIg |
| Ofatumumab (SC anti-CD20) | 1 case report: successful in relapsed CIDP (PMID: 39144145) | Yes | Subcutaneous; not established |
| CAR-T (anti-BCMA) | 1 patient: sustained remission >2 years (PMID: 40425008) | Yes | Experimental |

## 9. Recurrence Prevention: Evidence Gap

**Clinical bottom line:** There is no validated prophylactic therapy for recurrent GBS. The EAN/PNS 2023 guideline does not recommend prophylaxis. Recurrence occurs in 2-5% of GBS patients, with intervals ranging from months to decades. Subsequent episodes may be milder or more severe than the initial event. When recurrence occurs, the treating physician should reconsider whether A-CIDP is the correct diagnosis.

### 9.1 Epidemiology of Recurrent GBS (RGBS)

| Study | N RGBS / N GBS | Recurrence Rate | Key Finding | PMID |
|-------|---------------|-----------------|-------------|------|
| Grand'Maison 1992 | 12 patients, 32 episodes | - | Severity varied unpredictably between episodes | 1393505 |
| Kuitwaard 2009 | 32 patients, 81 episodes | 2-7% | Recurrent patients younger; 2-7 episodes over decades | 18931012 |
| Mossberg 2012 | 15/229 (17 years) | 6.6% cumulative | Swedish population-based study | 22507178 |
| Basta 2019 | 13/404 | 3.2% | All had exactly 2 episodes | 31857555 |

### 9.2 Patterns of Recurrence

**Wang et al. (2025)** documented a 49-year-old male with three GBS episodes (intervals of 5 years and 1 year). Key observations *[Level 4 -- case report with literature review]* (PMID: 40386770):

- Episodes became **shorter** and **less severe** with each recurrence (Hughes nadir: 5 -> 4 -> 3)
- Time to nadir shortened: 3 weeks -> 2 weeks -> 1 week
- The first episode required mechanical ventilation; subsequent episodes did not
- RGBS typically does NOT require long-term immunotherapy

**Othman et al. (2024)** reported the opposite pattern: an 80-year-old woman whose second GBS episode (17-year interval) was **far more severe** than the first, progressing to GCS 3 requiring tracheostomy and PEG. Spontaneous recovery began only after 110 days *[Level 4 -- case report]* (PMID: 39050281)

**Conclusion:** Severity of recurrent episodes is **unpredictable** -- they may be milder, equivalent, or more severe than the index event.

### 9.3 Potential Predictors of Recurrence

- **TSH >3.87:** One study (Wang et al., 2022) reported this predicts GBS recurrence with 88.9% sensitivity and 90.4% specificity, but this has not been replicated (PMID: 35342963) *[Level 4 -- single retrospective study]*
- **Younger age at onset:** Multiple studies report recurrent GBS patients are younger
- **Prior MFS variant:** Some suggest MFS history as a risk factor for recurrence
- **Anti-sulfatide antibodies:** Detected in one RGBS patient (Wang 2025); potential biomarker requiring validation

### 9.4 No Validated Prophylaxis

The **EAN/PNS 2023 guideline** does not recommend any prophylactic therapy for GBS recurrence prevention *[Level 1 -- guideline]*. This evidence gap is critical for Madeleine's case, where previous GBS at age 16 and current recurrence at age 52 raises the question of prevention for potential future episodes.

## 10. GBS vs A-CIDP: Diagnostic Implications

**Clinical bottom line:** The distinction between recurrent GBS (RGBS), GBS with treatment-related fluctuations (GBS-TRF), and acute-onset CIDP (A-CIDP) has direct treatment implications. A-CIDP requires long-term maintenance therapy; GBS does not. Reclassification criteria include: progression >8 weeks, >=3 treatment-related fluctuations, or relapse after initial improvement with consistent electrophysiological deterioration.

### 10.1 Diagnostic Criteria

| Feature | RGBS | GBS-TRF | A-CIDP |
|---------|------|---------|--------|
| Episodes | >=2 distinct episodes, separated by >=2 months (full recovery) or >=4 months (partial recovery) | <=2 fluctuations within 2 months of onset | Chronic/relapsing course >8 weeks |
| Interval | Months to decades | Days to weeks | Continuous or within 8 weeks |
| Treatment response | Full IVIg/PE course per episode | Additional course of initial treatment | Long-term maintenance required |
| Electrophysiology | May differ between episodes | Consistent with GBS | Progressive deterioration |
| Cranial nerves | May be involved | As initial presentation | Rarely involved |
| Autonomic dysfunction | May occur | As initial presentation | Rare |

> **Key reference:** Ruts L, Drenthen J, Jacobs BC, van Doorn PA. "Distinguishing acute-onset CIDP from fluctuating Guillain-Barre syndrome: a prospective study." *Neurology.* 2010;74(21):1680-1686. PMID: 20427754

### 10.2 The "Rule of Three" and "8-Week Rule"

- **Rule of Three:** If a patient has >=3 treatment-related fluctuations, A-CIDP should be strongly considered
- **8-Week Rule:** If disease progression extends beyond 8 weeks from onset, the diagnosis shifts from GBS to CIDP

### 10.3 Autoimmune Nodopathy: A New Differential

**Kmezic et al. (2025)** from Sweden described pan-neurofascin autoimmune nodopathy as an important differential diagnosis to GBS/A-CIDP. These patients have autoantibodies against neurofascin-155, neurofascin-186, or contactin-1 and respond dramatically to rituximab rather than IVIg *[Level 4 -- case series]* (PMID: 40135266)

Testing for anti-paranodal antibodies (anti-NF155, anti-NF186, anti-CNTN1) should be considered in:
- Treatment-resistant GBS
- Suspected A-CIDP
- Patients with prominent tremor, ataxia, or nephrotic syndrome alongside neuropathy

### 10.4 Implications for Madeleine

With a first GBS episode at age 16 and current recurrence at age 52, Madeleine meets criteria for RGBS (interval >4 months with partial recovery). However:

- If treatment-related fluctuations occur (deterioration after initial improvement with PE), A-CIDP reclassification should be considered
- If reclassified as A-CIDP: initiate maintenance therapy (SCIG preferred given IgA deficiency)
- Anti-paranodal antibody testing is warranted to exclude autoimmune nodopathy
- The IgA deficiency creates shared autoimmune diathesis via the 8.1 ancestral HLA haplotype (HLA-A1, B8, DR3), biologically linking the conditions

## 11. Rehabilitation and Recovery Treatments

**Clinical bottom line:** Rehabilitation is a cornerstone of GBS recovery. Structured exercise programs improve strength (81% of studies), functional independence (87.5%), and fatigue (all 5 studies assessing it). Persistent fatigue affects 53-80% of patients at 3 years and has no effective pharmacological treatment (amantadine failed in RCT). Vitamin D deficiency is common (91% of GBS patients) and associated with worse outcomes. Novel approaches including robot-assisted gait training, erythropoietin, and 4-aminopyridine show promise but require further study.

### 11.1 Exercise and Physical Rehabilitation

**Kiper et al. (2025)** conducted a scoping review of 16 studies (181 participants) on physical exercise in GBS *[Level 2 -- scoping review]* (PMID: 40283485):

| Intervention | Studies | Improvement Rate | Recommended Parameters |
|--------------|---------|-----------------|----------------------|
| Muscle strengthening | 10 (62.5%) | 81% showed improvement | 2-3x/week, 2-3 sets of 10-15 reps |
| Functional training | 11 (68.75%) | 87.5% showed improvement | Daily or 3-5x/week; gait, transfers, balance |
| Endurance training | 7 (43.75%) | All showed some benefit | 2-3x/week, 30 min/session; cycling, aerobic |
| ROM/stretching | 6 (37.5%) | Prevents contractures | 2-3x/day, 10-20 reps per joint |

**Key caution:** High-intensity exercise in denervated muscles may cause additional weakening and fatigue. Intensity should be titrated to the individual patient's nerve injury pattern.

### 11.2 Long-Term Recovery Outcomes

**Uz et al. (2024)** followed 24 adult GBS patients for 3 years *[Level 3 -- prospective cohort]* (PMID: 38362288):

| Measure | Admission | Discharge | 1 Year | 3 Years |
|---------|-----------|-----------|--------|---------|
| 6MWT (meters) | 114 | 211 | 358 | 414 |
| FIM motor score | 64.2 | 77.0 | 84.3 | 87.1 |
| Hughes score (median) | 3 | 2 | 1 | 1 |
| Fatigue (FSS >4) | 100% | 79% | 75% | **53%** |
| Neuropathic pain | 70.8% | - | - | **58.8%** |

**Key findings:** Functional recovery continues for years, but **fatigue persists in >50% at 3 years** and neuropathic pain in ~59%. Older age correlates significantly with slower recovery and higher fatigue.

### 11.3 Fatigue Treatment

- **Amantadine:** RCT in 80 GBS patients showed NO benefit vs placebo *[Level 1 -- RCT, negative]* (PMID: 16361594). Fatigue reduction during pre-treatment phase suggested a strong placebo/attention effect.
- **No pharmacological treatment has proven effective** for post-GBS fatigue

### 11.4 Vitamin D and GBS

**Lopez-Pizano et al. (2026):** 56 GBS patients vs 56 controls *[Level 3 -- prospective observational]* (PMID: 40520471):

- GBS patients: median vitamin D 17.1 ng/mL vs controls 29.9 ng/mL (p<0.001)
- 91% of GBS patients had insufficient/deficient vitamin D
- Vitamin D <= 15 ng/mL associated with:
  - Higher dysautonomia (52.4% vs 22.8%, p=0.040)
  - Reduced independent walking recovery at 3 months (log-rank p=0.047)
- Patients received vitamin D3 supplementation (800 IU/day for 3 months)

**Recommendation:** Check vitamin D levels in all GBS patients; supplement if deficient. While causation is not established, the association with poorer outcomes and the safety of supplementation support this approach.

### 11.5 Novel Regenerative Approaches

| Approach | Evidence Level | Key Finding | Status | PMID |
|----------|---------------|-------------|--------|------|
| **Erythropoietin (EPO)** | Level 3 (preclinical) | Reversed anti-ganglioside antibody inhibition of axon regeneration in animal models; 82% increase in myelinated fibers | Preclinical; no GBS clinical trials | 22046448 |
| **4-Aminopyridine** | Level 2 (Phase IIB RCT) | K+ channel blocker; safe but not statistically significant improvement; trend toward benefit (p=0.065) | Further study needed | 34179759 |
| **Robot-assisted gait training** | Level 4 (case report) | 75-year-old improved from bed-bound to moderate dependency (Barthel 15->65) after 1 month | Feasible and safe in elderly | 39202490 |
| **BDNF (activity-dependent)** | Level 3 (preclinical) | Electrical stimulation and exercise enhance BDNF-mediated nerve regeneration; Val66Met polymorphism affects response | Research phase | 30687012 |

## 13. Key Evidence Gaps and Research Needs

### 13.1 Critical Knowledge Gaps

1. **No prospective study** has ever examined GBS outcomes specifically in IgA-deficient patients. All management is extrapolated from separate GBS and IgA deficiency evidence bases.

2. **No registry data** tracks the GBS + IgA deficiency combination. The actual incidence is unknown.

3. **No guideline** from any major neurological society (AAN, EAN/PNS, ABN) specifically addresses GBS in patients with IgA deficiency.

4. **IgE anti-IgA cannot be commercially measured.** This is the most dangerous antibody type, yet no validated clinical assay exists. Skin prick testing is the only practical alternative.

5. **Tanruprubart, imlifidase, and efgartigimod** have not been specifically studied in IgA-deficient GBS patients. Their IgA safety is inferred from their mechanism (non-Ig products), not demonstrated in this population.

6. **No validated recurrence prevention** exists for recurrent GBS. The EAN/PNS 2023 guideline acknowledges this gap.

7. **The CARPA hypothesis** (Collet 2024) requires confirmation through larger prospective studies. If validated, it would fundamentally change the approach to IVIg reactions.

8. **Post-GBS fatigue** has no effective pharmacological treatment. The biological mechanisms remain poorly understood.

9. **Vitamin D supplementation** in GBS has not been studied in an interventional trial, despite strong observational associations with outcomes.

10. **Erythropoietin** shows strong preclinical evidence for enhancing nerve repair in anti-ganglioside antibody-mediated injury but has never been tested in GBS patients.

### 13.2 Recommended Research Priorities

| Priority | Approach | Feasibility |
|----------|----------|-------------|
| Registry of GBS + immunodeficiency | Cross-link national GBS registries (IGOS) with immunodeficiency registries | High -- uses existing infrastructure |
| Pre-IVIg IgA screening in GBS | Assess value of routine IgA measurement before GBS treatment | Moderate -- add to existing GBS protocols |
| Tanruprubart + IVIg combination trial | Phase 3 in settings where IVIg is standard care | Underway (planned) |
| IgE anti-IgA clinical assay | Develop commercial test for the most dangerous antibody type | Low (limited market) |
| Vitamin D interventional trial in GBS | RCT of high-dose vitamin D supplementation on GBS outcomes | Moderate |
| EPO for nerve repair in GBS | Phase 2 trial of EPO or non-erythropoietic EPO derivative | Moderate (existing drug) |
| Post-GBS fatigue mechanism study | Neuroimaging, biomarker, and interventional studies | High priority, moderate feasibility |
