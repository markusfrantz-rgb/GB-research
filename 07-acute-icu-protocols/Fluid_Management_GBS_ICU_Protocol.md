## 1. WHY FLUID BALANCE MATTERS IN GBS

Fluid overload is an independent, modifiable risk factor for ICU mortality and prolonged mechanical ventilation. In GBS specifically, three mechanisms compound the risk:

1. **Impaired autoregulation:** Dysautonomia eliminates normal vascular compensation — every liter of excess fluid produces exaggerated hemodynamic effects (Hilz et al., *Clin Auton Res*, 2019, PMID: 30022321)
2. **Prolonged ventilator dependence:** Median ventilator time in severe GBS is ~28 days. Cumulative fluid balance progressively worsens weaning prospects throughout this period
3. **Immobility:** Complete paralysis eliminates the skeletal muscle pump that normally assists venous return and lymphatic drainage, accelerating edema formation

> **Key message:** In general ICU populations, fluid overload is associated with 2–9× increased mortality depending on timing. In GBS patients with dysautonomia, these risks are likely amplified by impaired autonomic compensation.

## 2. THRESHOLDS: WHEN FLUID ACCUMULATION BECOMES DANGEROUS

### 2.1 Weight-Based Threshold: >5% Body Weight Gain

The most clinically practical threshold for dangerous fluid accumulation.

> **Citation:** Chittawatanarat K, Pichaiya T, Chandacham K, Jirapongchareonlap T, Chotirosniramit N. Fluid accumulation threshold measured by acute body weight change after admission in general surgical intensive care units: how much should be concerning? *Ther Clin Risk Manag.* 2015;11:1163-1167. PMID: 26251605

**Study:** Prospective cohort, 465 surgical ICU patients. Maximum body weight change (Max%ΔBW) during first ICU week evaluated against outcomes. *[Level 2 — prospective cohort]*

**Key findings (multivariable-adjusted):**

| Outcome | OR at >5% BW gain (95% CI) | p value |
|---------|---------------------------|---------|
| **ICU mortality** | **2.38 (1.25–4.54)** | 0.008 |
| ICU mortality without RRT | 2.47 (1.21–5.06) | 0.013 |
| **Reintubation within 72 h** | **2.51 (1.04–6.00)** | 0.039 |
| **RRT requirement** | **2.67 (1.13–6.33)** | 0.026 |
| Delirium | 1.97 (1.08–3.57) | 0.025 |

**Clinical implication:** Daily weight measurement is a simple surrogate for fluid balance. A gain exceeding 5% from admission weight demands active intervention.

### 2.2 Volume-Based Threshold: Per-Liter Risk

> **Citation:** Messmer AS, Zingg C, Müller M, Gerber JL, Schefold JC, Pfortmueller CA. Fluid overload and mortality in adult critical care patients — a systematic review and meta-analysis of observational studies. *Crit Care Med.* 2020;48(12):1862-1870. PMID: 33009098

**Study:** Systematic review + meta-analysis, 31 observational studies, 31,076 ICU patients. *[Level 1 — systematic review]*

**Key findings:**

| Metric | Adjusted Relative Risk (95% CI) |
|--------|--------------------------------|
| Fluid overload (>5% weight gain), day 3 | **8.83 (4.03–19.33)** |
| Fluid overload, any time point | **2.79 (1.55–5.00)** |
| Cumulative fluid balance, day 3 | 2.15 (1.51–3.07) |
| Cumulative fluid balance, any time point | 1.39 (1.15–1.69) |
| **Per liter positive balance** | **1.19 (1.11–1.28)** |

**Subgroup associations:**

| Population | Adjusted RR (95% CI) |
|------------|---------------------|
| Acute kidney injury | 2.38 (1.75–2.98) |
| Surgery | 6.17 (4.81–7.97) |
| Sepsis | 1.66 (1.39–1.98) |
| **Respiratory failure** | **1.19 (1.03–1.43)** |

**Clinical implication:** Risk increases linearly — every additional liter of positive balance adds ~19% to mortality risk. There is no safe zone above euvolemia; the relationship is continuous.

### 2.3 Late Fluid Balance Matters More

> **Citation:** Wang TJ, Pai KC, Huang CT, Wong LT, Wang MS, Lai CM, Chen CH, Wu CL, Chao WC. A positive fluid balance in the first week was associated with increased long-term mortality in critically ill patients: a retrospective cohort study. *Front Med (Lausanne).* 2022;9:727103. PMID: 35308497

**Study:** 4,610 critically ill patients, linked 2015–2019. *[Level 3 — retrospective cohort]*

**Key finding:** Cumulative fluid balance on days 4–7 (CFB 4-7) was a stronger independent predictor of 1-year mortality than days 1–3:

| Period | HR per 1-liter increment (95% CI) |
|--------|----------------------------------|
| CFB day 1–3 | 1.047 (1.037–1.058) |
| **CFB day 4–7** | **1.094 (1.080–1.108)** |

**Clinical implication:** Early fluid resuscitation (days 1–3) may be necessary for hemodynamic stabilization. However, continued positive balance after the acute resuscitation phase (day 4+) is independently harmful. This is the phase where active deresuscitation should begin.

> **For GBS patients:** The acute resuscitation phase typically coincides with PE treatment (days 1–7). After PE completion, there is no clinical justification for continued positive fluid balance. Day 4–7 marks the critical transition point.

## 3. SOURCES OF FLUID ACCUMULATION IN GBS ICU

Understanding the sources is essential for targeted reduction.

### 3.1 Typical Daily Fluid Input in Ventilated GBS

| Source | Typical volume | Modifiable? |
|--------|---------------|-------------|
| **Maintenance IV fluids** (e.g. glucose 5%) | 1,000–1,500 mL/day | Yes — reduce rate or discontinue if enteral nutrition established |
| **Enteral nutrition** (tube feeding) | 400–1,000 mL/day | Partially — use calorie-dense formulas (2 kcal/mL) to reduce volume |
| **Medication carrier volumes** (propofol, fentanyl, vasopressors, clonidine, KCl) | 200–500 mL/day | Partially — concentrate infusions where possible |
| **Fluid boluses** (hypotensive episodes) | Variable (250–500 mL each) | Yes — minimize boluses; use vasopressors instead when dysautonomia-related |
| **Total estimated** | **~2,000–3,500 mL/day** | |

### 3.2 Plasma Exchange Contribution

PE is designed to be isovolemic (removal ≈ replacement), but net positive balance commonly occurs:

| PE parameter | Value |
|--------------|-------|
| Standard volume per session | 1.0–1.5 plasma volumes (~2,200–3,300 mL for 56 kg patient at 40 mL/kg) |
| Replacement fluid | 5% albumin ± crystalloid |
| Net positive balance per session | +200–500 mL (common due to compensatory crystalloid) |
| Total PE contribution (5 sessions) | **+1,000–2,500 mL cumulative** |

(French Cooperative Group on PE in GBS, *Ann Neurol* 1987; Raphael JC et al., Cochrane 2012)

### 3.3 Output Considerations

| Output route | Typical volume | Notes |
|-------------|---------------|-------|
| Urine | 1,200–2,000 mL/day | May be reduced by low-dose vasopressors, autonomic dysfunction |
| Insensible losses | ~500 mL/day | Reduced by humidified ventilator circuits |
| Stool | 100–300 mL/day | Often reduced by immobility + opioids → constipation |
| **Typical total output** | **~1,800–2,500 mL/day** | |

**Net balance:** If input exceeds output by 300–700 mL/day, cumulative overload of 2–5 kg develops within one week — consistent with clinical observations.

## 4. DYSAUTONOMIA-SPECIFIC FLUID VULNERABILITY

GBS-related dysautonomia creates a unique pathophysiological context where fluid overload is more dangerous than in neurologically intact patients.

### 4.1 Impaired Autoregulation

In healthy patients, the autonomic nervous system compensates for increased intravascular volume:
- **Venodilation** redistributes excess volume
- **Baroreceptor reflex** adjusts heart rate and peripheral resistance
- **Renal sympathetic nerve activity** modulates sodium/water excretion

In GBS dysautonomia, all three mechanisms are impaired. This produces:

- **Exaggerated preload sensitivity:** Each liter of excess fluid produces a disproportionate rise in central venous pressure and pulmonary capillary wedge pressure
- **Amplified hypertensive episodes:** During sympathetic storms, fluid overload increases preload → higher stroke volume → higher systolic BP peaks
- **Increased pulmonary edema risk:** During parasympathetic phases, reduced cardiac output combined with elevated filling pressures → pulmonary congestion

(Hilz et al., *Clin Auton Res*, 2019, PMID: 30022321; Weiss et al., *Ann Intensive Care*, 2025)

### 4.2 Hemodynamic Interactions

```
Fluid overload + sympathetic storm:
  → Increased preload → ↑ SV → ↑ SBP → hypertensive crisis risk
  → Anti-hypertensives given → rebound hypotension when storm subsides

Fluid overload + parasympathetic phase:
  → High filling pressure + ↓ cardiac output → pulmonary edema
  → Pulmonary edema → ↓ oxygenation → delayed weaning

Fluid overload + dysautonomia + weaning attempt:
  → Transition to negative pressure ventilation
  → ↑ Venous return → ↑ Preload → ↑ LV wall stress
  → Cardiac dysfunction → Weaning failure (WiPO)
```

### 4.3 Documented Warning

The post-tracheostomy care protocol for GBS with dysautonomia states:

> "Avoid aggressive fluid resuscitation (risk of pulmonary edema)"
> — Section 2.4, Hypotension Management

(Post-Tracheostomy Care in GBS with Severe Autonomic Dysfunction, this database)

**Preferred approach for dysautonomia-related hypotension:**
- Small crystalloid boluses (250 mL, max 500 mL) if clearly hypovolemic
- Vasopressors (noradrenaline) preferred over volume expansion
- Correct triggers (pain, cold, bladder distension) before adding fluid

## 5. DERESUSCITATION: ACTIVE FLUID REMOVAL

### 5.1 Evidence for Active Fluid Removal

> **Citation:** Silversides JA, Fitzgerald E, Manickavasagam US, et al. Deresuscitation of patients with iatrogenic fluid overload is associated with reduced mortality in critical illness. *Crit Care Med.* 2018;46(10):1600-1607. PMID: 29985214

**Study:** Retrospective cohort, 10 ICUs (UK + Canada), 400 ventilated patients ≥24 h. *[Level 3 — retrospective cohort]*

**Key findings:**
- 87.3% of patients had positive cumulative fluid balance
- **Largest fluid input contributors: medications and maintenance fluids** (not resuscitative fluids)
- Fluid balance on day 3 was an independent risk factor for 30-day mortality: **OR 1.26 per liter (95% CI 1.07–1.46)**
- **Negative fluid balance achieved with deresuscitation on day 3 was associated with lower mortality**

**Clinical implication:** Active fluid removal (diuretics or adjusted intake) should begin by day 3 of ICU stay, not after the patient becomes visibly edematous.

### 5.2 Protocolized Diuretic Therapy

> **Citation:** Cinotti R, Lascarrou JB, Azais MA, et al. Diuretics decrease fluid balance in patients on invasive mechanical ventilation: the randomized-controlled single blind, IRIHS study. *Critical Care.* 2021;25:99. PMID: 33691730

**Study:** Multicenter RCT, 166 ventilated ICU patients with ≥3% weight gain. *[Level 1 — RCT]*

**Key findings:**

| Outcome | Diuretic group | Control group | p |
|---------|---------------|---------------|---|
| Fluid balance (median) | 1.4 kg | 6.4 kg | <0.001 |
| Mean difference | **−4.8 kg** (95% CI −7.3 to −2.5) | — | <0.001 |
| AKI worsening | 59.7% | 75.3% | 0.03 |
| Mortality | 14% | 18% | 0.5 |

**Critical finding:** Protocolized diuretics did NOT increase AKI — in fact, AKI worsening was significantly **less** frequent in the diuretic group. This directly addresses the common clinical concern that diuretics might cause renal harm in critically ill patients.

### 5.3 Conservative Fluid Strategy: The FACCT Trial

> **Citation:** Wiedemann HP, Wheeler AP, Bernard GR, et al. (ARDS Clinical Trials Network). Comparison of two fluid-management strategies in acute lung injury. *N Engl J Med.* 2006;354(24):2564-2575. PMID: 16714767

**Study:** Multicenter RCT, 1,000 patients with acute lung injury. Conservative vs liberal fluid strategy over 7 days. *[Level 1 — RCT]*

**Key findings:**

| Outcome | Conservative | Liberal | p |
|---------|-------------|---------|---|
| Cumulative fluid balance, 7 days | **−136 ± 491 mL** | 6,992 ± 502 mL | <0.001 |
| **Ventilator-free days (28 d)** | **14.6 ± 0.5** | 12.1 ± 0.5 | <0.001 |
| **ICU-free days (28 d)** | **13.4 ± 0.4** | 11.2 ± 0.4 | <0.001 |
| 60-day mortality | 25.5% | 28.4% | 0.30 |
| Dialysis requirement | 10% | 14% | 0.06 |

**Clinical implication:** Conservative fluid management gained +2.5 ventilator-free days and +2.2 ICU-free days without increasing organ failure or dialysis need. In GBS where ventilator durations are measured in weeks to months, this benefit is clinically significant.

### 5.4 Practical Deresuscitation Protocol for GBS

**Phase 1: Reduce input (immediately)**

| Action | Detail |
|--------|--------|
| Reduce/stop maintenance IV fluids | If enteral nutrition established and providing adequate volume |
| Concentrate infusions | Use higher-concentration medication formulations where available |
| Minimize fluid boluses | Use vasopressors for dysautonomia-related hypotension, not fluid |
| Review all fluid orders | Calculate total daily input — every mL counts |

**Phase 2: Increase output (when hemodynamically stable)**

| Action | Detail |
|--------|--------|
| Furosemide | 10–20 mg IV bolus, reassess. Target urine output 0.5–1.0 mL/kg/h above baseline |
| Monitor electrolytes | K, Na, Mg, PO4 q6–12h during active diuresis |
| Daily weight | Target −500 to −1,000 mL/day negative balance (0.5–1.0 kg/day weight loss) |
| Reassess vasopressor need | Furosemide-induced volume contraction may unmask autonomic instability |

**Phase 3: Maintain euvolemia**

| Action | Detail |
|--------|--------|
| Daily weight | Continue until target weight (admission weight) achieved |
| Strict I/O documentation | Cumulative balance should be tracked and visible on chart |
| Adjust enteral nutrition volume | Use high-calorie-density formulas (2 kcal/mL) to minimize fluid load |

**Cautions in GBS:**
- **Dysautonomia:** Diuresis removes preload. In a patient with impaired baroreceptor function, this may precipitate hypotension during parasympathetic phases. Start with low doses and titrate slowly.
- **Electrolytes:** GBS patients on tube feeding have high potassium and magnesium requirements. Aggressive diuresis can deplete both rapidly. Monitor closely.
- **Vasopressors:** Patients on noradrenaline may need dose adjustment during diuresis. This is expected and acceptable — the goal is euvolemia, not zero vasopressor support.

## 6. FLUID BALANCE AND VENTILATOR WEANING

### 6.1 Weaning-Induced Pulmonary Oedema (WiPO)

> **Citation:** Liu J, Shen F, Teboul JL, et al. Cardiac dysfunction induced by weaning from mechanical ventilation: incidence, risk factors, and effects of fluid removal. *Critical Care.* 2016;20:369. PMID: 27836002

**Study:** Prospective study, 81 patients, 283 spontaneous breathing trials monitored. *[Level 2 — prospective cohort]*

**Key findings:**
- WiPO was responsible for **59% of all weaning failures**
- Independent risk factors (logistic regression):

| Risk factor | OR (95% CI) |
|-------------|-------------|
| COPD | 8.7 (2.0–37.3) |
| Structural cardiopathy | 4.5 (1.4–14.1) |
| Obesity | 3.6 (1.2–12.6) |

- When treatment including fluid removal changed preload-independence to preload-dependence (positive PLR test), **the following SBT was very likely to succeed** (6/7 patients extubated)

**GBS relevance:** GBS patients do not typically have COPD or structural cardiopathy, but:
- Dysautonomia impairs cardiac loading response to ventilation mode changes
- Prolonged immobility reduces cardiac conditioning
- Fluid overload increases baseline extravascular lung water (EVLW) → lower threshold for WiPO
- The 59% figure highlights that fluid removal is the most impactful single intervention for improving weaning success

### 6.2 Cumulative Balance Predicts Extubation Failure

> **Citation:** Ghosh S, Chawla A, Mishra K, Jhalani R, Salhotra R, Singh A. Cumulative fluid balance and outcome of extubation: a prospective observational study from a general intensive care unit. *Indian J Crit Care Med.* 2018;22(11):767-772. PMID: 30598562

**Study:** Prospective cohort, 201 ventilated patients, general ICU. *[Level 2 — prospective cohort]*

**Key findings:**
- Cumulative fluid balance at extubation was significantly higher in the failure group: **4,337 mL vs 2,752 mL** (p = 0.036)
- Cutoff for predicting extubation failure: **cumulative balance >3,490 mL** (OR 2.191, 95% CI 1.015–4.730)
- Extubation failure group had **significantly higher in-hospital mortality, ICU LOS, and hospital LOS**

**Clinical implication:** Before any extubation attempt in GBS, cumulative fluid balance should be reviewed. If >3,490 mL positive, active fluid removal should precede the attempt.

### 6.3 BNP-Guided Fluid Management During Weaning

> **Citation:** Mekontso Dessap A, Roche-Campo F, Kouatchet A, et al. Natriuretic peptide-driven fluid management during ventilator weaning: a randomized controlled trial. *Am J Respir Crit Care Med.* 2012;186(12):1256-1263. PMID: 22997204

**Study:** Multicenter RCT, 304 patients, BNP-driven vs physician-driven fluid management during weaning. *[Level 1 — RCT]*

**Key findings:**

| Outcome | BNP-driven | Physician-driven | p |
|---------|-----------|-----------------|---|
| Fluid balance during weaning | **−2,320 mL** | −180 mL | <0.0001 |
| **Time to successful extubation** | **42.4 h** | 58.6 h | 0.034 |
| Ventilator-free days | Increased | — | — |
| Adverse events (electrolytes, renal failure, shock) | No difference | — | NS |

**Clinical implication:** BNP-guided diuresis shortened weaning time by ~16 hours without increasing adverse events. In GBS patients approaching weaning readiness, BNP measurement can guide intensity of fluid removal.

## 7. MONITORING PROTOCOL

### 7.1 Daily Assessment

| Parameter | Method | Target | Frequency |
|-----------|--------|--------|-----------|
| **Body weight** | Bed scale or lift scale, same time daily | Return to admission weight | Daily |
| **Cumulative fluid balance** | All intake − all output, running total | Negative balance −500 to −1,000 mL/day after resuscitation phase | Documented each nursing shift |
| **Urine output** | Hourly catheter measurement | >0.5 mL/kg/h | Hourly |
| **Peripheral edema** | Assess hands, feet, sacrum, periorbital | Absence | Twice daily |
| **Lung auscultation** | Bilateral crackles assessment | Clear | Every shift + before SBT |
| **Chest X-ray** | If clinical concern for pulmonary edema | No new bilateral infiltrates | As indicated |

### 7.2 Laboratory Monitoring During Active Diuresis

| Test | Frequency | Action threshold |
|------|-----------|-----------------|
| Potassium | q6–12h | <3.5 mmol/L → supplement |
| Sodium | q12–24h | >150 mmol/L → reduce diuretic dose |
| Magnesium | q12–24h | <0.7 mmol/L → supplement |
| Phosphate | q24h | <0.6 mmol/L → supplement |
| Creatinine | q24h | Rise >50% from baseline → reduce/hold diuretic |
| BNP / NT-proBNP | Before weaning trial | Elevated → more aggressive fluid removal before SBT |

### 7.3 Weight-Based Risk Stratification

| Weight gain from admission | Risk level | Action |
|---------------------------|-----------|--------|
| <3% | Low | Monitor, maintain neutral balance |
| 3–5% | Moderate | Reduce fluid input, consider diuretics |
| **>5%** | **High** | **Active deresuscitation: diuretics + input reduction. Do not attempt weaning until fluid status improved.** |
| >10% | Critical | Urgent diuresis. Consider ultrafiltration if renal function impaired. |

## 8. NUTRITION AND FLUID: AVOIDING HIDDEN OVERLOAD

### 8.1 Calculate on Dry Weight

Nutritional targets must be calculated on **admission/dry weight**, not current weight inflated by fluid retention.

| Parameter | Formula | Example (56 kg patient) |
|-----------|---------|------------------------|
| Energy | 25–30 kcal/kg/day (ESPEN 2023) | 1,400–1,680 kcal/day |
| Protein | 1.2–2.0 g/kg/day | 67–112 g/day |

(Singer P et al., ESPEN guideline on clinical nutrition in the ICU. *Clin Nutr.* 2023. PMID: 37517372) *[Level 1 — guideline]*

### 8.2 Formula Selection and Fluid Load

| Formula type | kcal/mL | Volume for 1,400 kcal | mL/day |
|-------------|---------|----------------------|--------|
| Standard (1 kcal/mL) | 1.0 | 1,400 mL | 1,400 |
| **High-density (2 kcal/mL)** | 2.0 | 700 mL | **700** |

Using a 2 kcal/mL formula (e.g. Fresubin 2 kcal HP Fiber) saves ~700 mL/day compared to standard formulas — equivalent to the daily negative balance target.

### 8.3 Total Fluid Audit

For effective fluid management, **all sources** must be counted:

```
Total daily fluid input =
  Enteral nutrition (mL)
+ Maintenance IV (mL)
+ Medication carriers (mL)
+ Fluid boluses (mL)
+ Flushes (mL)
= [should not exceed target output + desired negative balance]
```

## 9. CLINICAL DECISION ALGORITHM

```
DAILY FLUID ASSESSMENT (GBS ICU)
═══════════════════════════════════════

1. MEASURE WEIGHT (same time, same scale, daily)
   │
   ├── Weight gain <3% from admission → MONITOR
   │   • Maintain neutral balance
   │   • Review fluid orders
   │
   ├── Weight gain 3–5% from admission → REDUCE
   │   • Reduce/stop maintenance IV fluids
   │   • Switch to 2 kcal/mL enteral formula
   │   • Concentrate medication infusions
   │   • Target: −500 mL/day negative balance
   │
   └── Weight gain >5% from admission → ACTIVE DERESUSCITATION
       │
       ├── Hemodynamically stable (off vasopressors or stable low dose)?
       │   │
       │   ├── YES → Start furosemide 10-20 mg IV
       │   │         Monitor K/Mg/Na q6-12h
       │   │         Target: −500 to −1000 mL/day
       │   │         Daily weight until admission weight reached
       │   │
       │   └── NO → Reduce input first
       │           Concentrate infusions
       │           Minimize boluses (vasopressor for dysautonomi-related hypoTN)
       │           Reassess daily
       │
       └── BEFORE WEANING ATTEMPT:
           │
           ├── Cumulative balance >3490 mL? → Active fluid removal FIRST
           │   (Ghosh 2018: OR 2.19 for extubation failure)
           │
           ├── BNP available? → Consider BNP-guided diuresis
           │   (Mekontso Dessap 2012: 16h shorter weaning)
           │
           └── PLR test available? → Assess preload dependence
               (Liu 2016: if PLR negative → WiPO likely → remove fluid first)
```
