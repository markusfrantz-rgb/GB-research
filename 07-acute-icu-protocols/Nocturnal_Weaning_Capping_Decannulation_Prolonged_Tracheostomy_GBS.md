## 1. SCOPE AND WHERE THIS FITS

The ICU weaning document describes a bridge from full ventilatory support to decannulation in five steps (Cheng 2021): full support → nocturnal-only ventilation via tracheostomy → daytime capping/speaking valve with nocturnal ventilation → decannulation when daytime capping is tolerated ≥48 h → nocturnal NIV by mask if still needed. *[Level 4 — retrospective cohort, n=29]*

This document addresses steps 3–5 in detail, because they typically happen **outside the ICU**, on a neurology or rehabilitation ward, with less monitoring and a different team. In the only dedicated GBS series (Cheng 2021, specialist weaning centre, London), the median time from onset to decannulation was **193 days (range 49–527)**; 59% used NIV during weaning and 14% needed long-term nocturnal NIV after decannulation. A French ICU cohort of 212 GBS patients found 10% ventilated >2 months, with decannulation on average ~3 months after tracheostomy (cited in Weiss 2025). *[Level 4]*

**Key message for the ward team:** a patient at day ~180 who breathes unassisted by day and is coming off nocturnal support is *on schedule* for the natural history of severe GBS with bulbar and respiratory involvement. This phase is not a stall; it is the expected timeline.

> **Citation:** Cheng MCF, et al. Prolonged Ventilatory Support for Patients Recovering From Guillain-Barré Syndrome. *Neurol Clin Pract*. 2021;11(1):18-24. PMID: 33968468

> **Citation:** Weiss N, et al. Critical insights for intensivists on Guillain-Barré syndrome. *Ann Intensive Care*. 2025. PMC12092332

## 2. WHY NIGHTS ARE WEANED LAST — PHYSIOLOGY

### 2.1 REM sleep and the diaphragm

During non-REM sleep, accessory and intercostal muscles contribute to ventilation. During **REM sleep, postural and accessory muscles are atonic and the diaphragm works alone**. In any neuromuscular disorder, the first manifestation of ventilatory insufficiency is therefore REM-related hypoventilation — and by the same logic, the *last* thing to normalise during recovery is sustained unassisted breathing through REM sleep. *[Level 3 — physiological and observational studies]*

Practical consequences:

- A patient may be entirely comfortable off the ventilator for 14–16 daytime hours and still hypoventilate during REM periods at night.
- Hypoventilation is intermittent (REM occurs in 4–6 episodes per night, concentrated in the second half). A single morning blood gas can be normal even if there were REM-related CO₂ rises earlier in the night, because CO₂ is washed out during subsequent non-REM sleep and wakefulness.
- This is why **continuous overnight monitoring** — not spot morning gases alone — is the standard for deciding that nocturnal support is no longer needed (Section 3).

### 2.2 Supine vital capacity as a bedside diaphragm test

Diaphragm weakness is unmasked in the supine position, where the diaphragm must work against abdominal contents. In neuromuscular disorders, a **supine fall in VC >25%** identifies diaphragmatic weakness with **sensitivity 79% and specificity 90%** (Fromageot 2001). Healthy subjects fall <10%; a 20–30% fall suggests bilateral diaphragm weakness. *[Level 2b — diagnostic cohort against transdiaphragmatic pressure]*

The postural VC drop is therefore one of the most informative measurements available on a ward: it is free, takes two minutes, and directly addresses the muscle that matters at night.

> **Citation:** Fromageot C, Lofaso F, Annane D, et al. Supine fall in lung volumes in the assessment of diaphragmatic weakness in neuromuscular disorders. *Arch Phys Med Rehabil*. 2001;82(1):123-128. PMID: 11239298

> **Citation:** Won YH, et al. Postural vital capacity difference with aging in Duchenne muscular dystrophy. *Muscle Nerve*. 2015;52(5):722-727. PMID: 25727906

## 3. WARD-LEVEL ASSESSMENT TOOLKIT

Everything in this table can be done on a general ward. The 2023 CHEST guideline recommends spirometry (FVC or SVC), MIP/MEP or SNIP, and peak cough flow as the core respiratory assessment in neuromuscular weakness, repeated at least every 6 months when a patient is at risk of respiratory failure (Khan 2023). *[Level 5 — guideline, expert panel]*

| Measurement | How | Threshold / interpretation | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Vital capacity, sitting** | Handheld spirometer via tracheostomy (cuff up) or via mouth with tube capped | >20 mL/kg sustained ≥48 h for decannulation; >22 mL/kg predicted extubation success in GBS | Nguyen 2006; ICU doc §5 |
| **Vital capacity, supine** | Same, patient flat | Fall >25% from sitting = diaphragm weakness (sens 79%, spec 90%) | Fromageot 2001 |
| **VC as % predicted** | Compare to GLI/ECSC reference for sex, age, height | <50% predicted is a classical NIV-initiation criterion in progressive NMD; in recovering GBS the *trend* matters more than the absolute | ERS 2013; Khan 2023 |
| **MIP / NIF** | Occluded inspiratory effort | More negative than −50 cmH₂O predicted extubation success in GBS; −60 cmH₂O is a NIV-initiation threshold in NMD | Nguyen 2006 |
| **MEP** | Occluded expiratory effort | Reflects expiratory (cough) muscle strength; low MEP → augment cough | Khan 2023 |
| **Peak cough flow, unassisted** | Peak flow meter via face mask (tube capped) | **>160 L/min** = only variable predicting successful decannulation in NMD (Bach 1996); **<270 L/min** = at risk of failure during chest infection | Bach 1996; Chatwin 2018 |
| **Peak cough flow with speaking valve** | Peak flow meter at mouth with valve in place | **>100 L/min** used as decannulation cut-off in a prospective series (105 decannulated, 1.9% failure at 48 h) | Ge 2024 |
| **MI-E-assisted cough flow** | Read from device or peak flow meter | >190 L/min mechanically augmented flow considered sufficient for decannulation in Bach's protocol | Bach (JNRT 2022) |
| **Overnight pulse oximetry** | Standard ward oximeter, print report | SpO₂ <88–89% for ≥5 consecutive min = nocturnal desaturation criterion | ERS/AASM |
| **Overnight transcutaneous CO₂ (PtcCO₂)** | Transcutaneous monitor (e.g. SenTec/TOSCA), often available from sleep or pulmonary unit | PtcCO₂ >50 mmHg (6.7 kPa) for >25% of night, or >55 mmHg for ≥10 min, or rise ≥10 mmHg to >50 = hypoventilation | Ogna 2016; Orlikowski 2017; AASM |
| **Morning arterial/capillary blood gas** | Before first ventilator use of the day | pCO₂ >45 mmHg (6.0 kPa) awake = daytime hypercapnia; normal value is reassuring but cannot exclude REM-limited events | ERS 2013 |
| **Tracheostomy tube manometry** | Pressure at tube with cuff down and tube occluded | Recent evidence that low occlusion pressure predicts capping success (Vigna 2026) | Vigna 2026 |

> **Citation:** Khan A, Frazer-Green L, Amin R, et al. Respiratory Management of Patients With Neuromuscular Weakness: An American College of Chest Physicians Clinical Practice Guideline and Expert Panel Report. *Chest*. 2023;164(2):394-413. PMID: 36921894

> **Citation:** Nguyen TN, Badjatia N, Malhotra A, et al. Factors predicting extubation outcome in patients with Guillain-Barré syndrome. *Neurocrit Care*. 2006;5(3):230-234. PMID: 17290095

> **Citation:** Ogna A, Quera Salva MA, Prigent H, et al. Nocturnal hypoventilation in neuromuscular disease: prevalence according to different definitions issued from the literature. *Sleep Breath*. 2016;20(2):575-581. PMID: 26338464

> **Citation:** Vigna M, Del Giudice P, Ripamonti A, et al. Tracheostomy Tube Manometry Can Predict Capping Test Success in Subjects Undergoing Decannulation. *Respir Care*. 2026. PMID: 42663253

### 3.1 Which definition of nocturnal hypoventilation?

Ogna 2016 applied eight published definitions to the same 232 unventilated NMD patients and found prevalence varied from **10% to 61%** depending on the definition used, with agreement between definitions as low as κ 0.12 — the field has no single agreed cut-off. In the same group's prognostic study (Orlikowski 2017, n=124 with normal daytime gases), **nocturnal peak PtcCO₂ ≥49 mmHg (6.5 kPa)** was the best predictor of later need for home ventilation (HR 2.6), identifying patients missed by daytime blood gases and nocturnal oximetry. For a *recovering* patient the practical approach is: any definition met → keep nocturnal support and re-test; no definition met on two separate nights → nocturnal support can stop. *[Level 2b — prospective cohort]*

> **Citation:** Orlikowski D, Prigent H, Quera Salva MA, et al. Prognostic value of nocturnal hypoventilation in neuromuscular patients. *Neuromuscul Disord*. 2017;27(4):326-330. PMID: 28153460

## 4. DISCONTINUING NOCTURNAL VENTILATION

### 4.1 There is no trial evidence — the criteria are inverted initiation criteria

No randomised or even prospective study defines when to *stop* nocturnal ventilation in a recovering neuromuscular patient. The accepted practice is to invert the criteria used to *start* it (ERS statement 2013; Khan 2023): *[Level 5 — expert consensus]*

| NIV would be started if… | So nocturnal ventilation can stop when… |
|---|---|
| Daytime PaCO₂ >45 mmHg (6.0 kPa) | Morning gas repeatedly normocapnic |
| Nocturnal SpO₂ <88–89% for ≥5 min | Overnight oximetry without sustained desaturation, no O₂ |
| Nocturnal PtcCO₂ meets a hypoventilation definition | Overnight PtcCO₂ normal on ≥2 nights |
| VC <50% predicted and falling / MIP weaker than −60 | VC stable or rising on serial measurements; supine fall <25% |
| Symptoms: morning headache, unrefreshing sleep, daytime somnolence, orthopnea | Absent |

### 4.2 The "one hour in the morning"

A residual short ventilator period after an otherwise unassisted night is a common transitional arrangement and has three possible rationales:

1. **CO₂ washout** — only meaningful if the *pre*-ventilation morning gas shows hypercapnia. If the gas taken *before* the session is normal, there is nothing to wash out.
2. **Respiratory muscle rest** — a physiologically reasonable buffer while the patient's muscles adapt to 23 h of unassisted work. Rest periods reduce diaphragm fatigue in the recovery phase of neuromuscular weakness (extrapolated from weaning physiology). *[Level 5]*
3. **Habit / safety margin** — retained because no one has defined the criterion for removing it.

**Recommended approach:** define explicitly what must be true to remove the session (e.g. normal pre-session gas on 3 consecutive mornings + one normal overnight oximetry/PtcCO₂ recording + no symptoms), document it, then remove it. If the patient values it for comfort, a *shorter* session or a trial without it on alternate days is a reasonable compromise. The risk of an unnecessary daily session is small; the risk of leaving it undefined is that it persists by inertia and delays the decannulation timeline.

### 4.3 Two ways a residual ventilator session can do harm

**(a) Over-ventilation → hypocapnia.** A ventilator set for a patient who *needed* support will deliver more alveolar ventilation than a patient who no longer needs it produces CO₂. The result is acute hypocapnia and respiratory alkalosis, which lowers ionised calcium and produces the classical symptom triad of **paresthesias in the hands, feet and perioral region**, light-headedness and, at higher degrees, carpopedal spasm (Laffey & Kavanagh 2002). *[Level 5 — established physiology]* In a recovering GBS patient the symptom is easily misattributed to sensory nerve regeneration, which also causes paresthesias (positive sensory symptoms persist in ~40% at 6 months). The distinction is temporal: **hypocapnic paresthesias begin during or shortly after the ventilator session and fade within ~30 minutes**; regenerative paresthesias are present independent of ventilation. A blood gas taken *during or immediately after* the session (low pCO₂, high pH) confirms the mechanism — the customary *pre*-session gas cannot detect it. If confirmed, the appropriate response is to shorten or remove the session, not to adjust settings to make over-ventilation more comfortable.

**(b) Psychological ventilator dependence.** Fear of breathlessness after months of mechanical support is a recognised, independent cause of prolonged weaning. Jubran 2010 (prospective, 336 patients in a long-term weaning unit) found depressive disorders in 42% of patients during prolonged weaning, associated with weaning failure in 61% vs 33% and mortality 24% vs 10%. Staff reluctance to be the one who "removed support too early" operates in the same direction. *[Level 2b]* Both are legitimate — and neither can be distinguished from a true physiological need by clinical impression alone. Objective overnight measurement (Section 3) is what separates *reassurance* from *requirement*, and gives patient and staff a shared, documented basis for stopping.

> **Citation:** Laffey JG, Kavanagh BP. Hypocapnia. *N Engl J Med*. 2002;347(1):43-53. PMID: 12097540

> **Citation:** Jubran A, Lawm G, Kelly J, et al. Depressive disorders during weaning from prolonged mechanical ventilation. *Intensive Care Med*. 2010;36(5):828-835. PMID: 20232042

### 4.4 Suggested sequence

```
Night unassisted with HME on tube (cuff down if tolerated)
  ├─ SpO₂ monitored continuously for ≥3 nights: all fine?
  ├─ Morning gas before any ventilator use: normocapnic ×3?
  └─ Overnight PtcCO₂ (or full polygraphy if OSA history — see §9): normal?
        └─ YES to all → stop residual morning session
                          → begin nocturnal capping trial (§6.4)
        └─ NO → keep nocturnal/morning support, re-measure VC (sit/supine),
                 MIP, repeat overnight study in 1–2 weeks
```

## 5. AIRWAY CLEARANCE: COUGH THRESHOLDS AND MECHANICAL INSUFFLATION-EXSUFFLATION

### 5.1 Cough is the rate-limiting step, not ventilation

In Bach & Saporito's prospective series (49 NMD patients, 62 extubation/decannulation attempts), **only the ability to generate a peak cough flow >160 L/min predicted success**; VC, ventilator-free breathing time and blood gases did not. Patients with PCF <160 L/min needed the tube replaced. The authors' central argument — later formalised as the "new paradigm" (Bach 2010) — is that a patient can be decannulated *without* being able to breathe unassisted at all, provided secretions can be cleared, because ventilation can be delivered non-invasively but airway clearance cannot be delegated to a mask. *[Level 2b — prospective cohort]*

> **Citation:** Bach JR, Saporito LR. Criteria for extubation and tracheostomy tube removal for patients with ventilatory failure. A different approach to weaning. *Chest*. 1996;110(6):1566-1571. PMID: 8989078

> **Citation:** Bach JR, Gonçalves MR, Hamdani I, Winck JC. Extubation of patients with neuromuscular weakness: a new management paradigm. *Chest*. 2010;137(5):1033-1039. PMID: 20040608

### 5.2 Thresholds (ENMC consensus, Chatwin 2018)

| Unassisted PCF | Interpretation | Action |
|---|---|---|
| >270 L/min | Effective cough, adequate reserve | None |
| 160–270 L/min | Effective when well; **likely to fail during a chest infection** | Teach manually assisted cough; have MI-E available |
| <160 L/min | Ineffective cough | Regular MI-E; decannulation only with MI-E in place and assisted flows adequate |

**Mechanical insufflation-exsufflation (MI-E, "cough assist")** delivers a positive-pressure insufflation followed by a rapid negative-pressure exsufflation, producing expiratory flows that exceed unassisted or manually assisted cough. The ENMC review (21 experts) and CHEST 2023 both suggest MI-E for patients with NMD whose PCF is inadequate, particularly when manually assisted cough is insufficient or bulbar weakness limits glottic closure. Typical settings: +40/−40 cmH₂O, titrated; Bach uses 50–60 cmH₂O via mask and 60–70 cmH₂O via tube. *[Level 3–5 — observational series + consensus]*

> **Citation:** Chatwin M, Toussaint M, Gonçalves MR, et al. Airway clearance techniques in neuromuscular disorders: A state of the art review. *Respir Med*. 2018;136:98-110. PMID: 29501255

### 5.3 What MI-E use 2–4 times/day means for decannulation

Regular MI-E is **not** a barrier to decannulation — in the Bach paradigm it is the *enabling* condition. What matters is that:

- MI-E-assisted flows are adequate (>190 L/min in Bach's series) — this can be measured with the tube capped and a mask, i.e. it tests the route that will exist *after* decannulation.
- The patient, family or ward staff can deliver it by mask after the tube is out (training required before decannulation).
- The device follows the patient to rehabilitation and home; CHEST notes the resource implication and caregiver training need.

Unassisted PCF in recovering GBS is expected to rise over months as expiratory muscles (abdominals, intercostals) recover. Serial measurement every 1–2 weeks documents the trend and informs when MI-E can be reduced.

## 6. TUBE MANAGEMENT: CUFF, DOWNSIZING, SPEAKING VALVE, CAPPING

### 6.1 Cuff deflation

Cuff deflation is the first step in restoring upper-airway airflow. It re-establishes subglottic pressure, laryngeal sensation and phonation, all of which improve swallowing. A 2026 review argues that routine use of cuffed tubes in spontaneously breathing neurological patients is not evidence-based and that cuff deflation, downsizing and cuffless/fenestrated tubes improve cough, swallowing and voice. *[Level 4–5]*

### 6.2 Speaking valve — more than communication

- **Freeman-Sanderson 2016 (RCT, n=30):** early cuff deflation with an in-line speaking valve *during* mechanical ventilation significantly hastened return of phonation compared with standard care (valve only during self-ventilation). *[Level 1b]*
- **Sutt 2016 (Crit Care, n=20, electrical impedance tomography):** speaking valve use during weaning *increased end-expiratory lung impedance* — i.e. produced lung recruitment that persisted after valve removal — contrary to the assumption that the valve loads the patient. *[Level 3]*
- **Sutt 2023 (Acta Anaesthesiol Scand):** high-flow via tracheostomy combined with a speaking valve is feasible during weaning. *[Level 4]*

A patient wearing a one-way valve all day is already breathing *out* entirely through the upper airway; the step to a cap (breathing in *and* out through the upper airway) is smaller than it appears, and the valve period doubles as an upper-airway patency test.

> **Citation:** Freeman-Sanderson AL, Togher L, Elkins MR, Phipps PR. Return of Voice for Ventilated Tracheostomy Patients in ICU: A Randomized Controlled Trial of Early-Targeted Intervention. *Crit Care Med*. 2016;44(6):1075-1081. PMID: 26855430

> **Citation:** Sutt AL, Caruana LR, Dunster KR, et al. Speaking valves in tracheostomised ICU patients weaning off mechanical ventilation — do they facilitate lung recruitment? *Crit Care*. 2016;20:91. PMID: 27038617

> **Citation:** Sutt AL, et al. High-flow via a tracheostomy tube and speaking valve during weaning from mechanical ventilation and tracheostomy. *Acta Anaesthesiol Scand*. 2023;67(10):1403-1413. PMID: 37437910

### 6.3 Downsizing

Changing to a smaller tube (e.g. 7 → 6 mm ID) increases the annular space around the tube so that airflow around it during capping approximates post-decannulation airflow. A 6 mm cuffless tube in an adult female trachea is the usual capping configuration. If capping fails with a 6 mm tube despite adequate VC and cough, suspect a *structural* upper-airway problem (granulation, stenosis, vocal cord dysfunction) rather than muscle weakness and proceed to endoscopy. *[Level 5]*

### 6.4 Capping trials — duration and the alternative

| Protocol | Requirement | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Classical ward protocol | Cap tolerated 24–48 h continuously (including one night) with SpO₂ maintained, no distress, no suction via tube | Ceriana 2003; Singh 2017 |
| Graduated | 1 h → 4 h → 8 h → 24 h → 48 h | Common practice, McGill protocol |
| Suction-frequency protocol (NEJM 2020 RCT, n=330) | No capping at all; decannulate when suction needed ≤2×/24 h, on continuous high-flow oxygen | Hernández Martínez 2020 |

The NEJM trial (330 patients, 5 ICUs) found decannulation **faster (median 6 vs 13 days)** with the suction-frequency approach, with *lower* pneumonia/tracheobronchitis incidence and no difference in decannulation failure. Its population was general ICU patients, not neuromuscular, and the comparison arm used only 24 h capping. The finding relevant to this document is that **capping trials are not a validated safety filter** — their main function is to reveal upper-airway obstruction, which can also be assessed by endoscopy or manometry. *[Level 1b — RCT, indirect population]*

**Nocturnal capping deserves specific comment.** For a patient with an OSA history (Section 9), the first capped nights are the first nights in six months in which the upper airway can obstruct. Continuous oximetry on those nights is not optional.

> **Citation:** Hernández Martínez G, Rodriguez M-L, Vaquero M-C, et al. High-Flow Oxygen with Capping or Suctioning for Tracheostomy Decannulation. *N Engl J Med*. 2020;383(11):1009-1017. PMID: 32905673

> **Citation:** Ceriana P, Carlucci A, Navalesi P, et al. Weaning from tracheotomy in long-term mechanically ventilated patients: feasibility of a decisional flowchart and clinical outcome. *Intensive Care Med*. 2003;29(5):845-848. PMID: 12634987

> **Citation:** Singh RK, Saran S, Baronia AK. The practice of tracheostomy decannulation — a systematic review. *J Intensive Care*. 2017;5:38. PMID: 28649385

## 7. SWALLOWING AND AIRWAY PROTECTION

In neurological patients, **dysphagia and impaired airway protection are the main reasons decannulation is delayed** (Warnecke 2013). GBS/BBE overlap adds a specific problem: persistent laryngeal *sensory* deficit (cranial nerves IX/X) can cause silent aspiration even when motor swallowing looks adequate (Weiss 2025).

### 7.1 SESETD — the endoscopic protocol built for this decision

Warnecke's Standardized Endoscopic Swallowing Evaluation for Tracheostomy Decannulation (SESETD) evaluates three items during FEES: **secretion management, spontaneous swallowing frequency, and laryngeal sensitivity**. In the original series (n=100, neurological ICU), the endoscopic protocol cleared 54 patients for decannulation where clinical swallowing examination would have cleared only 29; one patient (1.9%) needed recannulation. A 2021 prospective evaluation and a 2020 reliability study confirmed the protocol. It does not require the patient to swallow food and is performed with the cuff deflated. *[Level 2b — prospective diagnostic cohort]*

> **Citation:** Warnecke T, Suntrup S, Teismann IK, et al. Standardized endoscopic swallowing evaluation for tracheostomy decannulation in critically ill neurologic patients. *Crit Care Med*. 2013;41(7):1728-1732. PMID: 23774336

> **Citation:** Schröder JB, et al. Standardized Endoscopic Swallowing Evaluation for Tracheostomy Decannulation in Critically Ill Neurologic Patients — a prospective evaluation. *Neurol Res Pract*. 2021;3:26. PMID: 33966636

### 7.2 Oral intake on a modified diet

A patient eating 4 texture-modified (purée/timbal) meals a day and drinking has demonstrated functional swallowing under speech-therapy supervision — a stronger real-world signal than most bedside tests. It does not exclude silent aspiration of thin liquids, which is the specific question FEES answers. Texture modification is expected to be stepped down over months as bulbar recovery continues. *[Level 5]*

## 8. DECANNULATION: CRITERIA, PROCEDURE, AFTERCARE

### 8.1 Consolidated criteria

No universally accepted standard exists (Singh 2017); the majority of published protocols are low-quality. The best-validated elements are Ceriana's flowchart (80% of spontaneously breathing long-term ventilated patients decannulated without major complications), objective cough (PCF) and objective swallowing (FEES/SESETD). The 2025 framework from the Münster/Osnabrück group (Dziewas 2025) integrates these into the **A2BC criteria** — Airway protection, Airway patency, Bronchial secretion management, Cough function — assessed with FEES, endoscopic airway inspection, cough-strength measurement and intrathoracic pressure. *[Level 2b–4]*

| Domain | Criterion | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Ventilation | ≥24 h unassisted breathing incl. one night, normocapnic, no O₂ requirement | Some centres accept nocturnal NIV by mask *after* decannulation (Cheng: 14%) |
| Upper airway | Cap (or valve) tolerated ≥24–48 h with cuffless/downsized tube; or endoscopy showing patent airway | Failure → look for stenosis/granulation before blaming weakness |
| Cough | Unassisted PCF >160 L/min, **or** MI-E-assisted flow adequate with MI-E available post-decannulation | Bach paradigm |
| Secretions | Suction via tube ≤2×/24 h; patient clears own secretions orally | NEJM 2020 threshold |
| Swallowing | FEES/SESETD passed; no gross aspiration on oral intake | Warnecke 2013 |
| Consciousness / cooperation | Alert, follows commands, can signal distress | Met in a communicating patient |
| Stability | No active infection, hemodynamically stable, no planned procedure requiring airway within days | |
| Logistics | Trained staff, emergency re-cannulation kit, oximetry for first 48 h, plan for MI-E by mask | |

> **Citation:** Dziewas R, Warnecke T, Labeit B, et al. Decannulation ahead: a comprehensive diagnostic and therapeutic framework for tracheotomized neurological patients. *Neurol Res Pract*. 2025;7(1):18. PMID: 40091074

### 8.2 Is bronchoscopy needed before decannulation after 6 months?

No guideline mandates routine endoscopy before decannulation. It is indicated when: capping fails despite adequate cough and VC; stridor or voice change with valve; suspicion of granulation at the stoma or cuff site; or after very prolonged cannulation, where the *pre-test probability* of tracheal pathology is higher. Tracheal stenosis after tracheostomy is uncommon (a few percent symptomatic) but disproportionately found after long-term cannulation. A pragmatic approach after ≥6 months: flexible laryngo-tracheoscopy through the stoma at the time of tube change, which adds minutes and can be combined with FEES. *[Level 5]*

### 8.3 Procedure and first 48 hours

- Decannulate in the morning, on a weekday, with ENT/anaesthesia reachable.
- Occlusive dressing over the stoma; instruct the patient to press over it when coughing or speaking. Most stomas close within 5–14 days; persistent fistula beyond ~4 weeks may need surgical closure.
- Continuous oximetry for 24–48 h; first two nights with particular attention (OSA — Section 9).
- MI-E by mask on the established schedule; expect a temporary increase in oral secretions.
- Voice: expect hoarseness or breathy voice for days to weeks; persistent dysphonia → laryngoscopy.
- Re-cannulation kit (tube one size smaller + dilator) at bedside for 72 h. Reported recannulation rates in protocolised series are 2–5%.

## 9. OBSTRUCTIVE SLEEP APNEA — A HIDDEN VARIABLE BEHIND THE TUBE

Tracheostomy is a **definitive treatment for OSA**: it bypasses the collapsible pharynx entirely. A meta-analysis of tracheostomy for adult OSA (Camacho 2014, 18 studies) found it significantly reduces apnea index, oxygen desaturation index, sleepiness and mortality. *[Level 2a]* The corollary for a GBS patient with pre-existing OSA is:

1. **For as long as the tube is open (HME, uncapped), OSA is absent.** Overnight oximetry via an open tube says nothing about pharyngeal collapsibility.
2. **Nocturnal capping is the first night OSA can reappear.** Desaturations on capped nights may be OSA, not hypoventilation — and the two need different treatment (CPAP vs bilevel/NIV). Continuous overnight oximetry on capped nights, ideally with PtcCO₂, separates them: OSA gives cyclical brief desaturations with normal CO₂; hypoventilation gives sustained desaturation with rising CO₂.
3. **After decannulation, the pre-morbid OSA is back at its pre-morbid severity** — possibly worse if body weight, muscle tone of the upper airway (bulbar recovery still incomplete) or supine positioning have changed. Mild positional OSA managed with positional therapy (pillow) or a mandibular device before illness may need reassessment once the tube is out, because pharyngeal dilator muscles (genioglossus, cranial nerve XII) may still be recovering.
4. **Do not misattribute.** Post-decannulation nocturnal desaturation in a patient with known OSA should prompt a sleep study (polygraphy), not an automatic return to NIV or a conclusion that respiratory muscle recovery has stalled.

> **Citation:** Camacho M, Certal V, Brietzke SE, et al. Tracheostomy as treatment for adult obstructive sleep apnea: a systematic review and meta-analysis. *Laryngoscope*. 2014;124(3):803-811. PMID: 24549987

## 10. AFTER DECANNULATION: FOLLOW-UP AND LONG-TERM RESPIRATORY OUTCOME

### 10.1 Structured follow-up

| When | What | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 24–48 h | Continuous oximetry, stoma check, voice, swallow observation | Early failure detection |
| 1–2 weeks | VC sit/supine, PCF (unassisted + MI-E), stoma closure | Baseline for trend |
| 4–6 weeks | Same + overnight oximetry (± polygraphy if OSA history); consider MI-E frequency reduction if PCF >270 | Decide on MI-E, positional therapy/CPAP |
| 3 and 6 months | Full PFT (spirometry, MIP/MEP or SNIP, PCF); overnight study if symptoms | CHEST: PFT every 6 months while at risk |
| Any chest infection | MI-E frequency up; low threshold for early antibiotics (IgA deficiency — see 02-IgA-deficiency) | PCF 160–270 group fails during infection |

### 10.2 What the GBS literature says about respiratory recovery after prolonged ventilation

- Cheng 2021 (n=29, all prolonged ventilation): 76% weaned and decannulated; of those, 14% needed long-term nocturnal NIV; 45% achieved short-distance assisted ambulation at 12–36 months; mortality 17% at 36 months (concentrated in patients with complications during the acute admission). *[Level 4]*
- Thille 2025 (n=513 ventilated GBS): 71% required MV >14 days, 57% tracheostomy; reintubation after extubation was more frequent in GBS than in myasthenia (see ICU doc §7). *[Level 2 — multicentre cohort]*
- Ventilated GBS patients have more residual weakness at 6 months than non-ventilated, but the proportion reaching complete recovery or minor limitation continues to rise between 6 and 12 months (86% → 92% in one cohort; see 06-monitoring-prognosis). *[Level 2b]*
- Specific serial-VC recovery curves after prolonged ventilation in GBS are not published; the general critical-illness literature shows respiratory muscle strength (MIP) continuing to improve for at least 6–12 months after ICU discharge. *[Level 3]*

**Clinical translation:** a VC of ~45–50% predicted at six months, with a normal postural drop, in a patient who was 100% ventilator-dependent at week 4 is compatible with a trajectory toward substantial further recovery. The 6-month value is a waypoint, not a plateau.

## 11. DECISION ALGORITHM

```
PATIENT: unassisted breathing by day, speaking valve all day, tube downsized,
         nocturnal support being withdrawn, oral intake on modified diet

STEP 1 — CONFIRM NOCTURNAL AUTONOMY
  Overnight SpO₂ (open tube) ≥3 nights: no SpO₂ <88% ≥5 min?        ── NO → keep nocturnal support, re-test 1–2 wk
  Morning gas BEFORE any ventilator use ×3: pCO₂ <45 mmHg / 6.0 kPa?  ── NO → keep, look for cause
  Overnight PtcCO₂ ×1–2 nights: no hypoventilation definition met?     ── NO → keep, repeat 2 wk
  VC sitting >20 mL/kg AND supine fall <25%?                          ── NO → keep, weekly VC
  → ALL YES: stop residual morning ventilator session

STEP 2 — AIRWAY CLEARANCE
  Unassisted PCF (capped tube, mask) >160 L/min?
     YES and >270 → MI-E on demand only
     YES but 160–270 → MI-E available, daily during infection
     NO → MI-E scheduled; measure MI-E-assisted flow (>190 L/min?) and
          confirm mask delivery works → decannulation still possible (Bach)

STEP 3 — UPPER AIRWAY
  Cap tolerated 24 h incl. one night on cuffless/6 mm tube, with continuous
  oximetry (OSA history → expect and characterise obstructive events)?
     NO, with adequate VC/PCF → laryngo-tracheoscopy (granulation/stenosis)
     NO, obstructive pattern on oximetry → sleep medicine: CPAP plan, then re-cap
     YES → proceed

STEP 4 — SWALLOWING
  FEES/SESETD: secretion management, spontaneous swallow, laryngeal sensation passed?
     NO → SLT programme, repeat 2–4 wk; decannulation can wait or proceed with NPO/thickened per SLT
     YES → proceed

STEP 5 — DECANNULATE
  Suction via tube ≤2×/24 h, no infection, stable, weekday morning, kit at bedside,
  oximetry 48 h, MI-E by mask trained, stoma occlusion taught
  → first 2 nights: continuous oximetry; obstructive pattern → polygraphy → CPAP/positional
  → PFT at 2 wk, 6 wk, 3 mo, 6 mo
```

## 12. WORKED EXAMPLE — APPLYING THE THRESHOLDS TO THE INDEX CASE

Index case: female, 53 y, 153 cm, ~54 kg, GBS–MFS–BBE overlap with total paralysis and ventilator dependence from day 1; tracheostomy day 5; neurology ward from ~day 140; nocturnal-only ventilation ~6 weeks; last 3–4 nights HME on open tube with a single ~1 h ventilator session each morning; speaking valve all day; tube downsized 7 → 6 mm; oral intake 4 texture-modified meals/day plus fluids; MI-E 2–4×/day; SpO₂ 96–99% around the clock; reports paresthesias in hands/arms and feet/legs (temporal relation to ventilator session not yet established); known mild OSA (positional pillow, mandibular device not tolerated, no CPAP).

| Parameter | Measured (day ~185) | Threshold | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| FVC sitting | **1.31 L** | >20 mL/kg → at 54 kg = 1.08 L; at 60 kg = 1.20 L | ✅ ~24 mL/kg |
| FVC % predicted | 1.31 / ~2.5–2.8 L (ECSC/GLI, F, 53 y, 153 cm) | — | ~47–52% predicted; trend more informative than absolute |
| FVC supine | **1.21 L** | Fall <25% (here **7.6%**) | ✅ no diaphragm-weakness signature |
| Morning gas (pre-ventilator) | Reported normal | pCO₂ <45 mmHg | ✅ (values not seen) |
| Gas during/after ventilator session | **Not done** | pCO₂ not below normal | ❓ patient reports paresthesias in hands/feet — timing vs session to be clarified (§4.3a) |
| SpO₂ overnight | 96–99% (spot/continuous?) | No <88% ≥5 min | ✅ if continuous; confirm recording |
| Overnight PtcCO₂ | **Not done** | No definition met | ❓ the single most useful missing test |
| Unassisted PCF | **Not measured** | >160 L/min | ❓ MI-E 2–4×/day implies <270, possibly <160 |
| MI-E-assisted flow | Not measured | >190 L/min | ❓ |
| MIP / MEP | Not measured | MIP < −50 cmH₂O | ❓ |
| Speaking valve tolerance | All day | Upper-airway patency for expiration | ✅ |
| Capping trial | Not started | 24–48 h incl. night | ⬜ next step |
| Tube | 6 mm (cuffed/cuffless?) | Cuffless preferred for capping | ✅/❓ |
| Swallowing | 4 modified meals + fluids | FEES/SESETD | ✅ functional; FEES status unknown |
| Suction frequency | Not reported | ≤2×/24 h via tube | ❓ |
| OSA | Mild, positional, untreated | Characterise on first capped nights | ⚠️ plan required |

**Reading:** the ventilatory criteria for decannulation are met or nearly met and the diaphragm signature is reassuring. The three open questions are all measurable on the ward within a week: **overnight PtcCO₂, peak cough flow (unassisted and MI-E-assisted, via mask with tube capped), and FEES/SESETD**. With those answered, the residual morning ventilator session has a defined exit criterion and a nocturnal capping trial can be scheduled, with oximetry on capped nights interpreted in the light of the OSA history.
