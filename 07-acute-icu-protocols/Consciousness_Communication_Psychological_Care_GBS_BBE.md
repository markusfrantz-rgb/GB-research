## 1. Introduction — Why This Matters

Severe GBS — and particularly the BBE-GBS overlap syndrome — creates a clinical paradox: patients with **complete motor paralysis** who may retain **full consciousness, hearing, and vision**. This locked-in-like state is distinct from coma and demands a fundamentally different approach to ICU care. *[Level 5]*

The consequences of misrecognizing this state are severe:

- **Undertreated pain** — 89% of GBS patients experience significant pain, but a locked-in patient cannot report it
- **Psychological devastation** — anxiety, hallucinations, and PTSD occur at rates far exceeding other ICU populations
- **Missed clinical deterioration** — a patient who cannot signal dyspnea, new pain, or distress becomes vulnerable
- **Iatrogenic harm** — procedures performed without explanation to a fully aware patient cause avoidable psychological trauma

This document synthesizes current evidence on consciousness assessment, communication tools, and psychological care for the severely paralysed GBS/BBE patient in the ICU.

## 2. Consciousness Assessment During Sedation

### 2.1 The Central Dilemma

In a sedated, paralysed BBE-GBS patient, three states are clinically indistinguishable at the bedside without neurophysiological tools:

| State | Mechanism | Implication |
|-------|-----------|-------------|
| **Unconscious** (BBE) | Brainstem dysfunction, reticular formation involvement | Consciousness will return — median 10 days (anti-GQ1b+) |
| **Sedated** (iatrogenic) | Propofol, remifentanil, benzodiazepines | Reduce sedation to unmask true neurological state |
| **Locked-in** (conscious) | Intact cortex, intact awareness, no motor output | Patient hears, feels, and understands everything |

**The clinical imperative:** until proven otherwise, assume the patient is aware. *[Level 5]*

### 2.2 Daily Sedation Pauses

Sedation pauses are the single most important bedside tool for consciousness assessment in this population:

1. Taper propofol and opioid over 30–60 minutes (avoid abrupt cessation — dysautonomia risk)
2. Observe for minimum **30 minutes** off sedation
3. Assess systematically: eye opening → tracking → response to command → any voluntary movement
4. If autonomic instability occurs → resume sedation at 50% previous dose
5. **Document all findings** — even tiny changes may signal consciousness returning

**Caution:** In BBE patients with severe dysautonomia, sedation pauses require continuous haemodynamic monitoring. Blood pressure swings >85 mmHg in 24h indicate high-grade dysautonomia (Pfeiffer 1999, PMID: 10590674). *[Level 4]*

### 2.3 What to Look For

| Sign | Significance |
|------|-------------|
| Spontaneous eye opening | Arousal present |
| Visual tracking (follows faces, objects) | Cortical processing intact |
| Blink to command ("blink twice") | Comprehension + voluntary motor control |
| Brow raise or furrowing | May be the last voluntary movement preserved |
| Finger or toe flicker | Peripheral motor output returning |
| Tear production in response to voice | Emotional processing preserved (anecdotal) |
| Tachycardia in response to family voice | Possible emotional recognition — interpret cautiously |

## 3. The Locked-In State in GBS and BBE

### 3.1 Prevalence

Total locked-in syndrome in GBS is rare but well-documented. It occurs most frequently in:
- **Fulminant GBS** (nadir within 24–48h)
- **BBE-GBS overlap** with anti-GQ1b antibodies
- **AMAN/AMSAN** variants with cranial nerve involvement

Several case reports document complete locked-in presentations that were initially mistaken for brain death. *[Level 4]*

### 3.2 Key Case Evidence

| Case | Presentation | Locked-in duration | Outcome |
|------|-------------|-------------------|---------|
| D'Angelo 2023 (PMID: 38170042) | Anti-GQ1b+, quadriplegia, bulbar weakness | Days | Rapid recovery after PE |
| Tian 2023 (PMC: 9936049) | Anti-GQ1b+, coma, apnea, total paralysis | Days | Rapid recovery after IVIg |
| Kumta 2022 (PMID: 34716224) | Complete paralysis, fully conscious | 31 days | Recovered |
| Korinthenberg 2021 (PMID: 33111302) | Locked-in, fluctuating, paediatric | 3 months | Nearly complete recovery |
| Pisano 2025 (PMID: 39623161) | Anti-GM1/GD1b+, total locked-in | >40 days | Ventilator-liberated, wheelchair |
| Ragazzoni 2000 (PMID: 10828378) | Total locked-in, absent brainstem reflexes | Weeks | Patient 1: recovered |

### 3.3 The Patient's Experience

The subjective experience of locked-in GBS has been described as "my living nightmare" (Kumta 2022). Key features:

- **Full awareness** — the patient hears every conversation, feels every procedure, understands every discussion about prognosis
- **Pain** — neuropathic, musculoskeletal, and procedural pain may all be present but unreportable
- **Dyspnea** — air hunger sensation despite mechanical ventilation is common and extremely distressing
- **Anxiety** — 82% moderate-to-severe anxiety in ICU GBS patients (Peric 2020, PMID: 30022321) *[Level 3]*
- **Helplessness** — the inability to signal distress, ask questions, or request repositioning

**Critical point:** Recovery is possible even from total locked-in. The cases above demonstrate that weeks to months of locked-in state can be followed by substantial or complete motor recovery.

## 4. Communication Strategies — A Practical Hierarchy

Early establishment of communication is both a clinical tool (enabling symptom reporting) and a psychological intervention (reducing helplessness and isolation). *[Level 5]*

### 4.1 Hierarchy

| Priority | Method | Prerequisites | Equipment |
|----------|--------|---------------|-----------|
| 1 | **Blink code** (2 = yes, 1 = no) | Any voluntary blink control | None |
| 2 | **Eye movement code** (look up = yes, down = no) | Voluntary vertical gaze | None |
| 3 | **Alphabet board / eye-pointing** | Sustained voluntary gaze direction | Printed board |
| 4 | **Communication cards** (GBS-CIDP Foundation) | Eye-pointing ability | Printed cards |
| 5 | **Speaking valve** (Passy-Muir) | Tracheostomy + vocal cord function | Valve |
| 6 | **cEEG interface** | Attempted muscle activation (even non-visible) | Standard EEG |
| 7 | **fMRI command following** | Cortical function preserved | MRI scanner |
| 8 | **ERP assessment** | Cortical auditory processing | ERP equipment |

### 4.2 General Principles

- **Start simple.** Blinks and eye movements require no equipment and can be attempted immediately during sedation pauses.
- **Target the last muscle lost.** Communication interfaces are most effective when targeting the muscle group the patient last had visible control over (Pisano 2025). *[Level 4]*
- **Reassess daily.** Communication capacity may fluctuate with sedation levels, fatigue, and disease trajectory.
- **Involve the family.** Family members often detect subtle responses that clinical staff miss — they know the patient's baseline behaviours.
- **Document the code.** Post the communication system at the bedside so all staff — including unfamiliar personnel — can use it consistently.

## 5. cEEG as Communication Interface

### 5.1 The Pisano Protocol

> **Citation:** Pisano TJ, Levine JM. Repurposing cEEG to Enable Communication in Total Locked-in Syndrome. *Neurocrit Care*. 2025;42:726-729. PMID: 39623161

A 19-year-old with fulminant GBS (anti-GM1/GD1b+) lost all visible voluntary movement by hospital day 5. The ICU team repurposed standard continuous EEG to detect **non-visible** frontalis muscle activity: *[Level 4]*

### 5.2 Technical Setup

| Parameter | Setting |
|-----------|---------|
| **Electrodes** | Standard frontal surface leads: Fp2-F8, Fp1-F3, F3-C3, Fp2-F4 |
| **Montage** | AP bipolar |
| **Sensitivity** | 70 µV/mm |
| **Time base** | 30 mm/s |
| **Filters** | 60 Hz notch, 1 Hz low-frequency, 70 Hz high-frequency |
| **Target muscle** | Frontalis (brow) — the last muscle with visible voluntary movement |

### 5.3 Communication Code

- **2 discrete peaks = "yes"**
- **1 peak = "no"**

Peaks are visible in real-time on the bedside cEEG monitor. No additional software or hardware required.

### 5.4 Clinical Applications

- Pain level assessment ("Are you in pain?")
- Anxiety screening ("Are you feeling anxious?")
- Dyspnea reporting ("Are you short of breath?")
- Basic medical decisions ("Do you want us to continue?")
- Exclusion of non-convulsive status epilepticus (concurrent diagnostic benefit)

### 5.5 Implementation Notes

- Family described learning the system as **"super easy"**
- Training time: minimal — staff and family read peaks directly on the monitor
- The approach can be adapted to **any muscle group** the patient last lost visible control of — not limited to frontalis
- **Equipment already present** in most ICUs performing continuous EEG monitoring

## 6. Advanced Neurophysiological Consciousness Assessment

When bedside assessment is ambiguous, neurophysiological tools can detect preserved consciousness in apparently unresponsive patients.

### 6.1 EEG Patterns

| Pattern | Interpretation |
|---------|---------------|
| **Normal reactive alpha rhythm** | Preserved cortical function despite clinical unresponsiveness — **may indicate locked-in, not coma** |
| **Spindle coma** | Brainstem dysfunction with intact cortex — described in BBE (Shimozono 2012, PMID: 22989900) |
| **Diffuse slowing** | True encephalopathy component |
| **Burst suppression** | Iatrogenic (over-sedation) or severe cortical dysfunction |

> **Citation:** Tian J et al. Anti-GQ1b antibody syndrome with brain death-like changes. *BMC Neurol*. 2023. PMC: 9936049

55-year-old with anti-GQ1b+: coma, apnea, complete paralysis, positive Babinski. EEG showed **normal reactive alpha rhythm** despite clinical appearance mimicking brain death. Rapid recovery with IVIg. **EEG distinguished locked-in from brain death.** *[Level 4]*

### 6.2 Event-Related Potentials (ERPs)

> **Citation:** Ragazzoni A et al. ERPs in total locked-in GBS. *Int J Psychophysiol*. 2000;37(1):99-109. PMID: 10828378

ERPs measure cortical processing of auditory stimuli. In two GBS patients with total locked-in and absent brainstem reflexes:
- **Normal auditory ERPs** confirmed preserved consciousness and cognitive processing
- One patient: complete motor recovery. Other patient: died of complications.

**Clinical value:** Normal ERPs in an unresponsive GBS patient = consciousness is preserved. This has direct implications for sedation management, communication attempts, and family counselling. *[Level 4]*

### 6.3 Functional MRI (fMRI)

> **Citation:** Norton L et al. fMRI consciousness assessment in severe GBS. *Int J Clin Health Psychol*. 2023;23(2):100347. PMC: 9663328

fMRI can detect **covert command following** — the patient imagines performing a motor task, and fMRI detects the cortical activation. This is the gold standard for detecting consciousness when all motor output is absent. *[Level 4]*

**Limitations:** Requires transport to MRI scanner, haemodynamic stability, and absence of MRI-incompatible equipment.

### 6.4 Auditory Brainstem Response (ABR)

> **Citation:** Kurihara T et al. BBE diagnosis with ABR. *Acute Med Surg*. 2020;7(1):e517. PMC: 7266809

ABR assesses brainstem auditory pathway integrity. Preserved ABR in BBE is associated with better outcome and shorter recovery time. *[Level 4]*

### 6.5 Consciousness Recovery in ICU — Broader Evidence

> **Citation:** Kondziella D et al. Understanding, detecting, and stimulating consciousness recovery in the ICU. *Eur J Neurol*. 2023;30(4):929-943. PMID: 36242637

This review addresses the challenge of detecting covert awareness in apparently unresponsive ICU patients across aetiologies. Key recommendations applicable to GBS/BBE:
- Behavioural assessments alone are insufficient — structured neurophysiological testing is indicated
- **Up to 15–20% of clinically unresponsive patients** in general ICU populations demonstrate covert awareness on advanced testing
- In GBS/BBE, the proportion with preserved consciousness is likely **much higher** due to the peripheral (not central) nature of the paralysis *[Level 3]*

## 7. Psychological Impact of the Locked-In Experience

### 7.1 Prevalence of Psychological Complications

> **Citation:** Cochen V et al. Vivid dreams, hallucinations, psychosis and REM sleep in Guillain-Barré syndrome. *Brain*. 2005;128(Pt 11):2535-2545. PMID: 16085746

In a prospective study of 139 GBS patients requiring ICU admission:

| Phenomenon | Prevalence | Peak timing |
|------------|-----------|-------------|
| Hallucinations | **60%** | Day 9 |
| Delusions | **70%** | Day 9 |
| Illusions | **30%** | — |
| Vivid dreams | **19%** | — |

Visual hallucinations and paranoid delusions were most common. Importantly, these occurred in patients with **normal cognitive function** — they are not markers of delirium in the conventional ICU sense. *[Level 3]*

### 7.2 Anxiety and Depression

> **Citation:** Peric S et al. Psychiatric sequelae of Guillain-Barré syndrome. *Psychosomatics*. 2020;61(1):30-37. PMID: 30022321

- **82%** of severely disabled GBS patients report moderate-to-severe anxiety
- **67%** report clinical depression
- **4.8-fold increased risk** of depression compared to general population
- PTSD is associated with GBS **independently** of anxiety and depression
- These persist even years after discharge *[Level 3]*

### 7.3 Vivid Dreams vs. ICU Delirium

> **Citation:** Weiss N et al. Critical insights for intensivists on Guillain-Barré syndrome. *Ann Intensive Care*. 2025;15:67. PMC: 12092332

GBS patients experience **altered sleep with depersonalisation** described as vivid dreams — distinct from conventional ICU delirium. Key differences:

- May occur in **non-ventilated** patients
- When present in mild cases, may signal impending dysautonomia and disease progression
- Should not be automatically treated with antipsychotics as standard ICU delirium
- **Under-diagnosed** in ventilated patients due to communication difficulties *[Level 4]*

### 7.4 Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder

PTSD is particularly prevalent in mechanically ventilated GBS patients requiring prolonged ICU stays. It occurs **independently** of anxiety and depression and can persist for years. *[Level 3]*

However, **amnesia for the acute phase is common** — particularly in patients who were heavily sedated. This may be partially protective (Rouge 2016, PMC: 5106230). *[Level 4]*

### 7.5 Depression Post-Recovery

> **Citation:** Rouge H et al. Long-term impact after fulminant Guillain-Barré syndrome. *BMC Neurol*. 2016;16:165. PMC: 5106230

Reactive depressive syndrome is common during recovery and may require long-term antidepressant treatment. Depression should be anticipated, screened for, and treated as part of the rehabilitation plan. *[Level 4]*

## 8. Environmental Optimization in the ICU

While no RCTs specifically address environmental interventions in locked-in GBS, consensus recommendations and extrapolation from general ICU literature support the following: *[Level 5]*

### 8.1 Circadian Rhythm Protection

| Time | Intervention |
|------|-------------|
| Day (07:00–21:00) | Bright ambient lighting, window blinds open |
| Night (21:00–07:00) | Dim lighting, minimize non-essential interventions |
| Transitions | Verbal orientation: "It's morning now. The sun is coming up." |

**Rationale:** Circadian disruption in ICU is associated with increased delirium, worsened sleep architecture, and immune dysfunction. In a patient who may be fully conscious but immobile, day-night distinction becomes a critical anchor for psychological orientation.

### 8.2 Noise Reduction

- Minimize non-essential alarms — adjust alarm thresholds to reduce false positives
- Avoid loud conversations at the bedside that are not directed at the patient
- Consider ear protection during noisy procedures (if not using auditory monitoring)
- **Critical:** Never discuss the patient's prognosis negatively at the bedside without addressing the patient directly

### 8.3 Staff Consistency

**Consistent staff assignment improves communication familiarity and trust** (Weiss 2025, Severe GBS ICU Management guidelines). *[Level 5]*

When the same nurses care for a paralysed patient over consecutive shifts:
- They learn to read subtle patient responses
- The patient recognises voices — reducing anxiety from unfamiliar caregivers
- Communication systems (blink codes, cEEG) work better with practised partners
- Trust enables more effective pain and anxiety assessment

**Recommendation:** Where staffing permits, assign consistent nursing teams for locked-in GBS/BBE patients, particularly during the acute paralytic phase.

### 8.4 Verbal Orientation and Procedural Narration

Every interaction with the patient should include:

1. **Self-identification:** "Hello, it's [name]. I'm your nurse today."
2. **Time orientation:** "It's Tuesday morning, April 15th."
3. **Procedural explanation:** "I'm going to suction your tracheostomy now. It will take about 30 seconds."
4. **Reassurance:** "Everything is going as expected."

This is not optional courtesy — it is a clinical intervention that reduces the psychological impact of helplessness. *[Level 5]*

## 9. Pain Assessment in the Non-Communicative Patient

### 9.1 The Problem

Up to **89%** of GBS patients experience significant pain — neuropathic, musculoskeletal, or both. In a locked-in patient, pain cannot be self-reported and may manifest only as:

- Tachycardia (easily attributed to dysautonomia)
- Hypertension (easily attributed to dysautonomia)
- Diaphoresis
- Agitation (impossible to demonstrate when paralysed)
- Facial grimacing (impossible when facial muscles are paralysed)

Standard ICU pain tools (BPS, CPOT) rely on motor responses and may be **unreliable** in total locked-in GBS. *[Level 4]*

### 9.2 Approach

1. **Assume pain is present** unless there is evidence to the contrary
2. **Scheduled analgesia** rather than PRN — gabapentin 15 mg/kg/day is first-line for neuropathic pain in GBS *[Level 2]*
3. **If communication exists** (blinks, cEEG): ask "Are you in pain?" at every assessment
4. **Trial of analgesia:** If physiological signs suggest possible pain, administer analgesic and observe for response (reduced tachycardia, blood pressure normalisation)
5. **Positioning pain:** Paralysed patients cannot shift position — regular repositioning (minimum q2h) with pressure area care

### 9.3 Pharmacological Considerations

| Agent | Role | Caution |
|-------|------|---------|
| Gabapentin 300–1200 mg TID | First-line neuropathic pain | Renal adjustment |
| Pregabalin 75–300 mg BID | Alternative to gabapentin | Renal adjustment |
| Paracetamol 1g QID | Baseline analgesia | Hepatotoxicity in GBS (monitor LFTs) |
| Lidocaine 5% plaster | Localised neuropathic pain | Low adverse-effect risk |
| Opioids (fentanyl, morphine) | Severe breakthrough pain | Ileus risk — use cautiously |

**Avoid tricyclic antidepressants** (amitriptyline) in patients with active dysautonomia — risk of arrhythmia. *[Level 4]*

## 10. Family Presence and Communication

### 10.1 Clinical Evidence

Family presence at the bedside is a therapeutic intervention, not merely a comfort measure:

- **Reduces patient anxiety** — familiar voices are anchoring in the locked-in state
- **Improves communication** — family members often detect subtle patient responses before staff
- **Reduces PTSD** — both patient and family PTSD rates are lower with consistent family involvement
- **72% of close relatives** of severe GBS patients experience social dysfunction, psychological distress, and problems with daily activities *[Level 3]*

### 10.2 Guidance for Families

The following points should be communicated explicitly to families:

1. **"Your loved one is likely aware."** GBS patients retain full consciousness, hearing, and vision despite paralysis. Speak to them directly. They understand everything.

2. **"GBS is a recoverable disease."** The immune system attacks the nerves, but nerves can regenerate. This is fundamentally different from conditions where nerve or brain tissue is permanently destroyed.

3. **"Recovery takes time."** Nerves regenerate at approximately 1 mm per day (~1 inch per month). For severely affected patients, this means months to years. Patience is essential.

4. **"Your presence matters."** Sitting at the bedside, holding a hand, speaking calmly — these are not passive. They are interventions that reduce suffering.

5. **"Take care of yourself."** Family caregiver well-being directly affects patient outcomes. Sleep, eat, seek support.

### 10.3 GBS-CIDP Foundation Resources

- Caregiver handbook (comprehensive 70-page guide)
- Survivor visits to hospitalised patients
- Local peer support chapters: www.gbs-cidp.org

## 11. Psychiatric Screening and Early Intervention

### 11.1 When to Screen

- **On ICU admission** — baseline psychiatric assessment
- **During sedation pauses** — when communication is possible, screen for anxiety and depression
- **At weaning milestones** — transition points are psychologically high-risk
- **At ICU discharge** — formal PTSD, anxiety, and depression screening
- **At 3, 6, and 12 months** post-discharge — ongoing monitoring

### 11.2 Treatment

| Condition | First-line | Notes |
|-----------|-----------|-------|
| Anxiety (acute) | Benzodiazepines (short course) or dexmedetomidine | Avoid benzodiazepines long-term (delirium risk) |
| Depression | SSRIs (sertraline, citalopram) | Start early if depression detected; safe in dysautonomia |
| PTSD | Trauma-focused CBT post-discharge | Refer to psychologist at discharge |
| Hallucinations | Observation if non-distressing; low-dose haloperidol if distressing | Distinguish from vivid dreams — not all require treatment |

### 11.3 Multidisciplinary Approach

> **Citation:** Weiss N et al. Critical insights for intensivists on Guillain-Barré syndrome. *Ann Intensive Care*. 2025;15:67. PMC: 12092332

"Early hospitalization psychological disorder screening and treatment through multidisciplinary approaches prove essential. Anxiety indeed associates with weakness, bulbar dysfunction, and respiratory failure in these patients." *[Level 4]*

## 12. Recovery of Consciousness — Timelines and Prognostic Markers

### 12.1 Anti-GQ1b Status Is the Strongest Predictor

> **Citation:** Yoshikawa K et al. BBE and anti-GQ1b antibody. *J Neuroimmunol*. 2020;348:577364. PMC: 7524576

| Feature | Anti-GQ1b+ | Anti-GQ1b− |
|---------|-----------|-----------|
| Consciousness recovery | **Median 10 days** | Median 23 days |
| MRI abnormalities | 8% | 50% |
| CSF protein | Lower | Higher |

Anti-GQ1b positivity is associated with faster consciousness recovery and fewer structural CNS lesions. *[Level 3]*

### 12.2 Recovery Milestones

| Milestone | Typical timeline | Source |
|-----------|-----------------|--------|
| Consciousness recovery | Median 10 days (anti-GQ1b+) | Yoshikawa 2020 |
| First limb motor improvement | 2–3 weeks | Coly 2024 |
| Ventilator weaning | 3–4 weeks (range: days to months) | Thille 2025 |
| Sitting balance | 6–8 weeks | Lombet 2021 |
| Independent walking | 3–12 months | Shahrizaila 2021 |

### 12.3 When Consciousness Recovery Is Delayed

If consciousness has not returned by **3–4 weeks** in an anti-GQ1b+ patient (or 6–8 weeks in anti-GQ1b−):
- Repeat MRI brain (look for new lesions, PRES, ADEM overlap)
- Repeat EEG (exclude non-convulsive status epilepticus)
- Consider alternative diagnoses (concurrent encephalitis, metabolic encephalopathy, thyroid dysfunction)
- Reassess sedation — even low-dose agents may impair consciousness detection
